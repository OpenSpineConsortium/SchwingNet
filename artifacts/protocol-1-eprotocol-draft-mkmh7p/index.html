      Summary & Purpose

      1. Summary, in lay language

      Surgeons and radiologists locate a vertebra by counting from a landmark. About one person in six has a spine with a different count, an extra or missing rib, or a vertebra fused to the pelvis, and counting goes wrong; operating on the wrong level is the result the field worries about most, and the thoracic spine and the lumbosacral junction are where it happens. Our group has built, from public CT scans, a program that outlines every bone of the spine and pelvis and names each vertebra from its own shape rather than by counting.

      This study uses images and records that already exist at DMC to:

      1. check that program on DMC patients;
      2. build a reference standard for the intraoperative x-ray images surgeons use to confirm the level, by matching each intraoperative image to the same patient's CT with an image-registration algorithm, and use that reference to train and test a program that names the level from the intraoperative image alone;
      3. train and test fracture detection on trauma spine and pelvis imaging;
      4. relate the imaging findings to treatment and outcome recorded in the trauma registry.

      No patient is contacted, treated differently, or has anything returned to the medical record.

      2a. Purpose and hypotheses

      The purpose is to develop and evaluate computer methods that name vertebral levels and detect injury on the spine and pelvis images DMC patients already receive, with an explicit focus on the patients whose anatomy makes counting unreliable.

      • H1A vertebra-naming program trained on public CT identifies each lumbar and lower thoracic vertebra on DMC CT scans in agreement with the radiology report and with a radiologist's read, and does so in patients with transitional anatomy (sixth lumbar vertebra, sacralized L5, lumbarized S1, thirteenth thoracic vertebra, hypoplastic or absent twelfth rib) as well as in patients with usual anatomy.
      • H2Intraoperative fluoroscopic images can be labelled with vertebral levels by registering them to the same patient's pre- or post-operative CT (three-dimensional to two-dimensional registration), and those labels agree with the level recorded in the operative note and with the CT-verified numbering in at least 98% of vertebrae.
      • H3A program trained on those registration-derived labels identifies the vertebral level on an intraoperative fluoroscopic image without a CT, and its per-vertebra accuracy against the registration reference is measurable with a two-sided 95% confidence interval narrower than ±1 percentage point in each pre-specified stratum (usual anatomy, transitional anatomy, instrumented, non-instrumented).
      • H4Fracture detection on trauma spine and pelvis radiographs and CT reaches sensitivity and specificity against the CT-confirmed radiology read that can be compared with the initial clinical read.
      • H5Among trauma patients, imaging findings identified by the programs are associated with the treatment given and the recorded outcome.

      2b. What the investigators hope to learn

      Whether naming a vertebra from its shape survives contact with a real, largely underinsured urban trauma population and its imaging; how often an intraoperative image alone would have led to a wrong level, and in which patients; whether a registration-based reference standard is a practical way to label intraoperative images at scale; and whether fracture detection and level naming change enough management to justify the prospective silent evaluation in Protocol 2.

      Background, Rationale and Analysis — Section A

      a(i). Current knowledge

      Wrong-level spine surgery occurred in 0.12% of 34,544 consecutive cases in a recent single-system series, in 0.42% of thoracic cases, and every event was at L4–5 or L5–S1, with image misinterpretation named in 18.8%. Lumbosacral transitional vertebrae have a pooled prevalence of 15.3% across 83 studies; numerical variants (23 or 25 presacral segments) occur in 7.7% of whole-spine MRIs and cannot be seen on a limited field of view; outpatient level counts were wrong in 54% of patients with transitional anatomy in a surgical series; on radiographs, readers agree on the transitional type only at κ 0.16–0.32.

      The one validated method for labelling intraoperative radiographs, LevelCheck (Johns Hopkins), registers the patient's preoperative CT to the intraoperative image and placed labels correctly on 398 of 398 intraoperative radiographs from 178 patients; it requires a CT and is not publicly available, but the registration components it relies on are (xReg; DiffDRR/DiffPose). No public dataset of intraoperative spine fluoroscopy with vertebra-level labels exists.

      Our group has released a public CT dataset of 802 abdominopelvic scans with every vertebra, rib, sacrum and pelvis labelled, including 18 six-lumbar spines and 33 graded transitional cases (Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.22642578; WSU determination 2026-269, Not Human Participant Research), and a vertebra-naming model trained on it. Insert full citations; see the reference list at the end.

      a(ii). Rationale

      Level naming from local shape has only been tested on public CT and on rendered images; whether it works on real intraoperative fluoroscopy, on instrumented spines, and in patients with transitional anatomy cannot be learned from public data because no such labelled data exist. Registration to the patient's own CT is the one route that produces exact labels for real intraoperative images without a human counting on the image. DMC archives intraoperative fluoroscopy to PACS for at least one neurosurgical service, and most spine-surgery patients have a pre- or post-operative CT, so the pairs exist.

      a(iii). Rationale for the participant population

      DMC's spine and trauma population is under-represented in public datasets and carries a heavy burden of high-energy injury and of prior surgery; it is the population in which the methods have to work. Adults only in this protocol.

      a(iv). Inclusion criteria

      Any patient aged 18 years or older with at least one of the following at a DMC facility between 1 January 2006 and the date of IRB approval: a CT that includes any part of the spine or pelvis; a radiograph of the spine or pelvis; an MRI of the spine; an intraoperative fluoroscopic image series of the spine or pelvis archived in PACS or in the C-arm or navigation archive; or an entry in the DMC trauma registry with a spine or pelvic injury.

      a(v). Exclusion criteria

      Age under 18 at the time of imaging; imaging that contains no spine or pelvis; studies whose images cannot be de-identified (burned-in identifiers that cannot be removed) are excluded from transfer but counted.

      b. Statistical methods

      Agreement of level names with the reference (radiology report and radiologist read for CT; registration-derived labels for intraoperative images; CT-verified count for radiographs) is reported as per-vertebra exact accuracy with Wilson 95% intervals, within-one-level rate, and the full confusion matrix, stratified by anatomy (usual; transitional with an unchanged count; transitional with a changed count), region (lumbosacral, thoracolumbar, thoracic, cervical), and instrumentation. Registration quality is reported as projected-centroid error and as the rate of level labels changed by adjudication. Fracture detection is reported as sensitivity, specificity and AUC against the CT-confirmed read, with the initial clinical read as comparator. Disagreements between the programs and the reference are adjudicated by a radiologist and a surgeon blinded to the program's output. Associations with treatment and outcome (Aim 4) use logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, injury severity and mechanism. Analyses are pre-specified in a statistical analysis plan filed before the first label is produced.

      c. Sample size justification

      To bound a per-vertebra accuracy of 99% with a 95% interval of ±0.5 percentage points requires about 1,500 verified vertebrae in each primary stratum; four primary strata (usual and transitional anatomy, each instrumented and not) give a target of 6,000 verified vertebrae on intraoperative images, i.e. roughly 1,500–2,500 intraoperative images from 800–1,500 patients, since about 12.9% of lumbar surgical patients have transitional anatomy and enrichment for those cases is deliberate. Trauma fracture detection at an expected sensitivity of 90% ± 3 points needs about 400 fractured studies. Because transitional anatomy and archived intraoperative imaging are both minorities, the records request is broad: 40,000 imaging studies from an estimated 25,000 patients — the same number must appear on the WSU form, this document and the DMC application; confirm with a PACS count before submission.

      Procedures — Section B

      a. Interaction, intervention, or data collection from participants: No.

      b. Chronological description

      1. DMC Radiology informatics and the neurosurgical service identify eligible studies by modality, body part and date (PACS query), and the trauma registry office identifies registry entries meeting the criteria.
      2. Within the DMC secure environment (Citrix), the study team pulls the DICOM studies (CT, radiographs, MRI, and intraoperative fluoroscopy as XA, RF or Secondary Capture objects, including C-arm and navigation-system archives where they exist), the radiology reports, the operative notes, and the registry and EMR variables on the data collection sheet.
      3. Medical record number, financial identification number and accession number are used once to link images to reports, notes and registry rows. Each patient receives a study code; each study a study code; the key linking codes to identifiers is kept on DMC Citrix, accessible only to the PI and one designated key holder, and nowhere else.
      4. Images are de-identified inside DMC before transfer: DICOM headers are stripped to the DICOM PS3.15 basic de-identification profile with dates shifted per patient and ages retained in years (capped at 89); pixel data are screened for burned-in text with an optical-character-recognition detector on every frame, flagged frames are masked, and 100% of frames are inspected by a study team member before release; frames whose text cannot be masked without destroying the image are excluded and counted. Reports and notes are de-identified with a validated clinical text de-identification tool followed by manual review of a 10% sample.
      5. De-identified images and text are transferred by encrypted transfer (SFTP over the WSU VPN, or an encrypted drive carried by the PI) to the WSU research cluster under a project directory readable only by the named study staff.
      6. Intraoperative fluoroscopic images are registered to the same patient's pre- or post-operative CT with a three-dimensional to two-dimensional registration algorithm; the CT's vertebra labels are projected into the image to give the reference level of every visible vertebra; registrations that fail their quality gate are flagged for reader labelling. Operative notes supply the surgeon-stated level for comparison.
      7. Programs for level naming on CT, level naming on radiographs and intraoperative images, and fracture detection are trained and tested on the de-identified data, with patient-level splits fixed before training.
      8. A radiologist and a surgeon adjudicate a stratified sample of level labels and every disagreement between program and reference, blinded to the program's output.
      9. Statistical analysis as in Section A(b). Aim 4 links imaging findings to registry treatment and outcome variables through the study codes only.

      c. Who, where, when

      Steps 1–4 by the PI and named DMC study staff inside DMC systems; step 5 by the PI; steps 6–9 by the PI and named WSU study staff on the WSU research cluster, over the three-year study period.

      Notes to the PI

      The design question you asked — CTs and fluoroscopy together?

      Yes, and it is the point of the protocol. Request, per patient: every intraoperative fluoroscopic image (XA, RF and Secondary Capture objects; ask Radiology whether OR C-arm saves and navigation screenshots land in PACS or in a separate archive — at one university centre 90 of 190 consecutive cases had intraoperative images only on film or outside PACS), the pre-operative and any post-operative CT, the radiology reports, the operative note (the surgeon's stated level is the clinical comparator), and whole-spine MRI reports when present, since they carry the numbering convention.

      The registration-labelled pairs are the reference standard; the registration-free model is the index test; the surgeon's intraoperative level call is the clinical baseline. Also request fluoroscopy from patients without a CT: they cannot enter the reference set, but they are the honest test of the registration-free model on cases nobody could register. Ask for post-instrumentation captures explicitly — every fusion case has them — so hardware arrives labelled, and ask for corpectomy cases by CPT code so that stratum is countable.

      Write one number for records on the WSU form, this document and the DMC application.
