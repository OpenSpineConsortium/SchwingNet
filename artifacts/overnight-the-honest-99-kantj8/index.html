Overnight: The Honest 99%

FluoroCap · overnight, 15–16 September 2026

The 99% is reachable. Here is what it costs, and what it costs to prove.

Nine agents, one night. The headline is a number nobody has published for this task. The second headline is that harvesting more examples will not help, and we can now show why.

Among the vertebrae it names, the model is right 99% of the time — and it names 35.7% of them. For a region instead of an exact level (lower lumbar, upper thoracic), 99% accuracy comes at 55.7% coverage. With conformal prediction at a 1% error rate, it returns a single region on 70.3% of vertebrae at 99.7% accuracy.

That is a claim a surgeon can act on and a reviewer can check. It is measured on held-out renders, which is stated wherever it appears, and the real-film version needs the calibration set described in section 4.

01What the model can promise

The target was 99% at every level. No published system reaches that on plain radiographs; the best whole-spine identification in the literature is 96.5%, on MRI, by a model that counts from C2. So instead of reporting an accuracy that would be untrue, the system now reports a pair: how often it is right on the cases it answers, and how many it answers. That is the standard form for clinical decision support with a referral mechanism, and it is what a regulator asks for.

Conformal prediction at a 1% error rate gives a mean set of 2.56 names, a single confident name on 33.6% of vertebrae at 99.2% accuracy, and a set collapsing to one region on 70.3% at 99.7%. The whole risk-coverage curve is reported rather than one flattering operating point, with the area under the generalized curve at 0.0466.

Why this is worth a paper on its own. The VerSe organisers asked in 2021 for algorithms that “identify (and skip processing) a rare case”. As far as five literature sweeps reach, nobody built it. A rigorous selective-prediction treatment of vertebral level identification, validated on real film, is unoccupied ground.

02The finding that redirects the project

More examples will not move the transitional flag. The learning curve was run from 10 to 160 positive patients: 0.652 at ten, 0.704 at forty, 0.713 at a hundred and twenty. Tripling the positives bought under 0.01. The ceiling is label quality and render fidelity, not sample count. This is the single most useful negative result of the night, because it says to stop harvesting and start verifying.

And the flag's failure on real films is now diagnosed, not guessed. A control model trained on the same renders to predict patient sex scores 0.951 on renders and 0.829 on 3,600 real radiographs — so the renders carry information that transfers, and the domain gap is not the explanation. On those same real films the transitional flag retrieves known cases at chance: 5 of 144 in the top 144, where chance is 5.8. Comparing the ten highest-scoring films with the ten lowest gives it away — mean age 63 against 32, mean brightness 0.36 against 0.22. The model is reading degeneration and exposure, not anatomy.

Framing beats data. The tight lumbosacral junction view is the only field whose interval excludes chance at 0.656; the standard lumbar film sits at 0.509 and the wide field that shows the twelfth rib at 0.406, below chance. The one published model attends to the twelfth rib and counts down from it. In our renders that framing is the worst of the three. Whatever is readable here is local lumbosacral shape, and widening the field dilutes it.

03Two defects worth more than the accuracy numbers

The abstention rule declined on exactly the patients it protects

It required L5 or L6 to be in frame. Four sacralised Castellvi IIIb patients have no L5 at all, so the safety rule abstained on the positives. It now asks for the lowest mobile lumbar segment by position, never by name.

A gap in the released dataset, and a wrong flag in a second one

Record 1043 is the only case in 802 with a break in its vertebral chain: T6 through T12, L1, L2, then L4 and L5, with L3 missing. Separately, the VerSeFusion manifest claims eighteen subjects with a thirteenth thoracic vertebra; reading the masks, twelve have no such label and six carry an L6 instead. The truth is six, and they match the published table exactly. Cause: label 25 means L6 in one scheme and T13 in another. That is the third time this project has been bitten by a label map returning a finite, plausible, wrong answer.

04What is waiting for you, and why it is first

Two benches. The first is the one that unblocks everything.

Level verification bench — 179 panels, 865 vertebrae

A uniform random draw within each route, with the model's names drawn on each box. Mark right, wrong, or can't tell; press a when a whole run is right. About half an hour gives the first unbiased estimate of real-panel accuracy this project has ever had.

Why random and why first: a published chest-radiograph study found that with noisy validation labels the apparently-best model measured 45.9% when its true accuracy was 69.9%. We are currently choosing models that way. And a set chosen by the model's own uncertainty is a biased estimator that cannot be reused as a test set.

Transitional bench — 585 candidate figures

Deliberately enriched, for finding anomalies rather than measuring accuracy. Mark what each figure actually shows and which variant is visible.

One published number puts the value of this in perspective. In a diabetic-retinopathy study of 1.67 million training images, changing only the 3,737-image tuning set from majority vote to adjudicated consensus — 0.22% of the labels — moved the area under the curve from 0.942 to 0.986.

Castellvi type I is the one to worry about. It is about two thirds of all transitional anatomy, it is not separable in any source we hold, and it therefore sits inside every negative set we train and measure against. No amount of harvesting fixes that; it needs a grader, or it needs to become an explicit abstain class. The published precedent on rare classes is sobering and specific: in VerSe, with two L6 training cases no algorithm out of about ten identified an L6; with fifteen, most identified some. With six T13 cases, one of twenty-five got them all — and the authors credit the counting prior, not local shape.

06The review, and a finding it gained by losing its hypothesis

The scoping review is drafted to PRISMA-ScR with a protocol, a checklist and a proper flow diagram: 12,294 records identified, 11,980 screened after de-duplication, and 1,933 included after abstracts were fetched and the screen re-run, which changed 5,767 decisions. Both passes are reported.

The hypothesis I wrote into the draft — that the prevention literature aims away from the failing step — is not supported. The test is null and underpowered: odds ratio 1.07, spanning 0.30 to 3.84. What replaced it is sharper and testable:

Fisher p = 0.024, and the same pattern at p = 0.002 in the wider stratum. The field has not neglected the imaging step in what it writes; it has neglected to test what it recommends there. A reader can learn that counting from C2 is advised and cannot learn whether it reduces wrong-level surgery.

Reported frequency spans 4.65 orders of magnitude, 0.09 to 4,000 per 10,000 procedures, and the review declines to pool it: the low end is registries and claims, where an event must be recognised and pursued to be counted, and the high end is small series that inspected every operated level. Those are two different quantities under one name.

07What I did not do, and why

  1. No synthetic data generation. Your instruction, and the learning curve agrees: flat past forty positives.
  2. No full re-render, despite finding real renderer bugs. The scatter term was putting 50–82% of the spine's pixel value into a blurred copy of the bowel beside it, and the photon budget left 0.9 to 12 detected photons at the vertebra — both fluoroscopic settings applied to every render. Fixed and committed, and being tested as one small matched comparison rather than a re-render.
  3. A claimed renderer defect that did not survive checking. It was reported that the material model renders trabecular bone as soft tissue. Computed against the definition of the Hounsfield unit, the old model was 4.4% over-attenuating and gave slightly more bone contrast. Fixed for correctness; not the cause of anything.
  4. Radiopaedia, still not scraped. Its content signals forbid AI training and its licence is non-commercial. Eurorad turned out to carry the same signal and was also left alone.

08What I would do next, in order

  1. Spend half an hour on the verification bench. Everything else is currently unmeasurable, and the published cost of choosing models on noisy labels is 24 points of apparent accuracy.
  2. Switch the level model to an ordinal loss. Vertebral level is ordinal and we train it as 27 unordered classes. On a six-class ordinal medical grading task, an ordinal loss was worth three times what noise cleaning bought on the same data. The arm is running.
  3. Calibrate the selective predictor on real film. The curve in section 1 is on renders. A deployed threshold needs real panels, because conformal coverage has been measured collapsing from 90% to 7% under shift, and class-conditional guarantees need about 99 real cases per class.
  4. Make Castellvi I an explicit abstain class rather than leaving it in the negatives, and say so in the paper. Nothing in the literature recovers a label that no source carries.
  5. Write the selective-prediction paper. It is the one genuinely unoccupied position: the community asked for this capability five years ago and no one has built it.
