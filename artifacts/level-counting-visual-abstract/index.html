Level Counting Visual Abstract

The level, counted from the sacrum on the C-arm you already own

A vertebra detector and a counter that link overlapping fluoroscopic frames from a trusted anchor to the level in hand. No tracker, no reference frame, no capital purchase. For spine surgeons at the hospitals that will never buy a navigation system.

TL;DR for the surgeon and the CFO. Wrong-level spine surgery is rare per operation and common per career: a 34,544-case registry puts it at 1 in 830 operations (1 in 240 in the thoracic spine), and half of surveyed spine surgeons have done it once. Almost every case is a counting error under fluoroscopy, and the tools that prevent it — navigation suites — cost more than most hospitals will spend on a problem this rare. This method counts the level on the C-arm the hospital already owns: no tracker, no reference frame, no capital purchase, one learned detector and an exact count. With correct boxes it is exact on 99.2–100% of walks. The savings that can be priced are operating-room minutes (a thoracic localization takes about 15 minutes by needle-and-count and 3 with a pre-placed fiducial that itself costs $1,750–1,950 and a second visit); the savings that cannot be priced are the never event a hospital does not report, the lawsuit it does not defend, and the surgeon who does not spend those minutes worried. We ask for our own fluoroscopy, retrospectively, to measure the one number none of this can predict: how it performs on our machines and our patients.

The never event

1 in 830

operations at the wrong level in a 34,544-case registry (0.12%; thoracic 0.42%, 1 in 240). Half of surveyed surgeons have done one; 81% of events are intraoperative disorientation — a count that went wrong.

Furuya 2026 [1]; Mody 2008 [2]; Groff 2013 [3]

Operating-room time

15 → 3 min

thoracic level localization by needle-and-count versus with a pre-placed fiducial, in two small series; an OR minute costs $34–37 before physician fees (2014 dollars, 30–40% more today).

Mongardi 2021, Upadhyaya 2012 [4,5]; Childers 2018 [6]

What a wrong level costs

$75k median

indemnity per paid surgical never-event claim (IQR $29k–$215k); 17% of self-reported wrong-level events led to legal action, and 81% of litigated wrong-disc-level cases ended in a payment. Non-payment by CMS; public reporting; the patient's second operation.

Bettis 2026 [7]; Mody 2008 [2]; Goodkin 2004 [8]

Navigation today

≈ $1–2.5M

list price of a spine navigation suite plus ~10% a year in service (vendor pricing; no verified capital figure exists in the literature). It addresses screw placement; no published study shows it reduces wrong-level surgery. Penetration by site of care is unreported.

assumption, labelled; see note

Peace of mind

unmeasured

No study measures surgeon workload or satisfaction with versus without navigation, or during level localization. What is measured: 15% of surgeons have prepared the wrong level and caught it before incision, and nearly one in five has been sued for it.

Mody 2008 [2]; Groff 2013 [3]

Model, with every input labelled. Events = N × 0.12% (registry rate, all regions). Expected event cost = events × 17% litigated × $135k mean paid claim (Mehtsun 2013, Tan 2023); the per-event figure is the NPDB median and IQR — the expectation is small, the event is not. Minutes = N × 10 (needle-and-count at about 15 minutes against a 3-to-5-minute sweep; the 15 comes from two small non-randomised thoracic series and is applied to all cases: the weakest input here) × $34–37 (2014) × 1.35 (to 2026). Navigation = $1.5M capital over 7 years plus 10% service, an assumption, not a source. Reoperation, non-payment, reputation and surgeon well-being are not priced. The honest reading: the dollars are operating-room minutes; the never event is a harm and a reputation, not a line item.
