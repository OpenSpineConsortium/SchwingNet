Registration-Free Level ID Feasibility

Deep research · 102 agents · adversarially verified · 15 September 2026

Is a registration-free vertebral level classifier feasible?

Verdict

The surviving evidence supports a narrow, honest verdict. Nothing in the verified set establishes a per-level vertebra-identification accuracy for a registration-free single-frame system on real radiographs or fluoroscopy: the only registration-free labeller in scope (Huang et al. 2022, MVMS R-CNN) survived only as a description of its protocol (trained entirely on images synthesized from 297 CTs, tested on 50 AP + 50 lateral cadaveric Long-Film images from 13 specimens, no clinical patient films), and its accuracy figures (88.3% labelling, 3.3 mm) did not survive verification. What is well established is the ceiling on the harder sub-task: a single 2-D lumbar view can flag that a lumbosacral transitional vertebra is present (deep learning on 3,116 lateral films: accuracy 76.4%, sensitivity 85.1%, specificity 61.9%, AUC 0.84, below an orthopaedic specialist at 83.9%) but cannot assign a Castellvi type (human readers on radiographs: 32% sensitivity, 42% accuracy; 35.2% of AP-radiograph subtypes disagree with CT, kappa ~0.50, with IIb and IV wrong 71–85% of the time), and clinicians already miscount levels in roughly half of LSTV patients (38/71, 54%) on limited-field lumbar imaging. The comparator, LevelCheck, needs the patient's preoperative CT (or MRI) and 3D–2D registration; it placed labels in the correct level on all 398 radiographs it was run on (after excluding 21 poor-quality films), was judged helpful in 42.2% and most helpful in the thoracic spine (77.7%) versus 33.5% lumbar, but has never demonstrated a reduction in wrong-level error, and no surviving claim gives wrong-level incidence, mechanism, or a public intraoperative fluoroscopy dataset with level labels. Realistic promise for a registration-free single-frame alert: a binary LSTV / TLTV / atypical-count flag with modest specificity that triggers a whole-spine count or preoperative-CT check, with explicit abstention when the sacrum, odontoid or twelfth rib is outside the field or when a numerical variant (T13, L6, lumbarised S1) is suspected; not a Castellvi type and not an authoritative level name at the lumbosacral junction.

Findings that survived verification (8)

Each claim was voted on by three independent verifiers who tried to refute it against its sources; two refutations kill a claim. Merged duplicates are noted in the vote field.

01high confidence3-0 and 2-1 (merged claims 0, 1)

Level determination on conventional imaging (fluoroscopy, ultrasound, CT, MRI) without a whole-spine reference is imperfect and fails systematically in transitional anatomy: across 29 studies in a 2026 systematic review, only nine reported 100% accuracy (four with n = 14–43), no study involving transitional vertebrae reached 100%, and in LSTV patients level nomination on outpatient lumbar radiograph/MRI was wrong in 54% (38/71; Nakagawa 2017, 550 consecutive lumbar-surgery patients, LSTV prevalence 12.9%) against a whole-spine AP radiograph reference, with the review citing a 45–54% risk of inaccurate MRI- or fluoroscopy-based nomination in LSTV.

Evidence. Liebrand et al., Eur Spine J 2026 (peer-reviewed systematic review, 29 studies 1962–2026): 'In the studies involving transitional vertebrae no 100% was achieved.' Nakagawa primary text confirms 38/71 incorrect counts. Independently corroborated by Paik 2014 (60% of correctly diagnosed LSTVs had L5 misnumbered without a localizer) and an iliac-crest-tangent study (43.1% correct numbering in LSTV vs 96.4% controls). Qualification: Nakagawa's miscounts were on outpatient radiograph/MRI, not intraoperative fluoroscopy; the fluoroscopy wording rests on the review's other references (interventional pain procedures).

Sources. link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00586-026-09925-x · Nakagawa et al., Tohoku J Exp Med 2017;242:223-228 · Paik et al., Korean J Radiol 2014 (72/120 L5 misdefined without whole-spine localizer)

02high confidence3-0

Limited field of view is a named failure mode that applies to any single-frame method: standard sagittal/axial lumbar MRI protocols miss transitional vertebrae because they image neither the thoracolumbar nor the lumbosacral junction; the review's remedy is to count caudally from C2, compare preoperative CT/MRI with the intraoperative image, and obtain a full-spine image when needed.

Evidence. Verbatim: 'this protocol fails to detect transitional vertebrae due to limited imaging of the thoracolumbar and lumbosacral junction'; 'Preoperative MRI or CT scans should be compared with intra operative imaging and, a full spine image obtained if needed.' Six included studies counted from C2 or T1. Caveat: 'count from C2 is most reliable' is the review's recommendation, not settled consensus; C2 counting has its own error rate, and Carrino 2011 / Farshad-Amacker 2014 show LSTV can often be flagged on routine lumbar MRI without a full count.

Sources. link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00586-026-09925-x

03high confidence3-0 (merged claims 3, 4, 5)

A single lateral lumbar radiograph supports only binary LSTV presence/absence detection at modest accuracy: a ResNet-50 trained on the 2,803-film training split of 3,116 standing lateral radiographs (whole dataset 1,941 LSTV / 1,175 normal, single Korean centre) reached accuracy 76.4%, sensitivity 85.1%, specificity 61.9%, AUC 0.84 on 313 held-out films (166/195 LSTV, 73/118 normal), below an orthopaedic specialist at 83.9% and only modestly above the 62.3% prevalence baseline. Ground truth came from MRI report conclusions restricted to patients whose enumeration was resolvable on whole-spine MRI/CT or enumeration-capable radiographs, not from the input film. Failure modes were false positives from spondylolisthesis or L5–S1 disc narrowing and false negatives from subtle transitional features or minimal spatulation; the authors state some borderline normals were 'nearly indistinguishable' from LSTV on one lateral view. No Castellvi subtype was output.

Evidence. Kwak, Ro, Kang, J Clin Med 2025 (PubMed 41227066). All numbers reproduced from the paper; confusion matrix arithmetic checks. Caveats: the 313-film test set was also used to pick ResNet-50 over DINOv2 and CLIP (mild optimism bias); labels are report text, no inter-reader kappa; single centre with enriched 62% prevalence; lateral view only, so AP-visible cues (transverse-process morphology) were unavailable.

Sources. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12610351/

04high confidence3-0 on all five merged claims (7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13); claim 12 was 2-1

Castellvi typing from a single plain radiograph is unreliable even for trained readers, so a single-frame system must flag rather than assert a type. Hanhivaara 2024 (852 patients screened, 100 LSTV, four blinded readers, multimodal expert gold standard): radiography sensitivity 32%, specificity 85%, accuracy 42%, balanced accuracy 59% versus CT 76/93/77/84% and MRI 54/88/56/68%; inter-reader kappa CT 0.63–0.71, MRI 0.24–0.56, radiography 0.16–0.32. Hou 2020 (91 CT-confirmed Castellvi II–IV, AP radiograph vs coronal CT): 35.2% (32/91) of radiograph subtypes disagreed with CT; per-type accuracy IIa 76.9%, IIb 29.4%, IIIa 100% (n = 2), IIIb 100%, IV 15.4%; radiograph-vs-CT kappa 0.502/0.493 while within-modality inter-observer kappa was 0.87–0.92, i.e. readers were consistently rather than randomly wrong, mostly under-calling bony fusion as pseudoarthrosis. Both groups conclude CT coronal reconstruction is the reference for typing; radiography can detect presence in suspected cases but not type.

Evidence. Hanhivaara verbatim: 'MRI and CR showed fairly poor sensitivity and accuracy, and thus consideration should be used when classifying LSTVs with these two modalities.' Hou verbatim: 'Although AP-LPR could correctly detect MA-LSTV, it could not give accurate type classification.' The 2026 Pain Practice systematic review (48 articles) restates CT as gold standard. Qualifications: Hou's cohort was pre-selected as radiograph-suspected LSTV (100% PPV, so radiograph sensitivity for detection was never measured and Hanhivaara reports it as poor); standard AP rather than Ferguson 30° view; Hanhivaara's gold standard is one radiologist's multimodal read (incorporation bias); the MRI half is disputed by Farshad-Amacker 2013 (coronal MRI reliable). A separate claim that radiograph kappa 0.16–0.32 makes plain-film labels unusable as ground truth was voted down 1-2, though the kappa values themselves are in the abstract.

Sources. journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/02841851241289355 · pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7260820/ · pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12969546/ · Farshad-Amacker et al., Spine J 2014;14:1470-5 (AP radiograph vs coronal MRI kappa 0.29)

05medium confidence3-0 (claim 14) and 2-1 (claim 18)

No single landmark visible on one frame names a vertebra reliably, and numerical variants defeat local counting: on MRI the iliolumbar ligament identifies L5 with 85% sensitivity / 100% specificity in mixed cohorts but originates solely from L5 in only 25–38% of LSTV cases (95% of controls); costal facets 78%/97.3%, aortic bifurcation 65.8%/82.2%, psoas origin 51.2%/58.0%, conus 58.5%/68.0%; thoracolumbar transitional vertebrae make LSTV seven times more likely (Doo 2020, whole-spine CT), so partial rib-based or fluoroscopic counts propagate errors and whole-spine imaging is the accurate method. LevelCheck's authors independently list the confounders of manual counting on an intraoperative radiograph as T13, L6, lumbarised sacrum, LSTV (cited at 15–25% prevalence) and absence of the sacrum, odontoid or twelfth rib from the field of view.

Evidence. Review quotes: 'In the presence of LSTV, the ILL is not a reliable marker for the identification of L5'; 'partial counts based on ribs or fluoroscopic references can easily lead to errors'; 'No single parameter in the magnetic resonance imaging can accurately indicate the number of vertebrae without counting the levels.' De Silva 2016 full text: 'Normal anatomical variations ... extra vertebrae, missing vertebrae, or composite vertebrae – can also confound manual level counting ... T13, L6, and/or a lumbarized sacrum'; 'the lack of such landmarks within the radiographic field-of-view can challenge reliable level localization.' Confidence is medium because the Garg/Doo/Peker primary abstracts were verified only through the review's quotations plus Farshad-Amacker 2014; the older Hughes & Saifuddin 2006 (ILL at L5 in all 433 normal patients, claimed usable in LSTV) contradicts but was overturned by the larger whole-spine-referenced 2014 and 2021 studies. The inference that these are exactly the cases a morphology-based single-frame classifier cannot resolve is the synthesis's own; De Silva et al. say nothing about registration-free classifiers, and whether local morphology can name an L6 or T13 is the open question, not a finding.

Sources. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12969546/ · www.researchgate.net/publication/299566770_Utility_of_the_LevelCheck_Algorithm_f · Farshad-Amacker et al., Eur Radiol 2014 (doi 10.1007/s00330-014-3277-8) · Garg et al. 2021; Doo et al. 2020; Peker et al. 2019; Lian 2018 (as cited in the review)

06high confidence3-0 (merged claims 15, 19)

The existing clinical decision-support tool, LevelCheck, is registration-based and not a single-frame classifier: it requires the patient's preoperative CT (later extended to MRI), registers the 3-D volume to the intraoperative C-arm image with a normalised-gradient-information metric and multi-start CMA-ES, and overlays labels. In the 2016 retrospective evaluation (178 patients, 398 intraoperative radiographs, 21 further films excluded for invisible vertebral boundaries) labels fell within the correct level in all cases (PDE < 30 mm, median 6.13 mm, IQR 4.23 mm; median runtime 44.4 s with coarse manual initialisation), with some labels sitting at the vertebral periphery where prone-vs-supine curvature change deformed the anatomy.

Evidence. Otake 2013 verbatim: 'normalized gradient information similarity metric and multi-start covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy optimization'; De Silva 2016: 'Vertebral labels annotated in preoperative CT image were overlaid on intraoperative radiographs via 3D-2D registration'; 'The system also requires ready access to the patient's preoperative imaging.' A PubMed search for LevelCheck returns only 3D-2D registration papers; none is CT-free. Caveats: 100% is after exclusions and cervical cases with intentional repositioning were excluded; retrospective, single institution, Siemens-funded academic–industry partnership.

Sources. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4915373/ (Otake et al., Phys Med Biol 2013) · www.researchgate.net/publication/299566770_Utility_of_the_LevelCheck_Algorithm_f · Otake et al. 2012 Phys Med Biol (PMID 22990022); Lo et al. 2015 Spine; De Silva et al. 2017 Phys Med Biol (MRI extension); Ketcha 2017

07high confidence3-0 (merged claims 16, 17)

LevelCheck's clinical evidence is confidence support, not a measured reduction in wrong-level surgery: in the three-surgeon reader study it was judged helpful in 42.2% (168/398) of cases and improved confidence in 30.6% (122/398), never diminished performance, and in no case did the surgeon's unaided read conflict with the algorithm. Perceived utility was region-dependent and inversely related to how easy the region is to count unaided: thoracic 77.7% (23/30), cervical 46.5% (47/101), thoracolumbar 44.9% (21/69), lumbar 33.5% (66/198). The authors themselves call for prospective multi-centre trials to demonstrate a safety benefit.

Evidence. Verbatim: 'In the current study there were no cases in which the surgeon's initial assessment was in conflict with the LevelCheck output.' Discussion: 'evaluation in prospective clinical studies and/or multi-center trials will help to demonstrate the improvements to safety.' The 2018 Manbachi follow-up evaluated operational modes (Independent Check / Active Assistant) and is likewise not an outcome trial. Caveats on the per-region figures: several published fractions do not reproduce their percentages (23/30 = 76.7%; 21/69 = 30.4%; 23+47+21+66 = 157 not 168) because 'helpful' was averaged across three surgeons (overall range 31.7–53.3%); the thoracic estimate rests on 30 cases; it is the only per-region utility survey found. Notably, the region where a registration-free tool would be most wanted (lumbar/lumbosacral, where LSTV lives) is where surgeons found automated labelling least useful.

Sources. www.researchgate.net/publication/299566770_Utility_of_the_LevelCheck_Algorithm_f · Manbachi et al., Ann Biomed Eng 2018 (PMID 30051244)

08medium confidence3-0 (claim 6); associated accuracy claims 0-3 refuted

The only registration-free intraoperative vertebra labeller in the verified set was trained purely on simulated images and tested on a small cadaveric set: Huang et al. 2022 (Siewerdsen/Uneri group) trained a multi-perspective region-based CNN on images synthesised from 297 CT scans and tested on 50 AP + 50 lateral Long-Film images from 13 cadaveric specimens, with no clinical patient films reported; the SPIE 2022 precursor used 11,805 simulated Long-Film images from 370 CTs tested on 50 images of 14 cadavers. The synthesised geometry is the O-arm Long-Film slot-scan, not conventional C-arm fluoroscopy or plain radiography. The paper's reported accuracy figures (88.3% labelling, F1 > 96%, 3.3 mm, lateral 35 points worse single-view) did NOT survive verification and are not asserted here.

Evidence. Abstract verbatim: 'The network was trained on images synthesized from 297 CT images and tested on 50 AP and 50 lateral Long-Film images acquired from 13 cadaveric specimens.' Full text was paywalled, so 'entirely' simulated and 'no clinical images' are inferences from abstract silence; real-image fine-tuning cannot be excluded; whether transitional vertebrae were present in the 13 cadavers is not stated. Confidence medium because the descriptive claim is unanimous but the accompanying performance claims were refuted 0-3, so the sim-to-real gap for real C-arm frames remains unquantified in this evidence set.

Sources. www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169260722006034 (Huang Y, Jones · Huang et al., Proc SPIE 12034 (2022) precursor

Open questions

  • What per-level accuracy does a registration-free CNN reach on real (not cadaveric, not Long-Film) C-arm fluoroscopy and plain films, broken out by cervical, thoracic, thoracolumbar and lumbar, and how much of the gap is sim-to-real versus the intrinsic ambiguity of mid-thoracic and lumbosacral levels? No surviving claim answers this.
  • Can local morphology on a single AP or lateral frame distinguish L6 from L5, T13 from L1, or a lumbarised S1 from L5 when the sacrum or twelfth rib is inside the field, i.e. is the abstention set defined by field of view alone or also by numerical variants that no local cue resolves? De Silva et al. list these as confounders of counting but do not test a morphology-based classifier.
  • Which public or licensable datasets contain real intraoperative spine fluoroscopy or radiographs with whole-spine-verified level labels and transitional-anatomy annotations, under what licences and sizes, and does any include Castellvi-typed LSTV with CT reference?
  • Does a level-confirmation alert change surgeon behaviour or wrong-level rates in a prospective trial? LevelCheck's own authors call for one, none has been reported, and the only utility survey shows the lumbar spine, where LSTV lives, is where surgeons found automated labelling least helpful (33.5%).
