Counting from the sacrum

Naming the vertebral level from C-arm exposures alone — no preoperative CT, no tracker, no registration. Every number below is verified against a file on disk.

The method in one page

Am I ahead of the literature?

On naming — yes, by a wide margin

On real intraoperative C-arm lateral frames the method names the exact level in 98.2% of vertebrae. The best published registration-free naming is 73.3%, and it imports identity from a preoperative radiograph. LevelCheck, the clinical state of the art, registers to the patient's own CT — it is a ceiling, not a rival, and it costs a CT.

On detection — no, not yet, and this is the binding constraint

Finding the bodies, not naming them, is what fails. On a device held out entirely, per-body recall is 90.6% (whole-vertebra outline truth, IoU 0.5); published class-agnostic C-arm detection reaches 0.96–0.98. Recall compounds over eight to twelve bodies, so 90.6% is not counting-grade. A detector trained on 6,650 renders and no real image gets 71.7% on real radiographs against 99.1% for the same architecture trained on real film — and that 28-point sim-to-real tax matches what the field reports elsewhere almost exactly.

So the honest shape of the claim: the naming problem is solved to a standard nobody has published, and the detection problem in front of it is not. Reporting both is what makes the first one credible.

What today's audit found

Three questions were asked of the synthetic-data pipeline. Two had good answers and one did not.

The C-arm cards do sample real distributions

Of 114 device×parameter pairs, 51 are drawn per frame from a fitted Gaussian copula conditioned on the patient's water-equivalent thickness; 9 values come from DICOM headers, with kilovoltage inside the copula for two devices and a vendor constant for two more. It is distribution sampling, not noise fitted to reproduce existing images.

But almost none of that distribution was reaching the renders

A clip meant to stop implausible draws was applied to 8–11 correlated terms at once, and it saturates rather than truncates — an out-of-band draw is pinned to the boundary value, not redrawn.

The replacement keeps every marginal intact and rejects only the combinations a device never produces, using the same typicality statistic each card is already validated against on held-out frames. It reaches 99% of the joint distribution. It is implemented and tested but not adopted: a sampler that reaches more of the distribution is not automatically better training data, and the A/B that decides it is running.

Found by looking at the figure, not the numbers — the clipped kilovoltage histogram is a set of stacked spikes rather than a narrowed distribution. Second time on this project that rendering settled what two rounds of reasoning from numbers did not.

The benchmark was scored on too few scans

The CT axis named 802 abdominopelvic records, which see only the lower spine. It now names every collection here by distinct subject, counted with the same keying the train/validation split uses: TotalSegmentator v3 1,735, v2 1,170, CTSpinoPelvic1K 801, VerSe 374, C-ArmLSpine 120, and more.

What is still open

  • Whether a release id is a person. CTSpinoPelvic1K is built from CT colonography, which scans each patient both prone and supine. If two ids are one human being then the corpus is mis-split, and that would be larger than either data leak already found and fixed. An audit is running; it is not assumed to be fine.
  • One model for the whole fleet. A single detector over every C-arm, tested on a holdout spanning every device, is training now — 18,906 training frames, 56 test specimens, and a list builder that refuses to write anything if a subject or a specimen lands on both sides of the split.
  • Exposure data on disk and unused. One card records its kilovoltage as unavailable; the same acquisitions' vendor files carry it on 2,654 frames across 84 distinct values.
