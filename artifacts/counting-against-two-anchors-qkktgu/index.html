Counting Against Two Anchors

How to make a network enumerate vertebrae correctly when the anatomy is anomalous — by training almost entirely on the anatomy that is not.

01

Predicting a level name is the wrong target

Ask a network for L5 and it has a defect no amount of capacity repairs. A sixth lumbar vertebra occurs in roughly 2% of people, so the model is rewarded for never predicting one. Worse, a model that learns normal anatomy well will call every lowest lumbar vertebra L5, because that prior is right about 97% of the time.

This is the documented failure mode, and it is a property of the target, not the backbone. Oversampling fights the symptom. The target has to change.

02

Predict two counts instead, and derive the name

Two anatomical landmarks bracket the lumbar column from opposite ends, and both are locally decidable — you can identify each without knowing anything about the count. So for every vertebra voxel, predict two ordinal fields:

HeadPredictsLearned from
n_uphow many vertebrae this one sits above the sacrumevery case
n_downhow many it sits below the lowest rib-bearing vertebraevery case

The level name becomes a deterministic function of the pair. The network never predicts it.

In typical anatomy the two counts agree, and n_up + n_down is constant down the column. In transitional anatomy they disagree, and the sign of the disagreement names the phenotype. A sacralised lowest segment shortens the interval from below; a lumbar rib shortens it from above; lumbarisation runs the other way.

Both counting functions are learned from the 97% of cases that are normal.

The model never memorises sixteen lumbar-rib examples in order to recognise one. It learns to count from the sacrum, and to count from the ribs, with ~800 cases of each. The anomaly is then detected as a violation of consistency between two things it learned well — not as a rare category it saw a handful of times.

That converts anomaly classification into anomaly detection by disagreement, which is a far better-posed problem when positives are scarce. It also yields an uncertainty for free: |n_up + n_down − K| is a per-case scalar, near zero in normal anatomy and near one at a transition. A calibrated flag, not a softmax over a class that barely exists.

03

The objection that breaks the naive version

Two anchors are not enough, and the reason is the entire point of the dataset. Every anchor named so far is perturbed by exactly the variants it is meant to detect. A lumbar rib moves the lowest rib-bearing vertebra down one. An aplastic twelfth rib moves it up one. A sacralised segment moves the top of the sacrum up; a lumbarised one moves it down.

And the failure is worse than it first looks. A lumbar rib and a sacralisation both reduce n_up + n_down by one. The sum cannot tell them apart. A design resting on it would flag both correctly and name both wrongly, half the time.

04

Choose anchors that fail to different variants

Anchors defined by morphology do not move when the feature moves. Four are available, and what matters is that each variant family perturbs a different subset of them.

Rib signature

A − B  =  0 · +1 · −1

An aplastic twelfth rib leaves its costal facet behind, and a lumbar rib grows on a vertebra that never had one. So A is the control for B, and the sign names the rib variant.

Sacral signature

C − D  =  0 · non-zero

The auricular surface belongs to the ilium and is not relinquished when a lumbar segment fuses above it. C and D moving together marks a higher Castellvi grade than D moving alone.

The two families are orthogonal: rib variants perturb {A, B} and leave {C, D}; sacral variants perturb {C, D} and leave {A, B}. A case carrying both — a lumbar rib and a sacralisation, which this corpus contains — is therefore decomposable, where a single-anchor design sees one net shift and cannot say what caused it.

No anchor is individually reliable, and that is fine. The pattern of which anchors disagree is a signature, and the signature identifies the variant.

05

Where morphology has to carry it alone

When the twelfth rib is aplastic there is no lowest rib, so n_down has no origin. This is exactly the case worth detecting, and counting cannot reach it.

Thoracic and lumbar vertebrae are different bones, and every discriminating feature is patch-local: costal facets on the body (thoracic only, and present whether or not a rib grew); the costotransverse joint, a joint space where a lumbar transverse process is continuous with the pedicle; and process shape — thoracic short and club-like, lumbar long and blade-like with mammillary processes.

So a third head predicts thoracic versus lumbar type per vertebra, from morphology, and repairs the rib anchor when the rib is missing. A vertebra with costal facets and no rib is a T12 with an aplastic rib. A vertebra with no facets and a small rib is an L1 with a lumbar rib. The vertebra names the rib, not the other way round.

This is learnable for the same reason the counts are: the facet is present in every thoracic vertebra in the training set — hundreds of cases, not sixteen. On this corpus, T12 separates from L1 at AUC 0.990 and 97.3% accuracy over 1485 vertebrae, using shape alone with each patient's own size divided out.

Heads, losses, and the constraint that is free

HeadTypeLossPurpose
semanticper-voxel classesDice + CEthe segmentation

n_downdense ordinalordinal-CEcount from the ribs
typeper-voxel binaryBCE + Dicethoracic vs lumbar morphology
rib-bearingper-voxel binaryBCE + Dicealready built

Ordinal regression rather than plain regression: the targets are small integers with a natural order, and rank-consistent formulations respect that where an L2 loss does not.

The strongest supervisory signal costs nothing. Penalise |n_up + n_down − K| against the case's own constant. In normal anatomy this is a self-supervised constraint that holds on every case — so it applies to all the data, including the cases carrying no transitional label at all.

07

The patch is the real architectural tension

An ordinal count is not local. n_up at L1 needs the sacrum in the receptive field — about 265 mm from T11 to the S1 endplate on this corpus.

  • A tall narrow patch, 112 × 112 × 320 at 1 mm, spans the column in 4.01 Mvox — cheaper than nnU-Net's default 192³ at 7.08.
  • The pelvis needs 309 mm of width at p95, which that patch cannot span.
  • Going anisotropic to reach it blurs the 2–4 mm costotransverse joint — the very feature that separates a stump rib from a lumbar rib.

The ordinal heads and the morphology classes want different patches. Naming that tension is more useful than pretending one configuration serves both, and the cascade is its principled resolution: a low-resolution stage locates the sacral anchors against the whole pelvis, and hands its prediction to a full-resolution stage that resolves the costal facet at native detail.

08

Arms, in order, each with a reason

  1. ResEnc-L, tall narrow patch, semantic heads only

    The honest baseline. Establishes what the column-spanning receptive field alone buys.

  2. The same, plus the two ordinal heads and the consistency loss

    Isolates the contribution of the formulation from that of the architecture. This is the comparison the whole argument turns on.

  3. 3d_cascade_fullres

    Global context for the counting heads, native resolution for the morphology.

  4. PrimusV2 on the winner

    The generic finding that CNNs beat Transformers came from organ segmentation, where long-range ordering is not the task. Here it is the entire task, and positional embeddings are the mechanism by which a network knows which vertebra it sees.

  5. The staged count-free pipeline

    The comparator that already exists.

Run arms 1 and 2 as a matched pair on the same folds. The delta between them is the result.

09

What to measure, since Dice would hide it

  • Rib-free count accuracy, split by typical and transitional anatomy. A model 97% accurate overall and 0% on transitional cases is the null result in costume, and only the split reveals it.
  • Anchor-agreement calibration. Does |n_up + n_down − K| separate transitional from typical? An ROC on that scalar is the cleanest single figure this design can produce.
  • Stump-rib recall specifically — the case where the rib anchor fails and morphology carries it alone.
  • The failure mode when it fails. An off-by-one traceable to a named vertebra, or a silent misnumbering. The argument for two anchors is that disagreement is visible.

An anchor that did not survive contact with the data

Sacral foramina are the textbook sacrum-intrinsic anchor — four pairs is normal, five means it absorbed a lumbar segment — and would be an ideal anchor C, needing no reference to the ilium.

It does not work here, and the corpus says so plainly. Counting foramina per side across 802 records gives, for cases labelled normal: 2 in 211 cases, 3 in 324, 4 in 163, 5 in 49. The mode is three, not four, and the distribution overlaps the labelled sacralisations and lumbarisations completely. The measurement is dominated by field of view — many sacra are truncated inferiorly in an abdominal CT.

An anchor whose distribution in normal anatomy overlaps the variants it should detect is not an anchor. It is recorded here so the next person does not spend a week rediscovering it.
