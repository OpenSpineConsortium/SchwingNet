1 · What v5 is

built

Both review cohorts were complete and both on the v4 label scheme — 169/169 rib cases and 107/113 spine cases finalized, zero rejects. That mattered more than it sounds: the spine ledger reachable under the personal namespace is source_revision: v2, where the ids mean different bones, and overlaying it on a v4 base would have silently relabelled vertebrae.

The merge is anatomy‑pure. Each cohort only ever writes its own id range (ribs 34–57, thoracic 8–19), so a reviewer who strayed outside their anatomy cannot leak into v5.

The signature of a real correction

The offsets are directional, not noise. In v4 the deltas ran −1: 129, +1: 32, −2: 9 — four to one in one direction, which is what an off‑by‑one entering at the top of a field‑limited scan looks like.

After the review: −1: 71, +1: 34, −2: 4. The −1 arm fell 45% while +1 barely moved. A pass that merely reshuffled noise would have moved both.

The lumbar‑rib count also went up (21 → 25), which is the right direction: the corrections preserved anomalies instead of smoothing them into normal. That was the main risk of a review pass and it did not happen.

3 · Two QC bugs, both mine

fixed

The first pass reported 230 of 801 cases with unequal left/right rib counts. At 29% that is far above any plausible rate of anatomical asymmetry, so it was measuring something else.

It was. In 228 of 236 unmatched ribs, the rib that is present touches the top slice of the scan: the patient is not square to the gantry, so the superior field edge clips one side a level before the other. The missing ribs peak at rib 5 — mid‑thoracic, the top of a lumbosacral field — not at rib 12, and only 3 of 230 cases differ solely in the last rib. Not hypoplastic last ribs. Where the scan stopped.

And then the fix had its own bug

Excluding truncated ribs one at a time manufactures the asymmetry it is meant to remove: if left rib 5 is cut and right rib 5 is not, dropping only the left one leaves “right has a rib 5, left does not.” That still read 112 asymmetric.

The unit of exclusion has to be the rib number — if either side’s rib n is cut, drop n from both sides and compare only the levels wholly inside the field.

Rib-count asymmetry, v4cases

15 of 801 (1.9%) is a plausible rate for genuine asymmetry, and those 15 are worth a human look. The other 215 were never anything. The same fix collapsed “no vertebra within reach” from 913 to 38: those ribs were being blamed for a gap the scan created.

The limitation this exposes

57% of ribs in this dataset are clipped by the field of view. Any rib‑count phenotype has to be computed on the paired, uncut subset, or it measures patient positioning rather than anatomy.

4 · Grouping by rib‑bearing

computed

Stop asserting names; record what the image shows — this vertebra bears a rib, that one does not. The quantity that falls out is the one clinicians already use: the count of non‑rib‑bearing vertebrae between the lowest rib‑bearing vertebra and the sacrum. Normally 5, and invariant to where you start numbering, so two readers who disagree about every vertebra’s name can still agree on it.

A criterion bug worth recording

The first run marked a vertebra rib‑bearing if any rib passed within 10 mm. Proximity is not articulation: a 12th rib head at the T12/L1 junction is within 10 mm of both, and that marked L1 rib‑bearing in 61 cases where the strict incidence QC found only 14 lumbar ribs in the entire cohort.

Assigning each rib to its single nearest vertebra reconciled the two and moved the “4” group from 71 cases (9.0%) to 26 (3.3%).

Why it fails, and it is not a tuning problem

The count is derived from labelled vertebrae, and the label volume assigns L1–L5 plus a sacrum regardless of the true anatomy. Counting labelled non‑rib‑bearing vertebrae therefore recovers the labelling convention, not the patient — which is why it returns 5 for 93% of the cohort and for almost every case a radiologist called transitional.

Rib‑bearing grouping removes the thoracolumbar ambiguity, which is real and worth having. But this dataset’s transitional cases live at the lumbosacral junction, and ribs say nothing there.

The 93.3 / 3.3 / 3.3 distribution above should not be quoted as a phenotype prevalence. It is largely the label convention, and this panel is the reason we know that.

6 · What would actually work

next

The sacral boundary has to come from something other than the label that already encodes it. Two tests, both computable from masks that already exist, neither needing new annotation:

  • Does the ilium contact a vertebra you called lumbar? Dilate the ilium a few millimetres and ask whether the L5 transverse process reaches it. If it does, that is sacralization — Castellvi II–IV by definition. It never has to know how the sacrum divides internally, which is exactly the part the labels get to decide.
  • Height of the sacroiliac articulation against the labelled S1 endplate. One scalar per case: tight in normal anatomy, shifted by about a segment in transitional. Calibrate on the cases where the rib and sacral anchors already agree.

Both fail for different reasons than rib counting does, which is the property that makes a second anchor worth having. Where they agree the numbering is settled without a human; where they disagree, that is the transitional cohort, and it triages itself.

The honest framing for the paper

With a lumbosacral field of view there is no imaging gold standard for vertebral identity — counting from C2 on whole‑spine imaging is the only definitive method, which is why LSTV is chronically mislabelled in practice. These scans cannot resolve it in principle. That is an argument for recording evidence and disagreement rather than a name, not a limitation to bury.

Method & thresholds

  • Incidence. Rib N is on T‑N when the minimum surface distance is ≤ 10 mm. Thoracic bodies sit about 20 mm apart, so 10 mm separates the right vertebra from an off‑by‑one neighbour without flagging a small segmentation gap. Panel C tests whether that threshold cuts through a mode or between two.
  • A vertebra anchors a rib only at ≥ 6,000 voxels, so an FOV‑edge sliver cannot claim a rib whose true vertebra is out of view.
  • Rib‑bearing means this vertebra is that rib’s nearest, not merely within 10 mm.
  • Truncated means the structure touches any face of the volume. Truncated ribs are excluded from counts by rib number, never individually.
  • Determinate means sacrum present, a rib‑bearing vertebra present, and every id between them labelled. 20 cases fail this and are excluded rather than undercounted — a missing level would fabricate a sacralization.
  • Misnumbered case means ≥ 3 ribs at one consistent delta. One stray offset is a segmentation artefact; a whole side shifted is a renumber.
