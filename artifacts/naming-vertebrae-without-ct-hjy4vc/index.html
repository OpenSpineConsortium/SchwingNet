Naming Vertebrae Without CT

Registration-free vertebral level identification

Naming vertebrae from the C-arm, without a CT

A short sweep of overlapping fluoroscopic exposures builds a map of the patient's own spine. Later spot films are matched onto that map and named — including in the mid-thoracic spine, where there is nothing to count from and where surgeons currently resort to implanted fiducials.

18 September 2026 · every figure below is measured on spines the system was not trained on

The number, and what it is. On held-out real radiographs, a spot film showing three vertebrae and containing no landmark is named correctly 93.9% of the time once matched onto the sweep, against 85.7% judged on its own pixels. With the automatic vertebra-finder in the loop rather than a human's boxes, 90.4%.

That single number is the end of a chain of six steps, each of which can fail on its own. The next section is that chain, with where each step actually stands.

The six steps, and where each one stands

1 — Take the sweep

The radiographer starts with a landmark at the edge of the field — the sacrum going up, C2 coming down — and moves the C-arm toward the level of interest, leaving two vertebral bodies of the previous image still in view. Three exposures covers most lumbar work; five or six reaches the mid-thoracic spine.

Two bodies is not arbitrary. With one body of overlap the linker has a single vertebra to agree on, and the lumbar column is periodic enough that one body can align a whole frame one level off. Measured on held-out spines: half a body completes the chain on 88.3% of spines, one body 96.7%, two bodies 97.5%, four bodies 82.5% — worse, at more than twice the exposures, because every extra frame is another link that can be joined wrongly.

2 — Find the vertebrae

A class-agnostic detector puts a box round each vertebral body. It never names anything; naming is a separate step. On held-out real film it finds 97.6% of bodies.

The misses are not evenly spread. Three in 243, and two of the three are the sacrum — which is not one miss among many, because the sacrum is the landmark the whole chain anchors on. Lowering the detector's threshold does not help: the miss rate is 2.42% at confidence 0.25, 0.10 and 0.05 alike, so those bodies are not sitting just below the cut. What a lower threshold does buy is twice the spurious boxes and a chain that survives on 8% of films instead of 40%.

So the sacrum gets its own detector. Trained on the 4,526 images that carry a sacrum box, single class, split by patient: precision 98.2%, recall 96.9%.

3 — Repair the boxes (what "box repair" means)

Vertebrae in one frame sit in a line at nearly constant spacing. The detector does not get to use that — it scores each box by itself. Two errors follow, and both are repairable without touching the model:

  • Two boxes much closer together than the spacing are almost certainly one vertebra found twice. Merge them.
  • Two boxes much further apart than the spacing mean a vertebra was missed, and its neighbours say exactly where it should have been. Put one there.

That is all it is: measure the typical gap between boxes in this frame, then fix the pairs that are obviously too close or too far. Held out, it takes duplicated bodies from 3.03% to 0.61% and naming from 83.0 / 85.6 / 90.2 to 87.2 / 90.4 / 91.4% at two, three and four vertebrae — closing more than half the remaining distance to what a human's boxes achieve.

The design decision that matters here. It uses the spacing measured in that frame, and never a count. The published lumbar baseline does the opposite: it decodes exactly five boxes because “the human spine typically consists of five lumbar vertebrae”, and its own authors report the consequence — on a six-lumbar spine it drops a vertebra, and on a fused one it puts the fifth box on the sacrum. That failure is structural, and it fires in exactly the population this work exists for. Repairing from local spacing means a six-lumbar spine is handled like any other.

Two things it deliberately refuses. It fills at most two bodies into one gap — beyond that the spacing estimate is being stretched further than it can carry, and a run of invented boxes is worse than an honest hole. And it never extrapolates past the ends of a run: a missing sacrum has no neighbour below it, and inventing one would fabricate the landmark everything else depends on.

4 — Link the frames

Consecutive exposures are matched by image correlation, restricted to a search window around where the protocol says the C-arm went. The vertebrae shared between two frames are identified, and the chain is built.

This step contained the single largest error in the project. The original rule accepted a shared block only if every pair in it was confirmed, so one bad box collapsed the whole overlap and every genuinely shared vertebra was re-entered as new — which is to say, duplicated. Requiring a majority rather than unanimity took duplication from 21.8% to 3.0% and chain naming from 67.4% to 91.8%. With a human's boxes the two rules are identical to the second decimal, which is what made the change safe to adopt.

5 — Name the chain

The encoder names each vertebra from its own pixels at a fixed anatomical scale, and a run-level head reads the sequence. On held-out real film every vertebra in the chain is named correctly on 100% of films; on CT renders, 95.0%.

Nothing counts from the landmark. That is the whole thesis: the landmark fixes the chain, and the chain is named by what each bone looks like.

6 — Place the spot film

A later limited-field exposure is matched onto the stitched map by correlation. This is the step that replaces CT registration, and it costs milliseconds.

Normal against transitional, per region

On ordinary spines the per-level figures are flat: 93–97% from T11 to L5 with three vertebrae in view, with no level at which the method falls away.

On transitional spines the honest answer is that we do not know. After excluding every spine the model was trained on, eight spines with transitional anatomy remain, and exactly one with six lumbar vertebrae — which the system named wrongly. Eight spines cannot distinguish 60% from 95%, and no figure is quoted from them.

A cross-validation over all 376 transitional patients is running: each round trains without a third of them and tests on exactly that third, which is the only way to get a real number from the data on hand. It cannot create examples that do not exist.

The two counting paradigms score near-zero not because they are inaccurate but because they refuse to answer: with no landmark in a tight field there is nothing to count from, so they abstain on 97–98% of frames. That abstention is the clinical problem, and it is what this work exists to remove. Per region at the tightest field, ours reads cervical 97.6%, thoracic 95.5%, lumbar 85.4%, sacrum 93.5%.

The published per-level detector of Klinwichit et al. is training on our split now, to add a fourth row measured rather than quoted. A broader search for registration-free comparators is running; any method with public code or weights will be re-implemented and added here.

Two of those rows are not the same task. A registration success rate says the software found the right alignment to a CT that already carried the labels; it never names a vertebra from a radiograph. And a whole lumbar film with the sacrum in it is a counting problem — a simple rule that finds the sacrum and counts up does about as well as any network. The task that separates methods is the one a surgeon actually faces: a tight field with no landmark in it.

For scale on the clinical cost: half of surveyed neurosurgeons report at least one wrong-level operation in their career, and one prospective series found the initial exposure at the wrong level in 15 of 100 consecutive lumbar discectomies. One quaternary centre performed 179 preoperative percutaneous gold-fiducial placements over 38 months, 176 of them thoracic, at a mean of 52 minutes and 183 mGy each — a second invasive procedure, scheduled in advance, because the level was judged un-nameable in theatre.

The bar that actually matters

The clinical comparator is not a network, it is an assisted surgeon. A surgeon working unaided on difficult C-arm films names the level correctly 69.6% of the time. The same surgeon with a CT-registration overlay reaches 97.6% — but only in a case that had a preoperative CT, that was segmented, and that was labelled.

So the question this work has to answer is not “is the network 97% accurate”. It is: does a surgeon assisted by this system reach the accuracy of a surgeon assisted by CT registration, in cases that have no CT? That is a different measurement from any reported above, and nothing here answers it yet.

The two are not interchangeable. Every figure in this paper is the machine judged alone against a reference standard. The 97.6% is a human and a machine together, and a human plus an imperfect aid can exceed either alone — a wrong suggestion that a surgeon overrules costs nothing, while a correct one they would otherwise have missed is the whole benefit. It can also go the other way: a confident wrong suggestion can talk a surgeon out of a correct read. Both directions have to be measured, not assumed.

What was decided along the way

  • Name from morphology, never from a count. Every failure mode in the published work traces to a count: a fixed cardinality, or a tally from a landmark that a transitional segment corrupts.
  • Repair from local spacing, not from an expected number. The same principle one level down.
  • A majority, not unanimity, when linking frames. Worth 24 points of chain naming, and provably free where the boxes are correct.
  • Delimit at the disc, not at the fusion. Whether a transverse process has bridged is a poor guide — on a third of these the bridge is joint-like in one place and solid in another, and expert readers disagree about which endplate is sacral (kappa 0.32–0.38). The disc is the one feature that is both local and decidable.
  • Give the sacrum its own model. It is not a four-cornered body and should not be asked of a model trained on four-cornered bodies.

A reader study of assisted naming

The measurement the clinical claim actually needs, and the next thing to be built. Surgeons sign in to a web tool and name the level on a series of limited-field films, each film seen twice — once unaided and once with the system's answer shown, separated in time and in randomised order so the first read cannot be recalled during the second.

What it yields: the unaided accuracy of this reader group, which anchors everything to a local baseline rather than to a published one; the assisted accuracy, which is the number comparable to the 97.6% reported for CT-registration overlay; the rate at which a correct suggestion rescues a wrong read; and — equally important — the rate at which a wrong suggestion overturns a correct read, which is the harm this kind of aid can do and which is rarely reported.

Films will be stratified so transitional spines are over-represented relative to prevalence, since that is where assistance should matter most and where a system trained mostly on ordinary anatomy is most likely to mislead. The tool is a variation of the reading sites already running.

This is a reader study on stored images, not a clinical trial: no patient is imaged for it and no operative decision depends on it. It measures whether the aid helps a human, which is the claim, and it does not measure outcome.

The reading programme, and what it can be expected to buy

The single intervention that has ever moved the six-lumbar number was real examples. Every algorithmic fix — re-weighting, synthetic oversampling, pretraining on simulated films, two different pseudolabel admission rules — moved nothing. Then 83 real clinical images of six-lumbar spines entered training and accuracy at that vertebra went from 67.4% to 73.5% on held-out patients.

Two review sites are live, and the queue is now ordered by how likely the model thinks each film is transitional — a score that runs from a median of 0.007 to a maximum of 0.9995, against the decoder's previous constant of 0.0288 at every percentile, which ranked nothing.

Pseudolabel quality, measured against human names. On ordinary spines under a shared counting convention the decoder's names agree with human readers on 96.3% of cervical bodies and 89.9% of lumbar bodies on anchored films. That is not evidence they are right on anomalous spines — training on them cost twenty points of anomalous accuracy, which is why the deployed configuration excludes them and why a reader's judgement is the intervention with the most to give.

What to expect. If the reading programme converts even a third of the 3,381 high-scoring films into confirmed labels, the six-lumbar training population goes from 213 patients to well over a thousand, and from almost entirely simulated to substantially real. On the one occasion that ratio moved before, it bought six points.

Limits, stated plainly

  • Sample sizes are small — 12 to 120 spines per arm — because everything the model was trained on had to be excluded. An earlier version of this work reported figures that did not have that property; the audit that found it is recorded in the repository.
  • Transitional performance is unmeasured, not poor. Eight held-out spines.
  • Oblique projections are much harder and a C-arm is rarely square to the patient.
  • A partially lumbarised S1 has no label anywhere in the corpus — 5,110 images carry a sacrum box and zero carry an S1 box — so no model can currently be asked to place one. Readers are now drawing them.
  • Nothing here has been used on a patient, and there is no prospective clinical result.
