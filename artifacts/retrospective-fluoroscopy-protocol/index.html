* * * Summary & Purpose * * *

Study Title: Retrospective collection of intraoperative fluoroscopic images and videos from spine surgery at the Detroit Medical Center for the development and validation of an algorithm that counts vertebral levels Proposed Start Date: Upon IRB and DMC approval [to confirm: target January 2027] Proposed End Date: [to confirm: suggest two years after approval to cover analysis and publication]

1. Summary

a) Brief summary in non-technical terms (≤200 words):

During spine surgery the surgeon must find the correct vertebra before cutting. This is usually done by counting vertebrae on X-ray pictures taken in the operating room with a C-arm machine. Counting can go wrong: in a registry of 34,544 spine operations, about one in 830 (0.12%) was performed at the wrong level, and most of those errors came from losing one's place while counting. We have built a computer method that counts vertebrae automatically from the same C-arm pictures the surgeon already takes. It has been tested on public CT scans and X-rays, but not yet on real operating-room images from our hospital, and computer methods often perform worse on images from a machine they have not seen. This study will collect the C-arm images and short videos already stored in the Detroit Medical Center's imaging archive from spine operations performed between 2021 and 2026, remove all patient identifiers, and use them to train and test the counting method. The surgeon's operative note tells us which level was actually operated on, so we can check whether the method counts correctly. No patient will be contacted and no care will change.

2. Purpose

a) Purpose and hypotheses/research questions:

The purpose is to obtain intraoperative fluoroscopic images from the C-arms actually used at the Detroit Medical Center, with the operated level known from the operative note, so that the vertebral-body detector at the front of our counting method can be trained and tested in the domain where it would be used.

Specific aims.

  • Aim 1. Assemble a de-identified, coded dataset of archived intraoperative C-arm still images and cine loops from spine operations performed by Dr. To Do at the DMC between 2021 and 2026, with the operated level recorded from the operative note and, where present, post-operative imaging.
  • Aim 2. Annotate vertebral bodies, the anchor structures (sacrum, C2) and radiopaque instruments on these frames; fine-tune the detector on a patient-level training split; measure per-body detector recall on held-out cases (primary endpoint).
  • Aim 3. On archived series that contain an anchor and consecutive overlapping frames (in practice, cine loops and some still-image series), measure count exactness against the operative note, the decline rate, and agreement between the vertebra the method assigns to the surgeon's confirming instrument frame and the level the surgeon documented (secondary endpoints).

Hypotheses.

  • H1. A detector fine-tuned on DMC fluoroscopy will reach per-body recall of at least 97% on held-out DMC cases (target 95% CI half-width 1 point). Context: the same architecture reached 99.14% on real radiographs when trained on real radiographs and 71.67% when trained on synthetic renders only.
  • H2. With in-domain detector boxes, count exactness on reached walks will be consistent with the 99.17–100% observed on public CT renders and real radiographs, rather than the 30.06% observed when the detector was trained out of domain.
  • H3. The vertebra assigned to the surgeon's confirming instrument frame will agree with the level documented in the operative note at a rate consistent with the 99.31% (5,385 confirmations) and 98.57% (6,343 confirmations) observed on public data.

b) What do the investigators hope to learn from this project?

Whether the learned component of the method — the detector, which is where the deployment risk lies — works on this hospital's own C-arm images and patients; what fraction of archived series permit a full count from an anchor (a feasibility outcome that is unknown); how the method's count and confirming-frame assignment agree with the surgeon's documented level; and where failures occur (spinal region, prior instrumentation, transitional anatomy, image quality). These answers decide whether a prospective observational study in the operating room (planned follow-on study, described in the attached Protocol document) is justified.

* * * Background, Rationale, Data Analysis and Procedures * * *

3. Background, Rationale, Data Analysis, and Procedures

Section A

a) Relevant background

i) Current knowledge and literature. Wrong-level spine surgery is rare per operation and common per surgeon. In a registry of 34,544 degenerative spine operations across 27 hospitals (2012–2022), the wrong level was operated in 0.12% (95% CI 0.09–0.16): thoracic 0.42% (CI 0.16–1.08), lumbar 0.13%, cervical 0.05%. Intraoperative disorientation was the cause in 81.3% (Furuya M et al. Bone Joint J 2026;108-B(5):673-680, PMID 42061893). In self-report, 418 events occurred in 1.3 million procedures (1 in 3,110); 50% of 415 surgeons had performed one, 15% had prepared the wrong level and caught it, and 17% of events led to legal action (Mody MG et al. Spine 2008;33:194-8, PMID 18197106). Almost 50% of 569 AANS/CNS Spine Section respondents reported a wrong-level event and nearly 20% had been sued for it (Groff MW et al. World Neurosurg 2013;79:585-92, PMID 22480979). In 2024 the Joint Commission recorded 127 wrong surgeries, 9 wrong-level and 7 spinal, with 36% causing severe permanent harm; NHS England recorded 185 wrong-site never events in 2024/25.

The cost is borne by patients, surgeons and institutions. Of 68 litigated wrong-disc-level cases, 81% (55/68) ended in a payment; surgeons won 42% of jury trials (Goodkin R, Laska LL. Surg Neurol 2004;61:323-7, PMID 15031066). The National Practitioner Data Bank median payment for a surgical never event is $75,220 (IQR $28,847–$215,003, 2025 dollars) (Bettis T et al. Patient Saf Surg 2026, DOI 10.1186/s13037-026-00508-9); the mean paid claim is about $133k–136k (Mehtsun WT, Ibrahim AM, Diener-West M, Pronovost PJ, Makary MA. Surgical never events in the United States. Surgery 2013;153(4):465-72, DOI 10.1016/j.surg.2012.10.005; Tan J, Ross JM, Wright D, Pimentel MPT, Urman RD. A contemporary analysis of closed claims related to wrong-site surgery. Jt Comm J Qual Patient Saf 2023;49(5):265-73, PMID 36925434). In the NHS, 978 spinal claims over five years were valued at £535.5M, including 26 never events (Machin JT et al. Eur Spine J 2018;27:2693-9, PMID 30151803).

The standard safeguard is a count. Needles are placed every few levels and counted cephalad from the sacrum on lateral fluoroscopy; surgeons disagree on which landmark to count (pedicle 67%, spinous process 49% in the thoracic spine) (Strong MJ et al. J Neurosurg Spine 2021;36:792, PMC10193475; Mayer JE et al. Spine J 2014;14:741-8). Site-verification protocols failed to prevent about one-third of wrong-level cases in the one study that assessed them (Longo UG et al. J Bone Joint Surg Br 2012;94-B:1546-50, PMID 23109638). The recommended safeguard — imaging after exposure against a marked fixed structure — is itself a count (DeVine J et al. Spine 2010;35(9 Suppl):S28-36, PMID 20407349). The alternative of pre-operative fiducial placement is costly: at Mayo, 179 fiducials (176 thoracic) over 38 months took a mean of 51.9 minutes and 183 mGy each (Siminski et al. AJNR 2023;44:618-622, PMID 37080723); at an operating-room cost of $33.78 median / $37.45 mean per minute, FY2014, before physician fees (Childers CP, Maggard-Gibbons M. JAMA Surg 2018;153(4):e176233) that is roughly $1,750–1,950 per placement in 2014 dollars. Not available in the literature, and stated so here: a verified capital cost of a navigation suite, any study showing that navigation reduces wrong-level surgery, and any measure of surgeon workload with versus without navigation.

The method under study. The investigators have developed a method that counts the vertebral level under intraoperative fluoroscopy on the C-arm the hospital already owns. The first exposure is anchored at the sacrum (or C2); the C-arm is moved one field minus two vertebral bodies; a second exposure is taken; consecutive overlapping frames are linked by normalised cross-correlation restricted to within 0.6 of a vertebral body of the commanded step; the target is read as the k-th vertebra from the anchor. The only learned component is a detector of vertebral bodies and the anchor class. Nothing learned decides the level, and no name (for example "L4") is formed. It requires no tracker, no reference frame, no pre-operative CT and no capital equipment. On public labelled CT renders and real radiographs the count was exact on 99.17–100% of reached walks across twelve arms of 16–801 spines: from C2, 834/834 walks on 76 whole-spine studies and 1,205/1,209 (99.67%; 95% CI 99.16–99.91) on 262 real lateral radiographs; from the sacrum, 6,234/6,255 (99.66%; CI 99.49–99.79) on 801 abdominopelvic spines and 7,168/7,228 (99.17%) on 522 second-institution spines. A confirming frame with a radiopaque instrument was assigned to the correct vertebra on 99.31% of 5,385 confirmations and 98.57% of 6,343. A manuscript is in preparation (private repository spinesurg-ct-nnunet). Work on public de-identified datasets was determined Not Human Participant Research by the WSU IRB (determination 2026_269, September 2, 2026); that determination does not cover PACS data, hence this protocol.

ii) Rationale for conducting the research. The deployment risk is the detector. The film-trained detector reaches 99.14% per-body recall on real radiographs; the same architecture trained on synthetic renders reaches 71.67%; and when a render-trained detector's boxes were used, count exactness fell from 100% to 30.06% on 79 held-out spines. Synthetic fluoroscopy from CT does not close this gap: on 366 real C-arm frames of six cadaveric pelves with matching CT and exact pose (the DeepFluoro dataset), a physics renderer explains 80.9% of a real frame's variance (93.9% with a per-frame smooth field, against a 99.5% quantum-noise ceiling); a detector trained only on calibrated renders of that dataset's own CTs finds the sacrum on about 96–98% of real frames, but trained on renders of other living whole-body CTs it finds it on about 64–77%, and three training seeds of one dataset spanned 26 points. Synthetic fluoroscopy is therefore close in physics but tuned to one C-arm and one population. The number that matters — performance on our own machines and our own patients — requires real fluoroscopy from this institution. No prospective study should be started until that number is known.

iii) Rationale for the selected participant population. Adults who underwent spine surgery with intraoperative fluoroscopy at the DMC, operated by a single neurosurgeon (Dr. To Do) between 2021 and 2026. A single surgeon gives consistent operative-note documentation of the localisation technique and the operated level, which is the reference standard. The five-year window is chosen to reach the target of [to confirm: N cases, from a pilot PACS query] and to span the C-arm units in use at the site. Cervical, thoracic and lumbar cases are all included because the method's measured performance differs by region (lumbar 99.73%, thoracic 95.45% on 801 spines; 99.33/97.41/83.84% lumbar/thoracic/cervical on 522) and the study must report each.

b) Statistical methods and plan for analysis

Data split. Cases are split by patient, never by frame. A fixed held-out test set of [to confirm: held-out fraction of cases] is drawn before any training, stratified by spinal region and by presence of prior instrumentation. The remaining cases are used for fine-tuning and for patient-level 5-fold cross-validation, so that every case receives a prediction from a model that did not train on it.

Primary endpoint. Per-body detector recall on the held-out test set: the fraction of annotated vertebral bodies (and anchors) that the detector finds, using the same box-matching criterion as the manuscript [to confirm: the overlap criterion]. Reported with a 95% confidence interval computed by patient-level cluster bootstrap (bodies cluster within frames and frames within patients; see c), overall and by region, by presence of hardware, and by image type (still versus cine frame). The pre-specified comparison is the render-only detector (71.67% on real radiographs) applied to the same held-out frames; the difference in recall is reported with its confidence interval.

Secondary endpoints.

  1. Count exactness: on each archived series that contains an anchor and a chain of consecutive overlapping frames, whether the k-th vertebra from the anchor equals the level named in the operative note (post-operative imaging is the reference where the note is ambiguous or a re-localisation is documented). Reported on reached walks with exact 95% CI.
  2. Decline rate: the fraction of series on which the method produces no count because the overlap constraint is not satisfied, and the fraction of cases with no anchored series at all. This is also the feasibility outcome for Aim 3.
  3. Agreement with the surgeon's documented level: on frames containing a radiopaque instrument at the target, whether the vertebra the method assigns to the instrument equals the level documented in the operative note; reported with exact 95% CI and, for cases with post-operative imaging, against that imaging.
  4. Failure characterisation: descriptive tabulation of misses and miscounts by region, hardware, transitional anatomy (as noted in the radiology report) and C-arm model (from the DICOM manufacturer/model fields; serial numbers are not retained).

Limitation stated in advance. Archived still images were not acquired under the method's stepping protocol, so the fraction of cases on which a full count is possible is unknown and is expected to be a minority [to confirm]. Cine loops, whose consecutive frames overlap by construction, are the main source for the count endpoints. The primary endpoint (detection) does not depend on this.

Analyses will be done in Python on de-identified data; the analysis code will be frozen and the test set sealed before fine-tuning begins.

c) Justification for sample size

The sample size is set by the primary endpoint. To estimate per-body recall of 97% with a 95% confidence-interval half-width of 1 point, the normal approximation n = z²p(1−p)/E² gives n = 3.8416 × 0.97 × 0.03 / 0.0001 ≈ 1,118, so about 1,100 labelled bodies are needed in the held-out set. (To hold 99% recall to a half-width of 0.5 points would need about 1,520.) Because bodies cluster within frames and patients, the confidence interval for the primary endpoint will be computed by patient-level cluster bootstrap; the target half-width of 1 point is a planning value and the achieved width will be reported. The held-out set is [to confirm: held-out fraction] of eligible cases; with [to confirm: mean number of archived intraoperative frames per case, from a pilot query of 20 cases] frames per case and several visible bodies per frame, whether the held-out set reaches 1,100 bodies after unusable frames are removed will be checked on the pilot query before the test set is sealed. For the secondary count endpoints, 5-fold cross-validation by patient gives every case a held-out prediction; for illustration, at 99% exactness the 95% half-width is about 0.6 points on 1,000 evaluated walks and about 1.4 points on 200, so the achievable precision depends on how many anchored series the archive contains. We therefore request all eligible cases in the window, estimated [to confirm: N cases] pending a pilot PACS query (about [to confirm: N] frames plus cine loops); all eligible cases will be used and the achieved precision reported [to confirm: Dr. To Do's five-year case volume with archived fluoroscopy].

Review type requested: Expedited, Category 5

Two review types could fit this study:

  1. Exempt Category 4 (45 CFR 46.104(d)(4)), qualification (iii): secondary research on identifiable health information regulated under HIPAA. All data exist before submission, no participant is contacted, and no attempt is made to re-identify anyone. The WSU form applies limited IRB review to Exempt Categories 2 and 3 only, so an Exempt 4 determination would carry no limited review.
  2. Expedited Category 5 (45 CFR 46.110): research involving materials (data, documents, records) that have been collected, or will be collected, solely for non-research purposes (medical treatment). The precedent protocol at this institution, IRB-21-10-4123, a retrospective chart review of 2,000 DMC records, was reviewed by the expedited procedure.

We request Expedited Category 5, for four reasons. (a) A coded master list linking study IDs to medical record numbers is retained for the duration of data abstraction, because the operative note and post-operative imaging must be retrieved from the EMR after the images are exported. (b) The images carry burned-in patient text in the pixel data and identifiers in the DICOM header, so the study team handles identifiable images before de-identification; the IRB should see and approve the de-identification procedure. (c) Multi-frame cine loops are clinical video recordings and the IRB may wish to review their handling under the expedited pathway. (d) Expedited review yields an IRB approval (rather than a concurrence of exemption), and research that may support an FDA submission is not eligible for exemption: the data from this study may be included in a future 510(k) pre-submission (checklist, FDA row), so the Exempt route is closed. Title. Prospective observational evaluation of an automatic vertebral-level count from intraoperative fluoroscopy during spine surgery at the Detroit Medical Center (shadow mode).

Review type. Human-subjects review: Expedited, Category 5 (clinical images and records collected solely for non-research purposes, collected prospectively) [to confirm with the IRB]. Device status: this is expected to be an IDE-exempt clinical investigation of a non-significant-risk diagnostic device under 21 CFR 812.2(c)(3) (noninvasive; output not used for patient management); 21 CFR 50/56 apply, and a waiver of consent under 21 CFR 50.22 will be requested. The IRB's written determination will be sought.

Design. No change to care. The surgeon localises the level exactly as usual (needle count on lateral fluoroscopy, confirming frame with an instrument). An external unit connected to the C-arm's video output by a frame grabber captures the images the surgeon already takes; the counting method runs on that unit; its output is not shown to the surgeon or to anyone in the room during the case. No additional exposure is made for the study. After the case, a study team member records the surgeon's documented level (operative note), the method's count and its decline status, and the post-operative imaging read where present. Discrepancies are reviewed after the case only.

Prerequisites (before submission). Protocol 1 endpoints met on DMC data; phantom sweeps on the WSU C-arm in Room 0312 Elliman under the OEHS Radiation Safety and DLAR rules (dosimetry enrolled and training forms filed at least 15 business days before use; RSC approval); cadaveric validation through the WSU Body Bequest Program (not human-subjects research; requires a program proposal and biosafety review); DMC biomedical engineering approval for connecting the frame grabber to the C-arm video output [to confirm].

Population. Adults undergoing spine surgery with fluoroscopic localisation at the DMC by participating surgeons [to confirm: which surgeons and sites]. Sample size to be computed from Protocol 1's measured exactness [to confirm].

Endpoints. Primary: agreement between the method's count and the surgeon's documented level, with the decline rate. Secondary: per-body recall on the captured frames; time from first exposure to confirmed level as documented in the anaesthesia and nursing records; number of exposures. No published measure of surgeon workload or satisfaction with versus without navigation was found; a short, standard workload questionnaire completed by the surgeon after the case would be added [to confirm the instrument], with the surgeon as a consented participant (WSU employee section applies).

Consent. Patients: a waiver of consent under 21 CFR 50.22 (minimal risk) will be requested, or an information sheet if the IRB prefers [to confirm]; there is no intervention, no added radiation and no change to care, and the images captured are the clinical images. Surgeons: written consent for the questionnaire.

Confidentiality. As in Protocol 1: coded data, de-identification of captured frames at the unit, master list held separately by Dr. Schwing, encrypted institutional storage.

Stopping rule. The study is observational; the method's output cannot affect a patient. It would be paused for IRB review if the captured data or unit interfered with any operating-room equipment or workflow [to confirm: DMC biomedical engineering requirements].
