# DRR realism against our results: is SyntheX's parity a contradiction?

Source: the thread "Make the textbook drive the research", 2026-10-24, issue #217, on the owner's concern of 2026-10-24: that the Johns Hopkins group around Mathias Unberath says the realism and noise of a digitally reconstructed radiograph (DRR) can be engineered until a network trained on DRRs matches one trained on real or fluoroscopic X-rays, while the owner's own attempts to do the same, with a neural network or with physics noise, came out decidedly negative, and the owner's hypothesis that the group asks less of its network, landmarks only. Confidential with the rest of this repository (docs/project.md): it names the project's methods and results, so nothing of it leaves before the provisional is filed.

Grades: Published is shown in the paper named, read on 2026-10-24 at the link given in section 6; Reported is a project result read from spine-program's STATUS.md, an artifact or a research note, whose result file was not read here; Inferred is reasoned here. A paper reached only through another note of this repository is marked so, with that note named.

## 1. The answer in short

- The owner's hypothesis is supported, with a qualification below, and it is not only about landmarks. The group's parity claim (SyntheX: Gao, Killeen et al., Nature Machine Intelligence 2023) is real but narrow: it is measured on the hip, on cadaveric pelves, with the synthetic training images rendered from the CTs of the same cadaver population at the poses of the real frames, against a real-trained control of a few hundred frames, on a metric that averages millimetres only over landmarks whose heatmap the network is confident about (a normalised cross-correlation above 0.9), a rule the real-trained control is scored by too, and on Dice over large structures in which the lumbar spine is one merged class (Published).
- Ours asks for more on three axes at once. The task is to find every vertebral body and give each its level, where a miss breaks the count and a one-level shift is the failure; the test is clinical C-arm frames from a device never seen in training, against training renders of living CTs; and the comparison is with a detector trained on thousands of real frames of other devices, at the high-recall end that counting needs (Reported, Inferred).
- Nothing in the group's work contradicts ours on the same task: no paper of theirs trains on renders and names or counts vertebral levels on real fluoroscopy. The one place their models meet our task, FluoroSAM prompted with a level's name on a real cadaver frame, gives Dice 0.16 to 0.25 for L3, L4 and L5, and the paper attaches Dice 0.04, naming no prompt, to its remark that one image may not tell similar levels apart (Killeen et al., arXiv 2412.08020, Table 1 and Section 4.1; Published), which agrees with our renders-only detection failure, though not with our encoder, which named lateral levels from renders (below). Outside that lab but at Johns Hopkins, Huang 2022 (Uneri, Siewerdsen) labelled levels at 88.3 percent on real intraoperative Long-Film images of cadavers with a render-trained model, the nearest counterexample, on an extended-field mode rather than fluoroscopy (section 3.6).
- And where our setting matched theirs, our result matched theirs. A detector trained on renders of DeepFluoro's own CTs found the sacrum on 90 to 97 percent of DeepFluoro's real frames, specimens held out in turn, against 64 to 77 percent when trained on renders of living CTs (Counting Levels Under Fluoroscopy; Reported, a record, not a final value: the record has not settled the exact values). That is SyntheX's matched design run on our detector, and the transfer is high as theirs was; but no detector trained on DeepFluoro's real frames under the same protocol was run, so parity in their sense, renders against a real-trained control, was not measured (the nearest, a real-frame detector, found the sacrum on 90 of 90 frames of one held-out specimen, STATUS.md A6.10) (Inferred). Our naming partly transferred: the level encoder, whose only intraoperative appearance came from renders, named lateral C-ArmLSpine frames zero-shot at 96.4 percent (595 of 617, [92.3, 98.4]) but AP frames at 43.7 percent (276 of 631); the lateral figure is over detected bodies, and counting the 59 undetected as wrong gives 88.0 percent (STATUS.md A2.1, A2.2, A2.6; Reported). What failed is detection on an unseen clinical device from living-CT renders, which SyntheX never tested.
- Both hold if synthetic parity depends on population match, task granularity, baseline size and metric threshold, and SyntheX sits at the favourable end of all four. The one experiment that would settle it for SchwingNet is SyntheX's realism and strong randomisation reproduced on our task and our held-out device, with parity stated in advance (section 5); it is workstream M, issue #69, and it waits for the owner's word.

Two of these are not tests of SyntheX-grade realism and should not be read as such. The plain-film DRRs were parallel-beam attenuation integrals that leave the spectrum, scatter and detector noise to DeepDRR, which was never plugged in (the pipeline's own PIPELINE.md and dataset_card.md); and the sweep linker's physics arm is scored on renders, not real frames. The living-CT renders on DeepFluoro were calibrated against real frames later found to be log-compressed display images, so their device terms were fitted to a bent target, and the matched comparison promised after the scatter fix of 15 to 16 September is reported nowhere (chapter 13). So the record's strongest negatives are the C-ArmLSpine detection rows and the refiner arms; the plain-film row says only that idealised DRRs hurt (Inferred).

## 3. Their side: what the group published

### 3.1 SyntheX (Gao, Killeen et al., Nature Machine Intelligence 5:294-308, 2023; PMC10959504)

- Tasks and anatomy: hip imaging (six structures, both hemipelves, the lumbar vertebrae as one structure, the upper sacrum and both femurs, and 14 landmarks); detection of a continuum manipulator, a robotic tool; COVID-19 lesion segmentation on chest X-rays (Published).
- Real test: for the hip, 366 X-rays of six cadaveric lower torsos on one Siemens Cios Fusion, tested leave one specimen out, plus 60 X-rays of one more cadaver on a Brainlab Loop-X; for the tool, 264 cadaver X-rays, specimen count and device not stated; for COVID-19, 2,951 chest X-rays of QaTa-COV19 (Published).
- What is trained on: in the controlled comparison the synthetic images "precisely recreate the spatial configurations and anatomy of the real X-ray images", rendered from the six specimens' own CTs, so they differ from the real set "in only the realism of the simulation"; the scaled arm adds renders of 20 CTs from the New Mexico Decedent Image Database (Published).
- Realism: naive (mono-energetic, one material, no noise or scatter), heuristic (thresholded materials) and realistic (DeepDRR: the spectrum, learned material decomposition and scatter, signal-dependent and readout noise, detector saturation). Randomisation: regular (Gaussian noise, gamma, a 90 percent crop) or strong (inversion, impulse noise, affine, contrast, blur, box corruption, dropout, sharpen and emboss, pooling, multiply, distortion, up to two at once) (Published).
- The network and what it is asked for: a TransUNet giving segmentation masks and landmark heatmaps; a landmark counts only when the normalised cross-correlation of its heatmap with a Gaussian exceeds a threshold, 0.9 in the tables, the error is "the average l2 distance error over all activated landmarks", and the share activated is shown only as curves in Fig. 4, with no table of it (Published). The rule scores the real-trained control too, Table 1 giving every row at the 0.9 threshold, and Fig. 4 sets error against the share activated for each arm on the same real test set; the Discussion says realistic simulation with strong randomisation "outperformed Real2Real training" at the 90 percent activation level (Published). So the threshold does not favour the renders over the control; what it forgives, in both arms, is a landmark left out.
- The numbers, Table 1 at 360 px, strong randomisation: real-trained 6.46 +/- 8.21 mm, Dice 0.79; realistic renders 6.44 +/- 7.05 mm, Dice 0.80; with CycleGAN 6.62, with ADDA 6.41; naive renders 10.50 mm, Dice 0.73; realistic and scaled to 10,000 renders 5.95 +/- 3.52 mm, Dice 0.86. The scaled arm's Dice gain over real is significant (P = 2.3 x 10^-5, one-tailed) and its landmark gain is not (P = 0.14). On the Loop-X cadaver, 6.16 +/- 5.15 mm and Dice 0.84 (Published).
- What "parity" means there: the abstract says "comparably", the findings "on a par", for a mean error over confident landmarks and a Dice over large structures, against a real-trained model that saw five specimens' frames, a few hundred images per fold (the per-fold count is not stated; about 305 is Inferred from 366 over six). For the tool, Sim2Real Dice 0.92 against Real2Real 0.41 on 264 frames; for COVID-19, where real data is abundant, 85.03 against 93.95 percent overall accuracy, and the authors write that Sim2Real "hardly matches Real2Real performance for use cases where abundant real data exist" (Published).
- The authors' own limits: datasets small, "may not accurately reflect all of the variability one may expect during image-based surgery"; for the anterior superior iliac spine and the sacrum, appearance changes most with projection geometry (Published).

### 3.2 DeepDRR (Unberath et al., MICCAI 2018; IJCARS 14(9):1517-1528, 2019; PMC7297499)

- Task: 23 pelvic landmarks, and segmentation and keypoints of a robotic end effector (Published).
- Real test: 78 X-rays of two cadaveric lower torsos on a Siemens Cios Fusion (Published).
- Numbers on real frames: naive DRR 78.2 +/- 26.5 mm; polychromatic multi-material with noise 34.4; with learned material decomposition 26.9; full DeepDRR with scatter 24.7 +/- 21.3; on synthetic test images every arm sits at 6.1 to 6.7 mm. The polychromatic step is significant (p < 0.01) and scatter's is not (Published).
- What it shows: physics moves real-frame error by a factor of three, yet the best arm is still about four times its synthetic error, and the authors say landmarks are usable only to initialise registration when filtered by confidence (Published). This is the group's own early negative: physics alone did not reach parity; strong randomisation and the matched population did, five years later.

### 3.3 Rapuri, Ko, Killeen, Taylor and Unberath, arXiv 2606.21414 (2026)

- Pelvic landmarks on DeepFluoro's 366 frames, six-fold, with SyntheX's activation rule. At NCC above 0.6: real 4.89 mm, DRRs of the real CTs 7.83 +/- 25.67, 2D diffusion 4.40. At NCC above 0.2, the high-coverage end: real 18.94, DRRs 17.92, 2D diffusion 25.40, DRRs with 500 views at +/-2.5 degrees 9.73 (Table 1; Published).
- What it shows for our hypothesis: as the threshold falls and coverage rises every arm's error grows by a factor of two to six and the order of the arms changes, but not against DRRs: DRRs of the real CTs trail real training at the confident end (7.83 against 4.89 mm) and match it at the high-coverage end (17.92 against 18.94), the authors finding "no clear advantage" between the two, and the pose-dense DRRs (RealCT-DRR-Cloud, 4.87, 6.02 and 9.73 mm) beat real training at the two lower thresholds; it is the diffusion arms whose rank falls (Published). So this line does not show DRR parity confined to the confident end; it shows that at the coverage counting needs every arm, real-trained included, errs by about 10 to 25 mm (Inferred).

### 3.4 Pelphix (Killeen et al., MICCAI 2023)

- Surgical phase recognition in percutaneous pelvic fixation, trained on simulated sequences: 99.2 percent on simulation, 71.7 percent on a cadaver study, up to 84 percent for the target corridor; the authors' rebuttal acknowledges "a notable sim-to-real gap" (Published, the MICCAI paper page with its reviews). The group's own recognition task, unlike its landmark task, did not reach parity.

### 3.5 FluoroSAM and the digital twin (Killeen et al., MICCAI 2025, arXiv 2403.08059; arXiv 2412.08020)

- FluoroSAM is trained from scratch on about three million renders with masks for 128 organ classes and 464 tools (Published, the arcadelab/fluorosam README and the arXiv abstract). Its real tests and its randomisation, intensity-space only with no detector or vendor model, were read by an earlier thread and are in docs/research/benchmarks-to-beat.md section 3: over 1,741 masks of one Loop-X cadaver, Dice 0.60, 0.69 and 0.77 against 0.59, 0.70 and 0.79 on synthetic validation (reached through that note, not re-read here).
- The digital twin, on a cadaveric torso from mid-femur to T2 on a Brainlab Loop-X, 80 images: "L3 vertebra bone" Dice 0.21, L4 0.16, L5 0.25, centroid errors 49 to 54 mm, against 0.68 for "lower lumbar vertebrae" as one group; and "a single image may not provide enough visual context to distinguish between similar vertebrae levels", with Dice 0.04 attached to no named prompt, and the authors point to point prompts as the remedy (Table 1 and Section 4.1; Published).
- What it shows: the group's render-trained model segments the lumbar spine as a group at near-synthetic quality on a real cadaver and fails at the level, which is our task (Inferred).

### 3.6 Spine work near the group, and independent work

- Grimm, Esteban, Unberath and Navab (arXiv 2011.07294, IEEE TMI 2021): X-ray to CT registration with domain randomisation, 4.2 +/- 3.9 mm and 86.8 percent success on real X-rays against 4.1 mm and 92 percent on simulated (Published, the abstract). A registration task with the patient's CT in hand, not naming.
- Inigo, Killeen et al. (arXiv 2512.05803, 2025): vertebroplasty path planning from biplane X-rays by differentiable rendering; reconstruction Dice 0.75, cadaver planning success 75 percent (Published, the abstract). Reconstruction of one known vertebra, not naming.
- Jaganathan et al. 2023 (thoracolumbar 2D/3D registration), Jecklin et al. 2024 (lumbar reconstruction from fluoroscopy of ex-vivo specimens) and Huang et al. 2022 (render-trained vertebra labelling at 88.3 percent on Long-Film images of 13 cadavers) are tabled with their numbers in docs/research/lumbosacral-to-cervical-extrapolation.md section 2 and were not re-read here. Each is a cadaver or ex-vivo test. Jaganathan's translated renders keep about three times the millimetre error of training on real pairs, while Jecklin's translated renders equal fine-tuning on real frames (that note's table). Huang 2022 is from Johns Hopkins too (Uneri, Siewerdsen), and it trains on renders of 297 CTs and labels levels on real intraoperative images, though of an extended-field tomographic mode, not fluoroscopy.
- Dong et al. (AnyCXR, arXiv 2512.17263, 2025), independent of the group: a DRR-only chest model segments the vertebrae as a group at Dice 0.943 on 20 real PA radiographs but 0.795 on 20 laterals, with real per-vertebra figures not reported (Published, the HTML). It agrees with the pattern: grouped bone transfers, the level is not measured.
- Sukesh et al. (BVM 2022, Siemens and Erlangen): adding synthetic spine X-rays from CT improved vertebra detection rates on real X-rays (Published, the abstract only; the numbers were not reached). Synthetic data as augmentation to real, not in place of it.
- No independent replication or critique of SyntheX's hip parity was found in this read.

The owner's three questions, answered:

- Is their task easier? Yes. Their landmarks are 14 points on a pelvis whose structures differ from each other in shape; their segmentation scores large structures, with the lumbar spine one class; and a landmark the network is unsure of is left out of the error. Ours must find each of a run of near-identical bodies and keep its identity, and a missed or doubled body moves every level after it. Where the group asked its renders for a level (the twin), they failed as ours did (Inferred from the Published task definitions).
- Is their test closer to their training? Yes. SyntheX's controlled test renders the test population's own CTs at the real frames' poses, in one lab on one device, cadavers in both; the scaled arm's CTs are decedents. Our test is clinical frames of living patients on a device whose processing (window, edge enhancement, automatic exposure, collimation) no training image came from, rendered from living CTs. When we matched population as they did, we matched their result (Reported, Inferred).
- Is their metric more forgiving? Yes, though not in favour of the renders. The landmark error is averaged over activated landmarks only (NCC above 0.9), with the share activated not tabulated, so a missed landmark costs nothing, where in counting it moves every level after it; but the control is scored the same way, Fig. 4 puts the realistic arm ahead at a 90 percent activation level too, and in Rapuri 2026 DRRs of the real CTs match real training at the high-coverage end. The threshold explains why their absolute errors are small, not why the arms tie. And the control is a real-trained model on a few hundred frames, so parity is with a small real set; where real data is abundant, as for COVID-19, the authors themselves say synthetic training "hardly matches". Our comparison is with real frames of other devices numbering in the thousands, at counting-grade recall (Published, Inferred).
- Does anything contradict ours on the same task? Not on fluoroscopy. No paper of the lab trains on renders and names or counts vertebral levels on real fluoroscopy; the nearest, the twin, agrees with our detection result. Huang 2022, from another Johns Hopkins group and not re-read here, labels levels at 88.3 percent with a render-trained model on real intraoperative cadaver images; it is the nearest thing to a counterexample, on an extended-field mode rather than fluoroscopy, and it agrees with our lateral naming more than with our detection (Inferred).

What would have to be true for both to hold, and the record is consistent with it, though no single run varies one condition at a time: synthetic parity holds when the training CTs come from the test population, the target is a large distinctive structure or a confident subset of landmarks, the real baseline is small, and the score is taken at high confidence; it fails when the population, the device's processing or the clinical field change, when every body must be found, and when the real baseline is large. SyntheX sits at the first end of every axis and SchwingNet's detection at the second. One axis is still open: SyntheX's strong randomisation over DeepDRR-level physics has never been run on our task. Our renderer reached only part of that recipe: the randomisation arms of 21 September were never scored on held-out real data (`dr_eval` is reported nowhere), and the device-card sampler reached 1.7 to 5.1 percent of each device's joint distribution (chapter 13). So "physics noise did not work" is established for the renders we made, not for the recipe they published (Inferred).

## 5. The one experiment that settles it for SchwingNet

Reproduce SyntheX's realism and randomisation on our task and our held-out device: DeepDRR-level physics (spectrum, material decomposition, scatter, quantum and readout noise, detector saturation) and SyntheX's strong randomisation list, rendered from living CTs whose persons are not C-ArmLSpine persons, at the scale SyntheX found useful (10,000 renders), training the detector and, separately, the naming encoder; three arms, physics with strong randomisation, the current styled renders and plain DRRs, each with three seeds; tested on C-ArmLSpine's held-out persons, device never seen.

What counts as parity, stated before the run: a renders-only arm reaches parity if its seed-averaged body recall at the working threshold (0.74, and at 0.25 beside it), under the stated truth convention, has a lower 95 percent Wilson bound no more than five points below the point estimate of the arm trained on other devices' real frames (80.5 percent at 0.74 today), with the recall curve over all thresholds reported, not one threshold; and, for naming, if per-level accuracy on AP and lateral frames is within the same margin of the real-frame arm. A renders-only arm below that, but above the current renders-only figure by more than the seed spread, means the recipe helps and does not reach parity; an arm inside the seed spread of the current renders means the recipe changes nothing on our task. The five-point margin is a proposal for the owner to fix before the run (Inferred).

Cost: the CT-priors note prices a smaller version, three arms with one short fine-tune each, at 3 to 5 days of work and 10 to 20 GPU-hours; the version above, three seeds per arm and the encoder too, costs more and is not priced here; DeepDRR is a new dependency, so an issue first (docs/research/ct-priors-for-2d-detection.md section 3, item 5; workstream M, issue #69, docs/research/xr-from-3d/README.md). A free step comes first: look on the grid for the outputs of `dr_eval` and the device-card A/B, which were queued and never reported. Nothing here runs without the owner's word and the number of jobs.
