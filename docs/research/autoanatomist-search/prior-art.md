## CT pre-processing

1. **nnU-Net: a self-configuring method for deep learning-based biomedical image segmentation (preprint: Automated design of deep learning methods for biomedical image segmentation)** (Isensee 2021; Nature Methods 18:203-211 (2021; online Dec 2020), doi 10.1038/s41592-020-01008-z; preprint arXiv:1904.08128; CT rule also in the nnU-Net GitHub documentation (documentation/explanation_normalization.md)). https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.08128
   What it does: The data fingerprint records image sizes before and after cropping to the nonzero region, spacings, modality, class and case counts, and foreground intensity statistics (mean, SD, 0.5 and 99.5 percentiles). Fixed rules follow: resample to the per-axis median training spacing with third-order spline for images and linear interpolation of one-hot labels, and when the largest/smallest spacing ratio exceeds 3 the low-resolution axis takes the 10th-percentile spacing and is interpolated nearest-neighbour out of plane; for CT, clip to the 0.5/99.5 percentiles of foreground voxels pooled over the training set and z-score with the global foreground mean and SD, whereas other modalities are z-scored per image (inside a mask when cropping shrank the image by 25% or more). Training augmentation is rotations, scaling, Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur, brightness, contrast, simulated low resolution, gamma and mirroring; the GitHub page says deviating from the CT default should be benchmarked.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The reference self-configuring pre-processing block for the Metaflow sweep: global percentile clip plus z-score is the CT default to put beside fixed Hounsfield windows, and its noise/blur/low-resolution augmentations are the same degradation family as our privileged-channel ladder, applied to the image rather than to the mask.
2. **Coarse to Fine Vertebrae Localization and Segmentation with SpatialConfiguration-Net and U-Net** (Payer 2020; VISAPP 2020 (VISIGRAPP), SciTePress; first place in VerSe 2019). https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2020/89752/89752.pdf
   What it does: Three stages at three spacings: a U-Net localises the spine at 8 mm isotropic (input 64x64x128 voxels), SpatialConfiguration-Net localises vertebrae at 2 mm (96x96x128 centred on the predicted spine, split into sub-volumes overlapping by 96 voxels at inference), and a U-Net segments each vertebra at 1 mm in a 128x128x96 mm crop centred on its centroid with a Gaussian centroid heatmap as a second channel. Every stage reorients the volume to a common orientation, smooths it with a Gaussian of sigma 0.75 mm to reduce noise, and normalises intensity by dividing by 2048 and clamping to [-1, 1] (a fixed linear window with no HU shift); training multiplies intensities by a factor in [0.75, 1.25] and shifts by [-0.25, 0.25]. Combined VerSe'19 score 0.691; the benchmark reports 94.25% id rate, 4.80 mm and 89.80% Dice on the hidden set.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The canonical vertebra-centred crop recipe (1 mm, 128 mm cube, centroid heatmap channel) with fixed-window-plus-smoothing pre-processing that won VerSe'19; a strong baseline arm for the sweep against percentile clipping and against no pre-smoothing.
3. **VerSe: A Vertebrae labelling and segmentation benchmark for multi-detector CT images** (Sekuboyina 2021; Medical Image Analysis 73:102166 (2021); arXiv:2001.09193). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.09193
   What it does: Reports the MICCAI 2019 and 2020 challenges (374 scans, 25 algorithms). VerSe'19 was won by Payer C. and VerSe'20 by Chen D. (hidden set: 96.58% id rate, 1.38 mm, 91.23% Dice), whose pipeline downsamples the CT to a coarse resolution for a shallow 3D U-Net spine localiser, then segments iteratively at 1 mm on small patches with the previous vertebra's mask as an input channel, then classifies each vertebra with a 3D ResNet-50 from mask plus CT under an anatomical reasoning constraint; Lessmann's entry moves a 128^3 region of interest along the spine, and the organisers' Anduin tool uses a low-resolution spine heatmap, Btrfly-Net labelling on sagittal and coronal maximum intensity projections, and U-Net segmentation on 1 mm patches around centroids. The paper states no organiser-side resampling or intensity normalisation for evaluation. Its failure analysis notes that almost every algorithm failed the single L6 in the 2019 hidden set, that methods counting from the last vertebra are thrown off for the whole scan by an L6, and that only one entry (Xiangshang Z.) identified every T13.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Top entries pre-process by coarse-to-fine resampling and vertebra-centred 1 mm patches rather than by any shared intensity recipe, and the L6/T13 failures are direct evidence that level naming has to be solved at column level, as our differentiable chain over level types does, not by counting from an anchor.
4. **Iterative fully convolutional neural networks for automatic vertebra segmentation and identification** (Lessmann 2019; Medical Image Analysis 53:142-155 (2019); arXiv:1804.04383). https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1804.04383
   What it does: Resamples every CT to 1 mm isotropic and applies no windowing, clipping or normalisation to CT intensities (percentile clipping to [-1, 1] is used only for MR). A 128^3 patch slides over the image until it holds a vertebral fragment of at least 1000 voxels, recentres on the fragment's bounding box until the position converges (at most 10 iterations), and a binary instance memory of already segmented vertebrae enters as a second input channel so the network segments the next unvisited vertebra; masks return to native resolution by nearest neighbour and a post-processing step strips surface voxels below 200 HU. Training augmentation includes random Gaussian smoothing, Gaussian noise, elastic deformation and z-axis cropping.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Shows raw-HU input at 1 mm (no window, no z-score) suffices for vertebra segmentation when the crop follows the vertebra, and its 200 HU surface threshold is exactly the hard Hounsfield gate our ladder makes soft; the memory channel is an alternative to centroid-centred crops.
5. **Vertebrae localization, segmentation and identification using a graph optimization and an anatomic consistency cycle** (Meng 2022; Machine Learning in Medical Imaging (MLMI 2022, MICCAI workshop), LNCS 13583, Springer, doi 10.1007/978-3-031-21014-3_32; extended version arXiv:2110.12177 v3). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.12177
   What it does: Resamples to 1 mm isotropic and reorients to a common anatomical orientation (results are mapped back), with no intensity normalisation stated. Spine segmentation runs an attention U-Net on 96^3 patches with stride 24; each vertebra is segmented from a 128^3 crop centred on its detected location plus a Gaussian location channel (sigma 20) and classified from a 128^3 mask with neighbours included. The local classifier calls T13 'T12' and L6 'L5'; a global graph of n x 24 nodes solved by Dijkstra, with higher-cost edges for T12->T12 (T13), T11->L1 (absent T12) and L5->L5 (L6), and an anatomic consistency cycle over learned volume and spacing statistics, resolve the variants, giving 96.59% id rate and 92.53% Dice on the VerSe'20 public test.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The closest published analogue to our soft-Viterbi over level types with explicit L6, T13 and absent-T12 transitions, and it fixes the pre-processing such a labeller expects: 1 mm, reoriented, vertebra-centred 128^3 crop with a Gaussian location channel.
6. **A computed tomography vertebral segmentation dataset with anatomical variations and multi-vendor scanner data** (Liebl 2021; Scientific Data 8 (2021), doi 10.1038/s41597-021-01060-0 (per the search listing); text read from arXiv:2103.06360). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.06360
   What it does: Documents the VerSe'20 release: new series kept at native resolution (inclusion required at most 1.5 mm craniocaudal, 1 mm anterior-posterior, 3 mm left-right), the 86 reused VerSe'19 series already reduced to 1 mm isotropic (or 1 mm in-plane for 2-3 mm sagittal series), all with edge-enhancing bone reconstructions and some with contrast; masks were made at 1 mm^3 by the Anduin pipeline (low-resolution spine CNN, Btrfly-Net, U-Net) and corrected in the original image space. Variant counts: L6 in 85 of 252 lumbosacral scans (33.7%), T13 in 6, absent T12 in 8, four lumbar vertebrae in 3, and Castellvi 2a/2b/3a/3b/4 in 35/25/14/28/7 scans (grades III-IV not segmented).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Fixes what registry CT from VerSe looks like before any pre-processing block runs (a mix of native and 1 mm-resampled series, sharp bone kernels) and gives the prior frequencies of L6, T13 and sacralization that the level chain's transition costs should reflect.
7. **Automatic Vertebra Localization and Identification in CT by Spine Rectification and Anatomically-constrained Optimization** (Wang 2021; CVPR 2021 (IEEE/CVF)). https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2021/papers/Wang_Automatic_Vertebra_Localization_and_Identification_in_CT_by_Spine_Rectification_CVPR_2021_paper.pdf
   What it does: Resamples CT to 0.3 x 0.3 x 1.25 mm and trains an nnU-Net keypoint model on 128x160x64 patches to emit 26 Gaussian activation maps; the per-slice centres of mass of the summed map give a centerline, a moving frame is built along it (tangent as e3, e2 the normal-plane axis nearest the image y-axis), and the maps are warped into this rectified frame and collapsed to one 1-D signal per vertebra. Labels come from an energy with an activation term and an exp(max(a/b, b/a)) spacing-regularity term under a consecutive-index constraint, searched by offset, hill climbing and expansion, then mapped back through the inverse warp. On SpineWeb CSI 2014 (60 test CTs): 2.55 mm mean error, 97.4% id rate; no intensity normalisation is stated.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: A deep-learning version of a spine-centred (rectified) frame, built from the network's own activations rather than from the raw image, feeding a 1-D anatomically constrained labelling that our differentiable chain generalises; note it is evaluated on SpineWeb, not VerSe.
8. **Joint Vertebrae Identification and Localization in Spinal CT Images by Combining Short- and Long-Range Contextual Information** (Liao 2018; IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging 37(5):1266-1275 (2018); arXiv:1812.03500). https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.03500
   What it does: Resamples CT to 1.25 mm longitudinal x 1.0 x 1.0 mm, states no windowing or HU clipping, and trains a 3-D CNN on random 32x112x96-voxel crops labelled by the centroid nearest the crop centre (about 40 samples per vertebra), then converts it to a fully convolutional network with an effective stride of 16 voxels. Features of positive windows are ordered along the spine into sequences (mean length 266) for a three-layer bidirectional LSTM that outputs identity and location per step; on SpineWeb (242/60 split) it reports 88.3% id rate and 6.47 mm mean error.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: An early vertebra-localisation recipe of near-isotropic resampling plus random vertebra-centred crops feeding a sequence model over the column; crop size and dense-scan stride are the kind of block parameters the sweep should expose.
9. **View it like a radiologist: shifted windows for deep learning augmentation of CT images (extended 2025 as Random Window Augmentations for Deep Learning Robustness in CT and Liver Tumor Segmentation)** (Ostmo 2023; IEEE MLSP 2023 (as cited by the 2025 extension); arXiv:2311.14990; extension arXiv:2510.08116 (2025)). https://arxiv.org/html/2311.14990
   What it does: Uses a base window spanning the 0.5-99.5 percentile of foreground HU, defers clipping to augmentation and randomly shifts the window level (p = 0.3) within a range set by per-volume median HU, so no empty or displaced values arise as they do when intensity augmentation is applied after clipping; inference uses only the base window. For liver tumour segmentation (LiTS plus external sets) it beat the nnU-Net intensity-augmentation set (Dice 0.565 vs 0.548) and a geometric-only baseline (0.447). The 2025 extension adds window-width scaling, shows a raw-HU window (width 2000, level 0) scoring 0.552 against 0.634 for a narrow tumour window, and finds some standard intensity augmentations score below no augmentation.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The order of operations is the finding: randomise on raw HU, then clip; and narrow task windows beat raw HU for soft tissue, a claim to re-test for bone where the relevant band (cortex, cancellous bone, canal contents) is wide.
10. **Stochastic tissue window normalization of deep learning on computed tomography** (Huo 2019; Journal of Medical Imaging 6(4):044005 (2019)). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6863984
   What it does: Compares a fixed soft-tissue window (level 40, half-width 200 HU), the whole range (-1000 to 1000 HU) and a stochastic window whose level and width are drawn per training slice from Gaussians centred on the fixed window, for 2D U-Net segmentation of six abdominal organs across six datasets. On same-scanner data the fixed and unwindowed inputs matched or beat the stochastic one; on cohorts with different contrast or pathology the stochastic window gave the clearest gains (non-contrast liver median Dice 0.90 vs 0.83 fixed and 0.88 unwindowed), with results depending on the sampling widths.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Evidence that fixed window, full range and randomised window trade in-distribution accuracy against generalisation rather than one being best, which argues for making the window a swept block and randomising it in training as the ladder's soft HU gate already permits.
11. **Practical Window Setting Optimization for Medical Image Deep Learning** (Lee 2018; Machine Learning for Health (ML4H) workshop, NeurIPS 2018; arXiv:1812.00572). https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.00572
   What it does: Replaces the fixed HU window with a trainable 1x1 convolution plus bounded activation (an upper-bounded ReLU, or a sigmoid scaled to U = 255) whose weight and bias are initialised from a chosen window level and width and trained jointly with an Inception-v3 classifier. On head CT haemorrhage and abdominal CT urinary-stone detection, full-range HU input scored average precision 0.807/0.813, the best fixed windows 0.934/0.946 and the learned sigmoid windows 0.950/0.971.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Prior art for a differentiable, learnable Hounsfield window, the same device as our soft HU gates; it also quantifies how much a fixed window buys over raw HU for a 2D classifier, a gap our sweep should measure for 3D landmark regression.
12. **Domain-specific cues improve robustness of deep learning-based segmentation of CT volumes** (Kloenne 2020; Scientific Reports 10:10712 (2020)). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7329868
   What it does: Clips each volume to its 0.6-0.99 percentile range (a radiologist-like window), z-scores with statistics from a random data subset, and augments by widening, narrowing and shifting that window plus Gaussian noise, rotation up to 16 degrees and slice skipping/interpolation, for kidney (KiTS19) and liver (CHAOS) segmentation with nnU-Net and MS-D Net in 2D and 3D. Mean Dice matched a general-purpose augmentation pipeline, but the standard deviation across folds fell by about an order of magnitude (e.g., 0.931 +/- 0.002 vs 0.929 +/- 0.009) and windowing shortened training; windowing was not isolated as a single variable in the main results.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: A per-volume percentile window with window-jitter augmentation is a third normalisation arm beside fixed HU and nnU-Net's pooled percentiles, and its robustness metric, variance across folds, is a cheap criterion to add to the sweep's ledger.
13. **TotalSegmentator: robust segmentation of 104 anatomical structures in CT images** (Wasserthal 2023; Radiology: Artificial Intelligence (2023); accepted manuscript read on arXiv:2208.05868). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.05868
   What it does: Trains nnU-Net models on CT resampled to 1.5 mm isotropic (five models of about 21 classes each) and one 3 mm model with all 104 classes; mirroring is removed from the augmentation so left and right structures stay distinct, and training is lengthened to 4000/8000 epochs. Vertebrae C1-L5 are among the classes, initialised during annotation from a VerSe model; the 3 mm model drops Dice from 0.943 to 0.840 (NSD 0.966), and misidentifying vertebrae and ribs by counting on partial spines is a stated failure mode.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: A concrete spacing-versus-accuracy data point (1.5 vs 3 mm) for the resampling block, a warning that mirroring is not a free augmentation for a column model, and a counting failure on partial fields of view that the differentiable chain is meant to avoid.
14. **Scale factor point spread function matching: beyond aliasing in image resampling** (Cardoso 2015; MICCAI 2015, LNCS, Springer, pp. 675-683, doi 10.1007/978-3-319-24571-3_81). https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1469251/
   What it does: Argues that resampling medical images as continuous functions onto a coarser grid ignores the sampling theorem and produces aliasing that can bias measurements, and proposes matching the point spread function with Gaussian kernels scaled to the resampling factor under arbitrary transformations, noting that multi-resolution pyramids already low-pass before downsampling. Reports statistically significant technical and clinical effects of the correction.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Justifies a smoothing-before-downsampling step (what Payer's sigma 0.75 mm Gaussian does in practice) whenever the sweep resamples sub-millimetre CT to 1-2 mm, so end-plate widths and canal depths read off resampled frames are not biased by aliasing.
15. **Dose robustness of deep learning models for anatomic segmentation of computed tomography images** (Tsanda 2024; Journal of Medical Imaging 11(4):044005 (2024), doi 10.1117/1.JMI.11.4.044005; conference version SPIE Medical Imaging 2024 ('On TotalSegmentator's performance on low-dose CT images', doi 10.1117/12.3000186)). https://tore.tuhh.de/entities/publication/d2e134bf-80f1-4340-8267-fe8f33607c27
   What it does: Simulates low-dose CT from full-dose raw projections (validated on a phantom) down to 20% dose and measures how several pretrained segmentation models deviate from their full-dose output, with and without existing denoisers applied before the network. TotalSegmentator stayed robust (the conference version reports Dice down at most about 3% at 20% dose); less robust models improved with denoising, CNN denoising keeping median Dice at or above 0.9 for all but one model, with volatile results for structures of effective radius under 19 mm.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The only direct test found of denoising as a pre-network step for CT segmentation: it buys little for an nnU-Net-style model trained with noise and blur augmentation and more for others, so a denoise block should be swept against noise augmentation rather than assumed.

## Mask degradation and privileged information

1. **Make a Strong Teacher with Label Assistance: A Novel Knowledge Distillation Approach for Semantic Segmentation** (Qiu 2024; ECCV 2024). https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2024/papers_ECCV/html/11288_ECCV_2024_paper.php
   What it does: The teacher receives the ground-truth label map as an extra input channel concatenated with the RGB image, after a Label Noising Module corrupts it (one-hot channels multiplied by Gaussian random weights and summed, plus per-pixel Gaussian noise scaled by alpha = 0.01); two teacher paths with independently noised labels are trained with a consistency loss, and an image-only student is distilled from one path with a channel-wise logit distillation loss. With a clean label the teacher shortcuts to copying the label and ignores the image; too little noise reproduces the shortcut and too much degrades the teacher. Students gain 3.5 to 6.3 mIoU on Cityscapes with gains on ADE20K, VOC and COCO-Stuff (full text: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.13254).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The closest published analogue of a privileged encoder that sees the mask: it shows the mask channel must be corrupted or the teacher learns to read the answer off it, which is what our degradation ladder (identity fade, blur, soft Hounsfield gate) guards against, and it gives a measured way to choose the corruption level; it keeps the teacher at full mask strength and hands over by distillation rather than annealing the mask weight to zero inside one network, so it is the design our ladder should be compared against in a sweep.
2. **Unifying distillation and privileged information** (Lopez-Paz 2016; ICLR 2016). https://bottou.org/papers/lopez-paz-2016
   What it does: Generalized distillation: a teacher is fit on the privileged representation x*, its softmax at temperature T gives soft labels, and the student on the regular input x minimizes (1 - lambda) times the hard-label loss plus lambda times the soft-label loss. The paper argues the student gains when the teacher's capacity is small, its approximation error is below the student's and the imitation-rate exponent exceeds 1/2, with the largest gain in low-data regimes, and it extends the recipe to semi-supervised and multitask settings (full text: https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICLR/2016/unifying_distillation_and_privileged_information__70a05343.txt).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Our oracle-with-mask to image-only handover is a generalized-distillation problem with the mask as x*, so lambda and T belong among the sweepable blocks; the later analysis of privileged features distillation (Yang et al., arXiv 2209.08754, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.08754) finds a non-monotone effect, an over-predictive privileged input makes the teacher high-variance and the student worse, and a teacher fed only privileged features underperforms one fed both, which argues for degrading the mask rather than giving the privileged encoder a clean one and for feeding it the image too.
3. **Learning Using Privileged Information: Similarity Control and Knowledge Transfer** (Vapnik 2015; Journal of Machine Learning Research 16(61):2023-2049). https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v16/vapnik15b.html
   What it does: Vapnik and Izmailov's statement of the LUPI paradigm: an Intelligent Teacher supplies, at training time only, privileged information x* beside each label, which can speed up the student in two ways, by correcting the student's similarity measure (SVM+, where the slack variables are modelled by a correcting function in the privileged space) and by transferring knowledge from the privileged space to the decision space. The paradigm originates in Vapnik and Vashist, 'A new learning paradigm: learning using privileged information', Neural Networks 2009, pp. 544-557 (seen as the MIT lecture slides adapted from it, https://web.mit.edu/zoya/www/SVM+.pdf).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Names the paradigm our privileged encoder belongs to and the two mechanisms to choose between. Its deep instantiations: Lambert et al., CVPR 2018, make the dropout variance a function of the privileged input (https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2018/html/Lambert_Deep_Learning_Under_CVPR_2018_paper.html); PI-Dropout in RL (Kamienny et al., arXiv 2005.09220, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.09220) feeds x* to a branch that sets log-normal noise on the regular branch, and its naive baseline that anneals a noise level from 0 to 1 over 3,000 episodes performed poorly, a caution for any fixed annealing of our mask weight; Learning by Cheating, CoRL 2019 (https://mlanthology.org/corl/2019/chen2019corl-learning) and Scaffolder, ICLR 2024 (https://iclr.cc/virtual/2024/poster/19090) keep privileged inputs in a teacher or in training-time components (critic, world model) and never in the deployed network.
4. **Input Dropout for Spatially Aligned Modalities** (de Blois 2020; IEEE ICIP 2020). https://rc.signalprocessingsociety.org/conferences/icip-2020/icip20vid368
   What it does: An extra spatially aligned modality (depth, thermal or a segmentation map) is concatenated as an input channel during training and set to zero with probability P_drop (0.5 when only the extra modality is dropped; a uniform three-way choice, 0.33, when either modality may be dropped); at test time the extra channel is always zero so the network runs on RGB alone. Gains are reported on dehazing (including RGB plus segmentation), NYU V2 classification, 6-DOF tracking and pedestrian detection (method details: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.02852).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The simplest form of mixing a segmentation channel into the input and removing it at test time, with one network and no distillation; its constant stochastic drop is the baseline our annealed, degraded mask channel should beat, and it suggests a Metaflow block that sweeps a constant P_drop against our schedule.
5. **Learning with Privileged Multimodal Knowledge for Unimodal Segmentation** (Chen 2022; IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging 41(3):621-632). https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/349616
   What it does: A multimodal teacher that sees all modalities during training transfers to a unimodal student used at inference through a pixel-level regularized distillation loss, in which a regularization factor down-weights incorrect teacher predictions, and an image-level contrastive distillation loss; evaluated on cardiac substructure and brain tumour segmentation. KD-Net (Hu et al., MICCAI 2020, LNCS 12261, pp. 772-781, https://researchportal.ip-paris.fr/en/publications/knowledge-distillation-from-multi-modal-to-mono-modal-segmentatio/) is the earlier form: a frozen four-modality teacher, a T1ce-only student and the loss 0.75 L_KD (T = 5) + 0.25 L_GT + 10 L_KL on the bottleneck features, raising BraTS 2018 enhancing-tumour Dice from 68.1 to 71.7 (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.09564).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The medical-imaging precedent for a teacher that sees what the student will not, with the loss shapes (soft-output distillation, bottleneck feature matching, down-weighting of wrong teacher outputs) that our privileged encoder could emit as auxiliary targets while its weight anneals. Related mask-as-privileged-information work: enhanced mammogram patches as x* with prediction-level distillation (Tzortzis et al., arXiv 2402.06379, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.06379), lesion masks used only in training for mask-free skin lesion classification via relational distillation (Rahi et al., arXiv 2607.18773, https://chatpaper.com/paper/312459), and masks as a VisualBackProp attention-loss target (Bisla and Choromanska, arXiv 1805.09474, https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1805.09474).
6. **Anatomically Constrained Neural Networks (ACNN): Application to Cardiac Image Enhancement and Segmentation** (Oktay 2018; IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging 37(2):384-395). https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/handle/10044/1/50440
   What it does: A convolutional autoencoder is trained on ground-truth segmentation masks to learn a low-dimensional shape code; the segmentation network's prediction is passed through that encoder and a squared L2 distance between its code and the ground-truth mask's code is added to the pixel-wise cross-entropy with weight lambda_1, the autoencoder's parameters not being updated during segmentation training (mechanism: https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1705.08302). The learnt 3D shape codes are also used as biomarkers to classify cardiac pathology.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Feature matching to an encoder that sees only masks, in cardiac MR and ultrasound, used purely as a training-time regulariser; for us it is the natural target once the privileged encoder's input weight has annealed to zero: keep its mask-trained code as a fixed shape space and match the image branch's features to it, and its use of the latent code as a measurement (biomarker) parallels reading Panjabi lines off masks.
7. **Learning with Side Information through Modality Hallucination** (Hoffman 2016; CVPR 2016, pp. 826-834). https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2016/html/Hoffman_Learning_With_Side_CVPR_2016_paper.html
   What it does: An RGB object detector is trained with depth as side information available only at training time: a hallucination network taking RGB is trained to mimic the mid-level convolutional features of a depth network, and at test time the RGB and hallucination networks run jointly on the image alone, improving detection on NYUDv2. Follow-ups replace the feature-matching loss with an adversarial discriminator over hallucinated and depth features (Garcia et al., arXiv 1810.08437, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.08437) and apply the idea to heatmap-based 3D human pose from RGB with a depth-pretrained teacher, depth used only in training (Simoni et al., ECCV Workshops 2024, pp. 189-203, https://mlanthology.org/eccvw/2024/simoni2024eccvw-depthbased).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The canonical 'feature matching to a teacher that sees the privileged modality' pattern, including a heatmap/keypoint instance close to our landmark head; it suggests keeping a mask-fed branch frozen as the target and training an image-only hallucination branch to match it, as an alternative to annealing the mask input inside one encoder.
8. **Scheduled Sampling for Sequence Prediction with Recurrent Neural Networks** (Bengio 2015; NIPS 2015). https://research.google/pubs/scheduled-sampling-for-sequence-prediction-with-recurrent-neural-networks/
   What it does: Training feeds the oracle (true previous token) with probability epsilon_i and the model's own prediction otherwise, and epsilon_i is decayed from 1 towards 0 on a linear (max(epsilon, k - c i)), exponential (k^i) or inverse-sigmoid (k / (k + exp(i/k))) schedule, explicitly framed as a curriculum from fully guided training to the inference regime (schedules: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.03099v1). It improved several sequence tasks and the 2015 MSCOCO captioning entry.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The oldest widely used recipe for annealing a privileged input (the oracle) out of a network over training, with named schedule families; our mask-weight annealing is the same handover for a spatial input, so the linear, exponential and inverse-sigmoid schedules are the obvious sweep axis, and its per-token coin flip suggests per-sample stochastic handover rather than one deterministic global weight.
9. **Curriculum By Smoothing** (Sinha 2020; NeurIPS 2020). https://research.nvidia.com/labs/srl/publication/sinha-2020-curriculum
   What it does: A fixed Gaussian kernel is convolved with every convolutional layer's output and its standard deviation is annealed towards zero (sigma 1, multiplied by 0.9 every 5 epochs; sigma 2 for VGG-16), so the network first learns from low-frequency features; it adds no parameters or losses and improved CIFAR, SVHN, ImageNet (ResNet-18 67.9 to 71.0 top-1), transfer and zero-shot domain adaptation, and ablations show that smoothing only the input images hurts and that a constant sigma underperforms the annealed one (details: https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2003.01367). Vogelsang et al., PNAS 115(44):11333-11338, 2018 (https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/129594) found that CNNs trained first on blurred faces and then on sharp ones form receptive fields integrating over larger areas and generalize better across resolutions.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Direct precedent for the blur rung of our ladder as a scheduled curriculum with a decay rule and a measured effect; that input-only smoothing hurts while feature smoothing helps, and that a constant blur is worse than an annealed one, bear on where in the privileged encoder the blur gate should sit and argue for sweeping the sigma schedule rather than fixing a blur.
10. **Differentiable Soft Morphological Filters for Medical Image Segmentation** (Guzzi 2024; MICCAI 2024, LNCS 15008, pp. 177-187). https://papers.miccai.org/miccai-2024/220-Paper0322.html
   What it does: Any binary morphological filter defined on 2D or 3D images (erosion, dilation, skeletonization) is turned into a differentiable soft filter on probability maps by rewriting its Boolean expression as a multilinear polynomial, with proxy polynomials to keep the cost of the binary filter; the filters are used as a loss (with clDice) or as a final morphological layer of a segmentation network, with code at github.com/lisaGUZZI/Soft-morph. The earlier soft skeleton of clDice (Shit et al., CVPR 2021, https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07311) is built from iterated soft erosion and dilation, implemented in MONAI as negated max-pooling with directional 3x1 kernels and 3x3 max-pooling for three iterations (https://docs.monai.io/en/latest/_modules/monai/losses/cldice.html).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Gives differentiable erosion, dilation and skeletonization on soft masks in medical imaging, so a morphology rung (soft dilation or erosion of the mask channel) can be added to our ladder and analysed by gradient, and it offers a differentiable route from a predicted mask to the end-plate and canal lines the measurement oracle reads, should the oracle ever need to pass gradients.
11. **Learning Grayscale Mathematical Morphology with Smooth Morphological Layers** (Hermary 2022; Journal of Mathematical Imaging and Vision, 2022 (doi 10.1007/s10851-022-01091-1)). https://www.lre.epita.fr/dload/papers/hermary.22.jmiv.pdf
   What it does: Two smooth morphological layers replace the non-differentiable min and max: LMorph uses the counter-harmonic (Lehmer) mean with a trainable order p and SMorph the alpha-softmax with a trainable alpha, each approaching dilation as the parameter goes to plus infinity and erosion as it goes to minus infinity, with the structuring-element weights and p or alpha learned together by backpropagation (Adam); PConv reduces to an ordinary convolution at p = 0. Introduced in Kirszenberg et al., 'Going beyond p-convolutions to learn grayscale morphological operators' (https://www.lre.epita.fr/dload/papers/kirszenberg.21.dgmm.pdf).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Exactly the device for a degradation rung whose parameter is learnt or analysed by gradient: one scalar interpolates between erosion, a convolution-like middle and dilation of the mask channel, so the ladder's morphology step can be a learnable layer whose gradient reports how much structure the network still draws from the mask.
12. **Practical Window Setting Optimization for Medical Image Deep Learning** (Lee 2018; ML4H workshop at NeurIPS 2018 (arXiv 1812.00572)). https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.00572
   What it does: The Hounsfield window is made a trainable layer: a linear ramp with clipping or a sigmoid, parameterized by window level and width, implemented as a 1x1 convolution followed by a bounded ReLU or U times a sigmoid, initialized from standard radiology windows and trained by backpropagation with Inception-v3; on head CT haemorrhage the sigmoid window initialized from two standard windows raised average precision from 0.934 (fixed windows) to 0.950, and on urinary stones from 0.946 to 0.971 (acceptance notice: https://lmic.mgh.harvard.edu/practical-window-setting-optimization-for-medical-image-deep-learning-accepted-by-ml4h-workshop-at-neurips-2018). Karki et al., Artificial Intelligence in Medicine 106:101850, 2020 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S093336571930939X) instead train a window estimator that predicts width and level per image through a scaling layer under distant supervision from the lesion label alone, and Kwon and Choi, BigComp 2020, pp. 169-172 (https://pure.seoultech.ac.kr/en/publications/trainable-multi-contrast-windowing-for-liver-ct-segmentation/) report trainable multi-contrast windows for liver CT segmentation.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: A soft Hounsfield gate (sigmoid in HU with learnable centre and slope) trained by gradient is established practice in CT deep learning, which supports making our threshold rung's level and softness learnable and initializing them from bone windows; the differentiable binarization of Liao et al., AAAI 2020 (https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/aaai/article/view/6812), B = 1 / (1 + exp(-k (P - T))) with k = 50 and a learned threshold map used only in training, is the same soft step applied to a probability map and is the form for the ladder's final hard-threshold rung.
13. **Enhancing MR Image Segmentation with Realistic Adversarial Data Augmentation** (Chen 2022; Medical Image Analysis 82:102597). https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/28861/
   What it does: AdvChain composes four differentiable transformations in random order, each applied with probability 0.5: bounded additive noise, a multiplicative bias field from a 4x4 grid of log-space B-spline control points, a five-parameter affine map through a spatial transformer, and a diffeomorphic deformation from a smoothed stationary velocity field integrated by scaling and squaring; their parameters take one projected gradient step that maximizes a consistency loss between predictions on the original and transformed images, then the segmentation network trains on the result (details: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.03429). Xu, Li and Zhu, MICCAI 2020, LNCS 12261 (https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-030-59710-8_37) search a seven-operation policy (scale, three rotations, elastic alpha and sigma, gamma, each with a magnitude interval and probability, 11^11 policies) by stochastic relaxation and natural gradient in under 100 GPU hours, beating nnU-Net's built-in augmentation on three Decathlon tasks; Qin et al., ICASSP 2020 (https://rc.signalprocessingsociety.org/conferences/icassp-2020/spsicassp20vid0917) learn an augmentation action sequence for CT kidney tumour segmentation with a dueling DQN rewarded by the change in validation Dice (DSC 84.0 against 83.2 for standard augmentation).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Shows degradation parameters (noise bound, bias field, deformation) learned by gradient inside a medical segmentation loop, with projection to keep them realistic, and both a gradient and an RL search over augmentation magnitudes for CT and MR; these are the mechanisms by which our ladder's blur sigma, threshold softness and fade rate could be set by gradient or by a search instead of by hand, and Xu's cost figure (about 100 GPU hours against 768 for an RL search) is a planning number for a Metaflow sweep block.
14. **AutoAugment: Learning Augmentation Strategies from Data** (Cubuk 2019; CVPR 2019, pp. 113-123). https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2019/html/Cubuk_AutoAugment_Learning_Augmentation_Strategies_From_Data_CVPR_2019_paper.html
   What it does: A controller RNN samples a policy of five sub-policies, each two operations with a probability and a magnitude; a child network is trained with it and its validation accuracy is the reward, the controller being updated by Proximal Policy Optimization because the reward is not differentiable (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.09501). Its descendants trade the RL search for cheaper learners: Population Based Augmentation learns a nonstationary policy schedule with about a thousand times less compute (Ho et al., ICML 2019, PMLR 97:2731-2741, https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/ho19b.html); Adversarial AutoAugment trains an LSTM policy by REINFORCE with the target network's training loss as reward, so the policy seeks harder augmentations in the same run (Zhang et al., ICLR 2020, https://www.iclr.cc/virtual/2020/poster/2091); Faster AutoAugment learns the policy by backpropagation with a differentiable operation selector and approximate gradients for discrete parameters, minimizing a distance between augmented and original data distributions (Hataya et al., ECCV 2020, https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2020/papers_ECCV/html/4830_ECCV_2020_paper.php); DADA relaxes the discrete selection with Gumbel-Softmax and the RELAX estimator for a one-pass search (Li et al., ECCV 2020, https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2020/papers_ECCV/html/4120_ECCV_2020_paper.php).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The reference family for choosing a degradation or augmentation schedule by reinforcement learning or by gradient: PBA is the one that learns a schedule that changes over training, which is what an annealed ladder is, and Adversarial AutoAugment's loss-as-reward is the natural signal if a learner is to choose how fast the mask weight falls; the compute figures (RL search expensive, population and gradient methods cheap) decide which of these a Metaflow sweep block can afford.
15. **Automated Curriculum Learning for Neural Networks** (Graves 2017; ICML 2017). https://icml.cc/virtual/2017/poster/701
   What it does: The path through a curriculum is chosen by a nonstationary multi-armed bandit whose reward is a learning-progress signal, either the rate of increase in prediction accuracy or in network complexity, yielding a stochastic syllabus; on LSTM tasks across three curricula it accelerated learning, in some cases halving the time to a set performance. Teacher-Student Curriculum Learning (Matiisen et al., arXiv 1707.00183, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.00183v1) samples subtasks by the absolute slope of the student's learning curve, so tasks being forgotten are revisited, with Online, Naive, Window and Sampling variants, matching hand-made curricula on LSTM decimal addition and Minecraft navigation.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: An automated alternative to a hand-set annealing schedule: treat the rungs of the degradation ladder (identity fade, blur, threshold) as bandit arms and let learning progress on the measurement loss decide when the mask is degraded further, which fits a sweepable Metaflow block with no gradient through the schedule; the revisiting rule guards against the network forgetting how to read the mask-free image while the mask is still strong.

## Landmark and heatmap post-processing

1. **Coarse to Fine Vertebrae Localization and Segmentation with SpatialConfiguration-Net and U-Net** (Payer 2020; VISAPP 2020 (SciTePress), pp. 124-133). https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2020/89752/89752.pdf
   What it does: Three-stage spine CT pipeline: a U-Net regresses a spine-centreline heatmap at 8 mm (centre of mass gives the spine position), a SpatialConfiguration-Net at 2 mm predicts one heatmap per vertebra with a learned per-vertebra sigma (large volumes run as overlapping z sub-volumes merged by max), and a binary U-Net at 1 mm segments each vertebra from a crop plus a Gaussian of its centroid. Readout is post hoc and rule based: several local maxima above a threshold are kept per vertebra heatmap, the first and last visible vertebrae are the strongest peaks nearest the top and bottom, and the final labelling is the longest candidate sequence in which consecutive vertebrae are 12.5-50 mm apart and none lies above its predecessor. VerSe 2019 test sets: identification 95.65% / 94.25%, point error 4.27 / 4.80 mm, Dice 0.909 / 0.898, first of 11 teams; in cross-validation the thoracic levels had the lowest identification rate.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: This is the closest published counterpart to our column chain: per-level heatmaps from an SCN followed by a hard, non-differentiable longest-valid-sequence search whose only constraints are inter-vertebral distance and ordering, with no explicit states for L6, T13 or sacralization. Its numbers (4-5 mm point error, thoracic levels weakest) are the baseline our soft-Viterbi chain over level types should be measured against on the same VerSe-style data, and its learned per-vertebra sigma is a precedent for letting heatmap width vary by level.
2. **Integrating spatial configuration into heatmap regression based CNNs for landmark localization** (Payer 2019; Medical Image Analysis 54:207-219 (DOI 10.1016/j.media.2019.03.007)). https://tugraz.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/integrating-spatial-configuration-into-heatmap-regression-based-c/
   What it does: The SpatialConfiguration-Net splits heatmap regression into a local-appearance component that gives locally accurate but ambiguous candidates and a spatial-configuration component that produces unambiguous but coarse heatmaps encoding the expected landmark layout; the two are combined by element-wise multiplication (Eq. 1) so both must fire at the true location, and the network is trained end to end. The predicted coordinate is the argmax of the combined heatmap. On 895 hand radiographs with 37 landmarks (3-fold CV) it beats the random-forest methods of Ebner, Lindner, Stern and Urschler and the authors' 2016 CNN, and greatly outperforms a localization U-Net when training is cut to 100, 50 or 10 images (full text seen at https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.00748).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Shows that a learned geometric prior can live inside the network as a multiplicative gate on candidate heatmaps rather than as a post hoc model, which is the natural place to fuse our per-vertebra landmark heatmaps with the chain's level posterior; its argmax readout has no sub-voxel precision, which matters when the lines feed mm-scale end-plate and canal measurements, and its data-limited advantage is relevant to the size of our oracle-labelled registry.
3. **Uncertainty Estimation for Heatmap-based Landmark Localization** (Schobs 2023; IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging 42(4) (DOI 10.1109/TMI.2022.3222730)). https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/195227/
   What it does: Compares three epistemic uncertainty measures read from heatmaps: S-MHA (inverted peak activation of a single model), E-MHA (peak of the mean heatmap of a T=5 ensemble, the proposal) and E-CPV (variance of the ensemble's peak coordinates), on a U-Net and a patch-voting PHD-Net, for cardiac MRI (303 short-axis, 422 four-chamber images) and the ISBI cephalometric set (19 landmarks, 11 annotators on a 5-landmark subset). Quantile Binning sorts validation uncertainties into Q=5 bins and maps bin edges to error bounds by monotonic isotonic regression; evaluation is the Jaccard index between predicted and true error-quantile bins and the share of errors inside the bounds. Spearman error-uncertainty correlations were 0.27-0.53; keeping only the lowest-uncertainty bin cut mean error by about 32-35%; E-MHA failed on landmarks with directional annotator ambiguity because the isotropic Gaussian target constrains the heatmap, E-CPV did not. The paper uses no test-time augmentation and no training augmentation (accepted PDF at https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/195227/1/Qbin_TMI_2022.pdf).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Gives a ready recipe for calibrating a heatmap-derived uncertainty into error bounds with a small validation set, which is what an evaluation page needs if landmark confidence is to gate a measurement; it also warns that peak-activation uncertainty from isotropic Gaussian targets is blind to anisotropic ambiguity, which end-plate corners along a cortex will have. Related measures we saw: Drevicky and Kodym 2020 (BIOIMAGING, https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2020/93753/93753.pdf) found MHA, 15-model ensemble variance and MC-dropout variance all track out-of-distribution strength (|rho| 0.81-0.95) with MHA free at inference; Liu et al. 2020 (arXiv, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.02067) found voxel-wise heatmap variance useless under sparse Gaussian targets and proposed Maximum Activation Dispersion over MC-dropout (T=100) and test-time augmentation (N=100) samples, with TTA raising acceptable targets from 55 to 57 of 60 while MC dropout did not.
4. **Modeling Annotation Uncertainty with Gaussian Heatmaps in Landmark Localization** (Thaler 2021; Machine Learning for Biomedical Imaging (MELBA) 2021:014, UNSURE 2020 special issue, pp. 1-27 (DOI 10.59275/j.melba.2021-77a7)). https://www.melba-journal.org/papers/2021:014.html
   What it does: Built on the SpatialConfiguration-Net, the method learns a full anisotropic covariance (orientation, major and minor extent) per landmark jointly with the weights, with a pixel-wise L2 loss plus a penalty on the Gaussian's area, so the shared target shapes absorb dataset-level annotation ambiguity. At inference a robust least-squares Gaussian fit to each predicted heatmap returns the location and a per-image covariance; MC dropout (K=20) is the comparison. On 895 hand radiographs (37 landmarks) and 400 cephalograms (19 landmarks, 11 annotations per landmark on 100 images) both uncertainty measures correlate with localization error and inter-observer variability, and the predicted uncertainty changes the classification of cephalometric abnormalities (details read at https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.09533). It extends Payer et al., UNSURE 2020, LNCS 12443 pp. 42-51 (https://tugraz.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/uncertainty-estimation-in-landmark-localization-based-on-gaussian/).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Directly relevant to reading sub-voxel landmarks and their covariance off heatmaps by Gaussian fitting rather than argmax, and to the oracle: our targets are deterministic lines on masks, so a learned per-landmark covariance would expose where the mask-derived definition is itself ambiguous (end-plate corners, canal margins) and would let a measurement carry an interval; the downstream experiment is a template for propagating landmark uncertainty into a Panjabi measurement.
5. **Contour-Hugging Heatmaps for Landmark Detection** (McCouat 2022; CVPR 2022). https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/McCouat_Contour-Hugging_Heatmaps_for_Landmark_Detection_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf
   What it does: Treats landmark detection as pixel classification: a ResNet-34 U-Net is trained with a 2D softmax and negative log-likelihood against one-hot targets, so the heatmaps are free to be asymmetric, multimodal and to follow anatomical edges; the landmark is the hottest pixel. Expected Radial Error (ERE) is the probability-weighted mean distance from the prediction, computed after zeroing values under 5% of the peak (raising correlation with true error from 0.9 to 0.96), and one learned temperature per landmark is fitted on the frozen net, giving an ECE of 0.7 and leaving localization unchanged; scaling did not help a Gaussian-trained model. ISBI 2015 cephalometric: MRE 1.20 mm (test 1) and 1.46 mm (test 2); an ERE threshold of 1.414 split test 2 into 85% good landmarks (MRE 1.30 mm) and 15% flagged (MRE 2.43 mm).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: A cheap, single-pass calibrated uncertainty: per-landmark temperature scaling of a softmax heatmap is exactly what a sweepable training block could add, and ERE is a differentiable scalar that could weight each landmark's contribution to a measurement line; the finding that temperature scaling fails on MSE-trained Gaussian heatmaps bears on the choice of heatmap loss in the ladder.
6. **Vertebrae Identification and Localization Utilizing Fully Convolutional Networks and a Hidden Markov Model** (Chen 2019; IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging 39(2):387-399 (online July 2019; DOI 10.1109/TMI.2019.2927289)). https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109/TMI.2019.2927289
   What it does: An FCN trained end to end on downscaled CT captures long-range context along the spine while an auxiliary network restores local detail, and a hidden Markov model over the ordered vertebra sequence imposes explicit sequential constraints on the per-vertebra outputs, which the authors credit with robustness and interpretable output. On the MICCAI 2014 CSI localization and identification set it reports 94.67% identification within 20 mm, a mean identification rate of 87.97% and a mean localization error of 2.56 mm. The related Liao, Mesfin and Luo 2018 (IEEE TMI 37(5):1266-1275, DOI 10.1109/TMI.2018.2798293, https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109/TMI.2018.2798293) uses a multi-task 3D FCN for short-range context and a bidirectional RNN over the visible vertebrae for the long-range ordering.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The HMM here is the post hoc Viterbi decode that our differentiable forward pass replaces inside the loss; its reported identification gap (94.67% within 20 mm versus 87.97% mean identification) is the kind of level-naming error budget our chain must beat, and neither it nor Liao's RNN carries explicit states for L6, T13 or sacralization, which is where our chain differs.
7. **Automated detection, labelling and radiological grading of clinical spinal MRIs** (Windsor 2024; Scientific Reports 14:14993 (extends the authors' MICCAI 2020 paper on whole-spine MRI labelling)). https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:822ac45e-ed5b-4e92-a268-c5a191f2bf22/files/rnv9354129
   What it does: Vertebrae are detected by a U-Net that outputs corner and centroid heatmaps plus vector fields pointing corners to their centroid, grouped into quadrilaterals and linked across slices by IoU. Labelling places each detection's appearance-only probability over 23 levels (C3-S1) into a probability-height map, refines it with a convolutional context network, and decodes a valid sequence with a penalised beam search that uses soft constraints: successive detections should take successive labels, while transitional vertebrae and numerical variation are penalised rather than forbidden; no anchor vertebra such as C2 or S1 is needed, and detections are dropped at random (p=0.1) in training so the context net learns to infer levels across gaps. Identification rates 96.5% (whole spine, 888 vertebrae), 98.4% (Genodisc lumbar, 2947) and 90.9% (Zukic), against 86.9-90.1% for an LSTM baseline.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The nearest published analogue of our level chain's treatment of transitional anatomy: soft penalties for L6-type variation inside a sequence decoder, no dependence on seeing a reference vertebra, and robustness training by dropping detections; it is still a non-differentiable beam search over a map, so our forward-pass formulation is a step beyond it, and its per-dataset IDRs are a benchmark for naming accuracy on partial fields of view.
8. **Integral Human Pose Regression** (Sun 2018; ECCV 2018). https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2018/papers_ECCV/papers/Xiao_Sun_Integral_Human_Pose_ECCV_2018_paper.pdf
   What it does: Replaces the argmax over a heatmap by its expectation after a softmax (soft-argmax), which is differentiable, continuous, parameter-free and less sensitive to heatmap resolution (halving the MPII heatmap cost 0.6 AUC with integral readout versus 4.8 with argmax). It studies heatmap losses (MSE to Gaussian, softmax cross-entropy to one-hot, BCE within 15 px) combined with an L1 or L2 joint loss (L1 more consistent), finds pretraining with a heatmap loss and then switching to the integral loss slightly better than joint training, and reports 67.8 AP on COCO (ResNet-101) against 66.3 for the heatmap baseline and 49.6 mm MPJPE on Human3.6M with mixed 2D data. Nibali et al. 2018 (arXiv 1801.07372, https://alphaxiv.org/abs/1801.07372) is the parallel DSNT formulation: the weighted mean of grid positions over a normalised map, with a Jensen-Shannon regulariser towards a Gaussian, weaker for joints near the image border.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Soft-argmax is what makes a landmark coordinate, and hence a line length or canal width, differentiable with respect to the heatmap, so it is the readout our measurement-oracle loss requires; its resolution robustness argues for coarser heatmaps in a 3D CT budget, and the heatmap-then-integral schedule and the DSNT shape regulariser are candidates for the sweepable blocks.
9. **Removing the Bias of Integral Pose Regression** (Gu 2021; ICCV 2021, pp. 11067-11076 (companion: Dive Deeper Into Integral Pose Regression, ICLR 2022)). https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021/html/Gu_Removing_the_Bias_of_Integral_Pose_Regression_ICCV_2021_paper.html
   What it does: Shows that integral (soft-argmax) regression carries a systematic bias that arises from taking the expectation after the softmax, proposes a compensation that improves integral regression on all 2D pose benchmarks, and a joint detection plus bias-compensated regression head. The ICLR 2022 follow-up (https://iclr.cc/virtual/2022/poster/5955) argues the expectation should in theory always beat the argmax but that the regression heatmap's activation shrinks on easy cases, which it identifies as a leading cause of integral regression's weaker accuracy and slower training, and adds spatial-prior supervision to fix both.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: If our landmarks are read by soft-argmax over a vertebra neighbourhood, the softmax-tail bias pulls every point towards the window centre and shrinks end-plate widths and canal depths systematically; the compensation and the spatial-prior auxiliary loss are the published fixes, and the shrinkage diagnosis says to monitor peak activation per level on the witness frames.
10. **Distribution-Aware Coordinate Representation for Human Pose Estimation** (Zhang 2020; CVPR 2020, pp. 7093-7102). https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2020/papers/Zhang_Distribution-Aware_Coordinate_Representation_for_Human_Pose_Estimation_CVPR_2020_paper.pdf
   What it does: Documents that standard decoding (argmax shifted a quarter pixel towards the second-highest activation) and quantised Gaussian encoding both bias heatmap methods. DARK smooths the predicted heatmap with the training Gaussian, takes the log so the Gaussian is quadratic, and applies a second-order Taylor expansion at the maximum, mu = m - H(m)^-1 grad D(m), for a sub-pixel estimate; encoding places the target Gaussian at the exact sub-pixel centre. HRNet-W32 on COCO val at 128x96: 61.2 AP with no shift, 66.9 with the quarter shift, 68.4 with the Taylor decode, 70.7 with unbiased encoding as well; on MPII, PCKh@0.1 rises from 37.7 to 42.0 while PCKh@0.5 moves only 90.3 to 90.6. Bulat et al. 2021 (arXiv 2111.02360, https://paperswithcode.com/paper/subpixel-heatmap-regression-for-facial) likewise find discretisation in both encoding and decoding has a surprisingly large effect and add a Siamese consistency loss across geometric transforms.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The clearest evidence that the gains from sub-pixel post-processing appear at tight thresholds, which is where Panjabi-scale measurements live; the Taylor step is a closed-form, cheap alternative to Gaussian fitting for the final mm-precise readout, and the unbiased encoding rule applies to how our oracle's continuous landmark coordinates are rasterised into training heatmaps on a coarse CT grid.
11. **LUVLi Face Alignment: Estimating Landmarks' Location, Uncertainty, and Visibility Likelihood** (Kumar 2020; CVPR 2020). https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2020/papers/Kumar_LUVLi_Face_Alignment_Estimating_Landmarks_Location_Uncertainty_and_Visibility_Likelihood_CVPR_2020_paper.pdf
   What it does: Reads each landmark as the weighted spatial mean of the ReLU-thresholded heatmap (sub-pixel; ReLU beat softmax and tempered softmax in ablation) and predicts a per-landmark 2x2 covariance through a Cholesky estimator head on the U-Net bottleneck, trained with a Gaussian or Laplacian negative log-likelihood whose log-determinant term stops the uncertainty growing without bound. Calibration is checked by binning landmarks by predicted variance and comparing with mean squared residuals (Pearson 0.98-0.99 across 734 bins, falling to 0.72-0.84 with 36 bins), and occluded landmarks on COFW-68 get about 3.5x larger predicted uncertainty without occlusion labels. The authors note the predicted covariances were often narrower than one 64x64 heatmap pixel, which is why heatmap-derived uncertainty underperformed the direct covariance head.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Offers the combination our setting needs in one network: a differentiable soft-argmax variant plus a heteroscedastic covariance per landmark with a binned calibration check; the warning that heatmap-pixel resolution bounds the uncertainty you can read off a heatmap is directly relevant to 3D CT at 1-2 mm voxels, where a separate covariance head may be the only route to sub-voxel error bars on the measurement lines.
12. **Integrating geometric configuration and appearance information into a unified framework for anatomical landmark localization** (Urschler 2018; Medical Image Analysis 43:23-36 (DOI 10.1016/j.media.2017.09.003)). https://tugraz.elsevierpure.com/de/publications/integrating-geometric-configuration-and-appearance-information-in/
   What it does: Combines image appearance and inter-landmark geometry in one random-forest framework and refines the joint landmark configuration iteratively by coordinate descent, weighting appearance or geometry by how strongly the landmarks are correlated and without fixing a graphical-model structure in advance; evaluated on five 2D and 3D datasets with state-of-the-art results (abstract-level read). Two deep successors that constrain landmarks post hoc or in-network: Yang et al., IPMI 2017 (LNCS 10265 pp. 633-644, https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-319-59050-9_50) refine DI2IN vertebra-centroid probability maps by message passing on the mutual relations between centroids plus sparsity regularisation, 90% identification on 302 CT volumes in about 3 s; Li et al., ECCV 2020 (https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2020/papers_ECCV/html/781_ECCV_2020_paper.php) learn an adaptive landmark graph end to end with two GCNs from local image and global shape features on cephalometric, hand and pelvis radiographs.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: These are the explicit geometric-constraint family (MRF-like coordinate descent, message passing, graph convolution) that sits between raw heatmaps and final coordinates; for us the relevant lesson is that the constraint weight should depend on how correlated the landmarks are, which for a vertebra's own end-plate corners is high and across levels is weaker, and that Yang's message passing over per-level maps is a differentiable alternative to a hard sequence search.
13. **Fully Automatic System for Accurate Localisation and Analysis of Cephalometric Landmarks in Lateral Cephalograms** (Lindner 2016; Scientific Reports 6:33581 (DOI 10.1038/srep33581)). https://www.nature.com/articles/srep33581
   What it does: Random-forest regression voting first finds skull pose and scale, then a Constrained Local Model (RFRV-CLM) searches coarse to fine for shape and pose parameters of a PCA statistical shape model that maximise the votes over all 19 landmarks jointly, so individual noisy votes are regularised towards shapes seen in training. On 400 cephalograms annotated by two orthodontists the mean point-to-point error is 1.2 mm with 84.7% of landmarks within 2 mm, and eight derived clinical measurements reach 78.4% average successful classification and 83.4% overall accuracy, within the manual inter-observer figures of 75.4% and 79.7%.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The reference example of a statistical shape model as the post-prediction constraint and, more usefully, of reporting a measurement-from-landmarks error budget against inter-observer agreement rather than against landmark error alone; our evaluation of Panjabi lines should do the same, with the oracle's mask-derived lines and a second reader as the two references.
14. **Evaluation and comparison of accurate automated spinal curvature estimation algorithms with spinal anterior-posterior X-Ray images: The AASCE2019 challenge** (Wang 2021; Medical Image Analysis 72:102115 (DOI 10.1016/j.media.2021.102115)). https://repub.eur.nl/pub/136086
   What it does: Reviews eight top methods from the 12 teams of the MICCAI 2019 AASCE challenge on anterior-posterior spine radiographs with manual landmarks, scored by symmetric mean absolute percentage error over three Cobb angles; the best reached SMAPE 21.71%. The challenge page (https://aasce19.grand-challenge.org/) lists Seg4Reg (segmentation then regression) first, a keypoint-detection method second and centreline extraction with cascaded ensembles third. Khanal et al. 2019 (https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1910.14202) is a typical landmark route: Faster R-CNN finds 17 vertebrae, a DenseNet regresses four corners each, angles come from the per-vertebra slopes, and the post-processing of rejecting outlier boxes, padding or trimming to exactly 17 vertebrae and smoothing corners with a degree-6 polynomial took SMAPE from 33.3% to 25.69%. For the manual budget, Carman et al. 1990 (J Bone Joint Surg Am 72(3):328-333, https://utsouthwestern.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/measurement-of-scoliosis-and-kyphosis-radiographs-intraobserver-a) found a mean difference of 3.8 degrees between repeat Cobb readings on scoliosis films, 95% of differences within 8 degrees, and a 10-degree change needed for 95% confidence of true change.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Angles and lengths derived from four corners per vertebra amplify corner noise, and the winning entries either avoided landmarks or spent most of their effort on post hoc geometric cleanup (count enforcement, polynomial smoothing); our oracle-supervised lines plus the level chain are the in-training version of that cleanup, and Carman's 8-10 degree manual budget is the bar a derived angle has to be judged against.
15. **Vertebral Deformity Measurements at MRI, CT, and Radiography Using Deep Learning** (Suri 2021; Radiology: Artificial Intelligence 4(1), e210015 (DOI 10.1148/ryai.2021210015)). https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryai.2021210015
   What it does: SpineToolKit detects each vertebral body, places the six landmarks of quantitative morphometry, derives anterior, middle and posterior heights and a lumbar lordosis angle from L1 and L5 corners, and picks the imaging section with the most visible vertebrae. On the holdout set height error was 1.5 +/- 0.3% (MR), 1.9 +/- 0.2% (CT) and 1.7 +/- 0.4% (radiographs); median landmark error relative to vertebral size was 4.1/4.5% in x/y on CT; detection 97.0% at IoU 0.70; lordosis MAE 2.26 degrees on CT; the research-assistant ground truth itself differed from musculoskeletal radiologists by about 0.88 mm on CT. Companion budgets: Zakharov et al. (Medical Image Analysis, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.06818) localise six keypoints per vertebra anchor-free on a straightened mid-sagittal CT (soft-argmax for the spine, Butterfly-IoU on keypoint hexagons for NMS), grade by G = min/max of the three heights with thresholds 0.8 and 0.74, and reach vertebra-level ROC AUC 0.959 against inter-expert 0.971 on LungCancer-500 and 0.951 on VerSe; Arakawa et al. 2025 (JBMR Plus, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40098981/) report landmark errors of 2.9-4.0% of the axis with larger errors at central landmarks of collapsed vertebrae and a systematic underestimate of anterior and central height loss; Engelke et al. 2019 (Arch Osteoporos 14:18, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11657-019-0577-2) find automated six-point heights 2.2-3.6% larger than manual morphometry, rmsCV 4.2-4.7% in unfractured and 14.5% in fractured vertebrae, with manual six-point morphometry itself at 3-6% rmsCV.

## Vertebra labelling with a chain prior

1. **VERIDAH: Solving Enumeration Anomaly Aware Vertebra Labeling across Imaging Sequences** (Moeller 2026; arXiv preprint 2601.14066 (TUM / Kirschke and Menze groups; code in github.com/Hendrik-code/spineps)). https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14066v1
   What it does: Labels vertebrae in T2w MRI and CT of arbitrary field of view from existing instance segmentations: a 3D DenseNet with four heads (23-class label, region, 'transition' class such as last thoracic / first lumbar, and visibility) is followed by a constrained dynamic-programming sequence predictor over smoothed head outputs. Hard constraints give infinite cost to non-consecutive or non-monotone labels, cap counts at 7 cervical, 11-13 thoracic and 4-6 lumbar, allow L1 after T11 once and a doubled T12 (T13) or doubled L5 (L6) once, with each vertebra's cost scaled by predicted visibility so boundary vertebrae count less; T13/L6 are trained as T12/L5 and relabelled afterwards. On 365 CT test scans it reaches 99.18% of subjects with every vertebra correct versus 77.81% for Meng et al., with thoracic/lumbar enumeration-anomaly recall of 96.30%/97.22%; on 1,000 NAKO T2w scans (test set enriched to 8.2% thoracic and 14.6% lumbar anomalies) 98.30% versus 94.24% for TotalSpineSeg and 91.49% for SpineNetV2, whose thoracic-anomaly recall was 0.0%.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The closest published precedent to our differentiable chain over level types: an explicit path model over the column with L6, T13 and the 11-thoracic variant as extra transitions, calibrated head weights, and visibility-weighted costs for cut-off vertebrae, but solved by hard-constrained DP after training rather than by a soft forward pass inside the loss. Its metrics (per-subject all-correct rate, TEA/LEA recall, anomalies defined by a true rib) and its finding that the sequence step is what makes multi-head evidence pay off are directly reusable for our evaluation; it does not model sacralization as a state, which leaves room for ours.
2. **Vertebrae localization, segmentation and identification using a graph optimization and an anatomic consistency cycle** (Meng 2022; Machine Learning in Medical Imaging (MLMI 2022), LNCS 13583, pp. 307-317 (arXiv 2110.12177v3)). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.12177
   What it does: After localization and per-vertebra segmentation, a 3D VGG-style classifier on the mask with its neighbours predicts a hierarchical label (region, then vertebra), training T13 as T12 and L6 as L5; identification is then a shortest path (Dijkstra) through a layered graph of n vertebrae x 24 labels whose edges force consecutive labels, with extra higher-cost edges T12->T12 (T13), T11->L1 (missing T12) and L5->L5 (L6), and a post-step that renames a second T12 or L5. An anatomic consistency cycle re-inserts or removes candidates using volume and inter-vertebral-distance priors and reports regions that still violate them. Ablation: 70.5% with a single mask, 84.6% with neighbours, 97.4% with the graph; VerSe20 identification rate 96.59% (public) / 95.38% (hidden), falling to 93.61% / 89.41% without the anatomic priors.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: A worked example of naming after localisation by a chain graph whose transitions encode the variants we care about (L6, T13, T11-to-L1), with the transition weights set on training data and a candid note that they did not generalise as hoped and that un-separated sacralized S1s were called L6; our soft-Viterbi learns those transition costs end to end and could add a sacralization state. Venue confirmed at https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-031-21014-3_32.
3. **VerSe: A Vertebrae labelling and segmentation benchmark for multi-detector CT images** (Sekuboyina 2021; Medical Image Analysis 73:102166 (arXiv 2001.09193)). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.09193
   What it does: Reports the VerSe'19 (160 scans, 141 patients, 1,725 vertebrae) and VerSe'20 (319 scans, 300 patients, 4,141 vertebrae) challenges and benchmarks the submitted pipelines. A vertebra counts as identified when its predicted centroid is the closest predicted landmark to the true centroid and within 20 mm; the per-scan identification rate is averaged over scans, missing vertebrae lower it, and Dice/Hausdorff are averaged per vertebra. Labels run 1-24 from C1 down, with 25 for L6 and 28 for T13; VerSe'19 held two L6 cases (training only) and no method identified the hidden-set L6, VerSe'20 held 6 T13 and 47 L6 cases, only one team identified every T13 in both phases, and the worst VerSe'19 scan lacked L5 so most methods shifted L4 to L5. The paper describes how the top entries label the sequence: Payer (rule-based spacing then a graph MRF solved as a shortest path), Lessmann (bottom-up from the lowest visible vertebra with a global maximum-likelihood labelling, so an L6 can shift a whole scan), Chen D. (a reasoning module constraining sequences to valid ones).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Defines the identification-rate convention our tables should follow and quantifies how rare L6/T13 are in the standard CT benchmark (and that experts anchor on the last rib at T12). It documents the exact failure mode a column-wide chain must prevent: a one-label shift of the whole column from a mis-anchored end vertebra. Venue record: https://vivo.weill.cornell.edu/display/pubid34340104.
4. **A computed tomography vertebral segmentation dataset with anatomical variations and multi-vendor scanner data** (Liebl 2021; Scientific Data 8 (2021) (arXiv 2103.06360)). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.06360
   What it does: Describes the VerSe'20 release: 300 subjects, 319 series, 4,142 vertebrae (581 cervical, 2,255 thoracic, 1,306 lumbar), enriched with 77 enumeration abnormalities and 161 transitional vertebrae. Per series it counts 11/12/13 thoracic vertebrae in 8/131/6, 4/5/6 lumbar in 3/165/85, stump ribs in 90, cervical ribs in 18, and Castellvi 0-I/IIa/IIb/IIIa/IIIb/IV in 146/35/25/14/28/7, with Castellvi III-IV partially fused segments left unsegmented. Labelling counts down from C1; when T1 is outside the field of view the thoracic spine is assumed to have 12 vertebrae; a rib longer than about 38 mm marks a vertebra thoracic; the best docker reached 95.6% mean identification rate.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The one public CT set with enough L6, T13 and Castellvi-graded sacralization cases to validate the anomaly states of our chain, with the annotation conventions (count from C1, assume 12 thoracic without T1, rib-length rule) that our oracle and level-naming must either adopt or state a difference from. Venue confirmed at https://doaj.org/article/700b8e5595af4301b434c41fb2464e97.
5. **A Convolutional Approach to Vertebrae Detection and Labelling in Whole Spine MRI (and its successor SpineNetV2)** (Windsor 2020; arXiv 2007.02606 (Windsor, Jamaludin, Kadir, Zisserman); SpineNetV2 technical report arXiv 2205.01683 (2022); journal version Scientific Reports 14:14993 (2024)). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.02606v2
   What it does: Vertebrae detected by a corner/centroid vector-field network are labelled in three parts: an appearance CNN gives a calibrated softmax over levels per vertebra, a fully convolutional context network refines a probability-height map built from all detections (trained with random removal of end vertebrae), and a beam search decodes the column, giving zero probability to repeated levels, penalising skipped levels and adding a small penalty for a lumbar count of 5+-1. No anchor vertebra is required; the main error is S2 called S1, which shifts every label; a 25th vertebra between S1 and C2 is annotated L6. Identification rate 96.5% on 37 whole-spine scans (888 vertebrae), 98.4% on 421 lumbar Genodisc scans and 90.9% on the Zukic set, against 86.9-90.1% for a bidirectional-LSTM labeller trained by the same authors. SpineNetV2 keeps the design (23 classes S1-C3, beam width 100 from the bottom detection, double S1/C3 detections tolerated) and is the labeller used by the group's Spinal Context Transformer for grading (https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2206.13173).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The context-aware labelling the task asked about: a learned column-wide context map plus a sequence decoder with an explicit prior on the lumbar count, a direct comparison showing it beating an RNN labeller, and an honest account of the S1/S2 anchoring ambiguity. Our soft chain replaces the hand-set beam penalties with learned transitions; the SpineNetV2 journal record is at https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:822ac45e-ed5b-4e92-a268-c5a191f2bf22.
6. **Automatic Localization and Identification of Vertebrae in Arbitrary Field-of-View CT Scans (with the 2013 follow-up on pathological spines)** (Glocker 2012; MICCAI 2012 (Glocker, Feulner, Criminisi, Haynor, Konukoglu); follow-up MICCAI 2013, LNCS 8150 p. 262). https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/glocker2012spine.poster.pdf
   What it does: Regression forests vote for the 26 vertebral centroids, then a generative hidden Markov model over the chain refines and identifies them: the hidden states are the 26 centroids plus a global scale, the emission compares local intensities to a per-vertebra template, the transition is a Gaussian on the inter-centroid offset scaled by s, and inference is dynamic programming with a 7-value scale search. On 200 trauma CTs (2,595 vertebrae, 4 vertebrae to whole-body) it identified 81% (72% cervical, 78% thoracic, 85% lumbar) by two-fold cross-validation. The 2013 paper (https://iacl.ece.jhu.edu/proceedings/miccai2013/html/8150/81500262.htm) replaces the model with dense classification forests trained from sparse centroid annotations on 224 pathological spines plus 200 abdominal CTs; later tables on the 302-scan public split quote it at 74.0% with 13.2 mm mean error.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The origin of both the HMM-over-the-column formulation and the arbitrary-field-of-view, 20 mm identification-rate evaluation that every later CT labeller inherits; its chain has a fixed 26-state alphabet with no L6/T13, which is exactly the limitation our level-type alphabet addresses, and its scale state is a reminder to let the transition model absorb patient size.
7. **Vertebrae Identification and Localization Utilizing Fully Convolutional Networks and a Hidden Markov Model** (Chen 2020; IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging 39(2):387-399 (published online July 2019)). https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109%2FTMI.2019.2927289
   What it does: Treats the task as detecting and indexing a long sequence in a volume: a fully convolutional network on down-scaled input captures long-range context along the spine, an auxiliary network restores fine detail, and a hidden Markov model imposes sequential constraints on the per-vertebra outputs. On the MICCAI 2014 vertebra localisation and identification challenge data (the 302-scan set) the abstract reports a 94.67% identification rate within 20 mm on the test set, a mean identification rate of 87.97% and a 2.56 mm mean localisation error, the best figures on that set at the time.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The clearest modern CT precedent for 'network emissions plus HMM decoding' and therefore the baseline our differentiable (soft-Viterbi) chain should be contrasted with; only the OpenAlex abstract was reachable, so the two reported rates could not be reconciled from the full text.
8. **Joint Vertebrae Identification and Localization in Spinal CT Images by Combining Short- and Long-Range Contextual Information** (Liao 2018; IEEE Transactions on Medical Imaging 37(5):1266-1275 (arXiv 1812.03500)). https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.03500
   What it does: A multi-task 3D CNN on 32x112x96 sub-volumes predicts a 26-way label plus a centroid offset for each positive sample; the samples are ordered by position along the scan and a three-layer bidirectional LSTM (256 units) re-labels and re-regresses them, so each vertebra sees the whole visible column. No hard ordering rule is imposed; dense predictions are aggregated by the median location per label. On the 302-scan public set (242/60 split) it reaches 88.3% identification (95.1% cervical, 84.0% thoracic, 92.2% lumbar, 6.47 mm mean error) versus 83.8% for the CNN alone and 74.0% / 84.2% for Glocker and Chen 2015.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The bidirectional recurrent approach the task named, showing how much long-range context adds over a local classifier on arbitrary-field-of-view CT; it leaves monotonicity and variant handling to the learned network, which our explicit chain makes structural. Venue confirmed at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109%2FTMI.2018.2798293.
9. **Btrfly Net: Vertebrae Labelling with Energy-based Adversarial Learning of Local Spine Prior (and the Radiology: AI 2020 extension)** (Sekuboyina 2018; MICCAI 2018 (arXiv 1804.01307); extension in Radiology: Artificial Intelligence 2(2):e190074 (2020)). https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1804.01307
   What it does: Labels C1-L5 from sagittal and coronal maximum-intensity projections with a two-armed fully convolutional 'butterfly' that fuses the views and outputs 25 heatmap channels; an energy-based adversarial autoencoder with a receptive field of about two to three lumbar vertebrae scores the plausibility of the predicted arrangement during training only, lifting identification from 81.8% to 86.1% on the 302-scan set. 10. **Automatic Vertebra Labeling in Large-Scale 3D CT using Deep Image-to-Image Network with Message Passing and Sparsity Regularization** (Yang 2017; Information Processing in Medical Imaging (IPMI 2017), LNCS 10265, pp. 633-644 (arXiv 1705.05998)). https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.05998
   What it does: An encoder-decoder network regresses one Gaussian probability map per vertebra; the centres then form a chain-structured, MRF-like graph in which each map is iteratively updated by convolved messages from its neighbours with kernels learned from ground-truth spatial relations (a learned approximation of sum-product inference), recovering missing responses. A sparsity step refines coordinates against a dictionary of training centroid vectors after dynamic programming picks the longest descending subsequence of z-coordinates to drop outliers. Identification rate 80%/85% on the two settings of the 302-scan set, rising to 83%/90% after training on 1,000+ additional expert-annotated volumes; absent vertebrae are declared by thresholding each channel's maximum.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The Markov-random-field style labelling in the task: a differentiable, learned message-passing chain between adjacent level maps that can be fine-tuned end to end, which is the nearest ancestor of a soft forward pass over the column; its fixed 26-channel design has no variant states and its dictionary prior assumes a normal count. Venue confirmed at https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-319-59050-9_50.
11. **Iterative fully convolutional neural networks for automatic vertebra segmentation and identification** (Lessmann 2019; Medical Image Analysis 53:142-155 (arXiv 1804.04383)). https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1804.04383
   What it does: One network segments one vertebra per iteration in a 128 mm patch, with an instance memory of already segmented vertebrae as a second channel and a completeness output that excludes partially visible vertebrae; the patch walks along the column. Each vertebra also receives a regressed label value (C1-L5 as 1-24); afterwards every consecutive labelling of the detected sequence is scored by its average likelihood and the best is kept, so labels have no gaps or duplicates whatever the field of view. Across five CT and MR datasets it reports 94.9% Dice, 93% correctly labelled scans and 97% completeness accuracy; its VerSe entry works bottom-up from the lowest visible vertebra with thoracic/cervical votes weighted threefold, which the VerSe paper notes lets an L6 shift a whole scan.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Naming after localisation by maximum likelihood over an offset of a consecutive label sequence, which is the non-differentiable special case of our chain (one path per offset, no variant transitions); its completeness head is the same idea as VERIDAH's visibility weight and worth a channel in our oracle. Venue record at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30771712/.
12. **Automatic Vertebra Localization and Identification in CT by Spine Rectification and Anatomically-constrained Optimization** (Wang 2021; CVPR 2021 (arXiv 2012.07947)). https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2021/papers/Wang_Automatic_Vertebra_Localization_and_Identification_in_CT_by_Spine_Rectification_CVPR_2021_paper.pdf
   What it does: An nnU-Net predicts 26 vertebra-centre activation maps; their sum gives a centreline along which the maps are resampled and collapsed into one 1D signal per vertebra. Labels and positions are then found by an anatomically constrained optimisation on these signals: consecutive indices are a hard constraint built into the search space, even neighbour spacing is a soft regulariser, the end vertebrae C1, C2, S1, S2 carry double weight, and the solver runs an exhaustive search over the starting index, hill climbing on positions and insertion of missed vertebrae. 13. **Robust vertebra identification using simultaneous node and edge predicting Graph Neural Networks** (Buergin 2023; MICCAI 2023 (arXiv 2308.02509)). https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2308.02509
   What it does: A U-Net proposes body and pedicle keypoints; a message-passing GNN over each point's k nearest neighbours (edge features: direction and distance) jointly predicts the level C1-S2 of every body keypoint, a legitimacy score that discards false positives, and body-pedicle association on the edges, with no explicit decoding or ordering constraint. Training uses synthetic spines with and without T13, L6 and S2 and simulated missing or duplicated detections. On a 2,118-scan mixed dataset it identifies 97.19% (89.88% on a hard subset) against 94.29% (79.46%) for an HMM baseline; on VerSe 2019 a single-head GNN reaches 93.26%/93.02% (validation/test) while the HMM baseline collapses to 48.6%/49.1%; the dominant failure is an off-by-one shift at the thoracolumbar junction.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: A recent graphical-model alternative that learns consistency implicitly and reports a direct head-to-head with an HMM, plus evidence that off-by-one errors at the T12/L1 boundary survive even with column-wide context; it argues for keeping the thoracolumbar transition (T13, 11-thoracic) as explicit states in our chain and for synthetic variant augmentation. MICCAI page: https://conferences.miccai.org/2023/papers/558-Paper2279.html.
14. **Spine-Transformers: Vertebra Detection and Localization in Arbitrary Field-of-View Spine CT with Transformers** (Tao 2021; MICCAI 2021, LNCS (doi 10.1007/978-3-030-87199-4_9); code at github.com/gloriatao/Spine-Transformers; a journal extension titled 'Spine-transformers: Vertebra labeling and segmentation in arbitrary field-of-view spine CTs via 3D transformers' exists in Medical Image Analysis (2022) but could not be read). https://miccai2021.org/openaccess/paperlinks/2021/09/01/451-Paper0853.html
   What it does: Casts vertebra detection in arbitrary-field-of-view CT as one-to-one set prediction: a light 3D transformer with skip connections and learnable positional embeddings outputs all vertebrae in a patch in parallel under a set-based global loss that forces unique predictions, and an inscribed-sphere (centre plus radius) detector replaces boxes; a 3D U-Net refines each detection. Reviewers describe the set loss as preserving the sequential order of levels. After re-evaluation on the corrected VerSe 2019 annotations the authors report 97.22% (test) and 96.74% (hidden) identification versus 96.94% and 94.25% for the two best challenge methods, 1.12 mm error on 1,845 in-house T11-L5 vertebrae, and a failure in which L4 was labelled L5 because L5 was not attached to the sacrum.
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: The transformer end of the sequence-model spectrum the task asked about: ordering and identity are learned by attention over the column with no explicit prior, and the reported failure is precisely a lumbosacral-junction naming error of the kind a sacralization-aware chain is meant to catch.
15. **Lumbosacral Transitional Vertebrae: Classification, Imaging Findings, and Clinical Relevance** (Konin 2010; American Journal of Neuroradiology 31(10):1778-1786). https://www.ajnr.org/content/31/10/1778
   What it does: A clinical review of lumbosacral transitional vertebrae (prevalence quoted at 4-30%) and of how to number levels in their presence: full-spine imaging lets the reader count caudally from C2 and recognise hypoplastic ribs, a cervicothoracic MR localiser allows counting down from C2 instead of up from L5 but assumes 7 cervical and 12 thoracic vertebrae and so misses thoracolumbar transitions, the iliolumbar ligament marks L5 but fails with segmentation anomalies, and the Castellvi types I-IV describe the transition without helping the numbering. Measured prevalences in related work: in 147 subjects with radiographic reference, 8.2% had an anomalous total count, 15.0% an LSTV and 4.1% a thoracolumbar transitional vertebra, LSTV predicting an anomalous count (OR 7.4) and the two variants co-occurring far above chance (OR 13.7) (Carrino 2011, Radiology 259(1), https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/10.1148/radiol.11081511); in 880 spinopelvic-fixation patients counted on CT, 12.6% had an LSTV and 78.4% of those also had a thoracolumbar transitional vertebra, most often a T13 (Tatara 2021, JBJS Open Access 6(3), https://doaj.org/article/6a7a79f73ae84764a9e3289d2b6ccc86).
   What bears on the auto-anatomist: Settles the counting convention question for our level naming: count from C2 when the field of view allows, treat the lumbosacral and thoracolumbar junctions as coupled uncertainties rather than independent ones (a T13 and an L6/sacralization often travel together), and expect roughly one scan in ten to carry a variant, which sets the prior mass our chain should give its anomaly states.
