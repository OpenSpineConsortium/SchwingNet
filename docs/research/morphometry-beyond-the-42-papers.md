# What the 42 papers assume, and the data beyond them

Source: the thread "Get full texts through Elsevier key", 2026-10-28, answering the owner's question of 17:00 UTC ("can you tell me about what assumptions im making by limiting to these papers? surely there is more data on the internet particularly given the introduction of ct based morphometrics"). The datasets and studies below that are not among the 42 papers were read on 2026-10-28, each on a page of its own; the links are under Sources. Masharawi 2008 and Shin 2024 were read only as abstracts, on university research-portal records. The claims were then checked a second time against the same pages, and the corrections of that check are in the text. ## The short answer

The naming rule (bordercall's SchwingCheck) does not rest on 42 papers. It rests on one source, Panjabi's twelve cadaveric spines, described in four papers published from 1991 to 1993. No paper says when the spines were measured; the first of the series was accepted for publication on 18 July 1990, so they were collected before then. The other 39 papers of spine-program's synthetic_spine collection were gathered for one figure of the CTSpinoPelvic1K article and feed nothing the rule computes. So the assumptions are Panjabi's, plus every constant a table of means cannot supply, which were set by hand.

Much more data exists, most of it new since CT segmentation became routine. Some of it relates one person's levels to each other, but only over part of the column or in measures other than Panjabi's. The search for this note found no source for what the rule needs most: the within-person correlations of Panjabi's measures over the whole column, C2 to L5, from hundreds of people. Those have to be measured, in many people with many levels measured in each. The source bordercall names for that is one the lab already holds, the census's existing SPINEPS masks counted from C2, but how many of them reach C2 is not yet known. The owner's 2,000 annotated CTs are a separate set (below).

The rule has not been run on a real spine. Every number below about how well it names levels comes from synthetic columns.

## What the rule takes from the papers

- Three papers in the 42: Panjabi's cervical (1991), thoracic (1991) and lumbar (1992). They give the seven measures of the archive's rule.
- One paper outside the 42: the facets paper (Spine 1993;18(10):1298-1310). With the other three it brings the build-up study's pool to 32 measures.
- All four measured the same twelve spines: 8 men and 4 women, aged 19 to 59 (mean 46), half dead of cancer (thoracic paper, Table 1, p. 889), ancestry not stated, boiled and bleached (thoracic p. 888), measured with an electromechanical morphometer. The cervical, lumbar and facets papers say the spines were "radiographed to identify any significant abnormalities" (p. 861, p. 299, pp. 1298-1299). None of the four gives the criteria, what the films showed, or whether any spine was set aside. Table 1 numbers the twelve S3 to S24, not S1 to S12. The thoracic paper chose its reference points because they "showed minimum variation in the presence of osteophytes" (p. 889).
- The papers' acceptance dates, printed on each first page (bordercall archive/bordercall/reference/papers/): thoracic 18 July 1990, cervical 8 November 1990, lumbar 19 April 1991, facets 16 March 1992.
- A level's spread is its printed SEM times the square root of 12.

The other 39 were collected on 2026-09-10 for Figure 6 of the CTSpinoPelvic1K article, which was later cut back to Panjabi alone. The level table covers T11 to L5, and synthetic_spine's README says "There is no record of a systematic search."

The second half of the owner's plan is "all literature populations similar to panjabi". bordercall's build-up study is to pool them hierarchically as he brings them in. That rung has not begun (bordercall TODO.md, item P step e). Besides Panjabi's own three papers, two of the 42 measured the same people at every level from the neck to the lumbar spine. Tan 2004 measured 220 vertebrae of 10 cadavers with a three-dimensional digitiser; its figure captions give C3 to L5, and 10 cadavers of 22 levels make 220, though its abstract prints C3-T12. Busscher 2010 measured six male cadaver spines, dissected from C3 to S1, on CT with 1.00 mm slices (its Methods). Only their T12 to L5 rows were copied (synthetic_spine sources.csv). Five more reach the upper thoracic or cervical spine, but for one region or one measure, not the whole column. Lien 2007 measured pedicles from T1 to L5 in 15 sets of vertebrae. Zindrick 1987 gives pedicles from T1, counted in studies, not people. Kunkel 2011 covers the thoracic spine. Gilad 1985 measured the cervical and lumbar spine on radiographs of 157 men, with no thoracic level. Hinck 1966 gives canal widths from C3 on anteroposterior films, with 50 to 59 adults at each lumbar level (synthetic_spine README.md). The rest are lumbar or thoracolumbar series.

## The assumptions

|---|---|---|
| Twelve spines give every level's mean and spread | The error of a level's mean is about 0.29 of a spread (1 over the root of 12), about what neighbouring levels differ by; the same twelve people stand behind every level | Hundreds of whole columns measured the same way |
| Panjabi's printed tables are what was measured | The record checks the copy against the page, not the page against the bones. Two reads of the page images by eye agree on all 3,786 cells of the four papers (bordercall TODO.md, item P step b). One finding is open. The lumbar paper prints its upper and lower end-plate inclinations at L1 to L5 as the same 20 numbers, means and standard errors, that the cervical paper prints at C3 to C7 (Table 2 of each). The cervical paper's text gives ranges that match its own table, 2 to 5 degrees for the upper and 2 to 4 degrees for the lower. The lumbar paper's text gives no figure for them (archive/bordercall/reference/provenance/prose.csv). The record leaves it to the owner which paper's values are right, if either. The rule scores the lumbar values as printed (TODO.md, item P step b). The two angles are among the 32 measures, not the seven (schwingcheck/README.md), so every result with all 32 uses them. Inside the tables, 6 of the 288 printed ratios and sums are not what the table's own means give. The largest is thoracic Table 5 at T3, printed 1.4 where 12.0 / 6.5 gives 1.85. At T1 the facets paper prints the left superior facet's X-card angle as -47.7 degrees, where the transverse-plane angle, which the rule scores in its place, makes it -57.7. The papers' text contradicts their tables 18 times in 193 numbers, and their comparison tables 8 times in 213 restated values. None of these changed a value in the tables the rule reads (archive/bordercall/reference/provenance/CONTRADICTIONS.md; TODO.md, item P step b). With ostk's tilt-corrected measure it ran 2.3 to 5.2 mm above (L1, L3, L5). End-plate width, canal width and canal depth were within a millimetre at L1 and L3. The mask's edge and the millimetre scale were ruled out. What is left is the population, or what Panjabi's posterior height names, and the cause is not settled. The investigation's reason, that elderly donors had lost height, does not fit: the donors averaged 46 years and the CT patients were 50 and over (spine-program 03 docs/ctsp/BODY_HEIGHT_INVESTIGATION.md; 05 docs/OSTK_VS_EXTRACTION.md; 05 STATUS.md F.35 and F.37; bordercall schwingcheck/data/panjabi_specimens.csv). | Levels move together at rho 0.5 (0.25 between levels given size), equal at every distance | Set by hand (exact.py); no decay with distance is modelled | Within-person correlations from whole columns |
| The measures are independent given size | Set by hand: each measure's offset is drawn independently, r_between 0 by default (covariance.py, schwingcheck/columns.py), and no published within-person correlation between the rule's measures was found (covariance.py). The one outside figure the record holds is a Spearman rho of 0.800 between the anteroposterior and laterolateral body diameters of 125 adult dry lumbar vertebrae (BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2026, abstract). The abstract gives neither the levels nor the number of donors, so it is not shown to be a correlation given size or within one person; covariance.py uses it only to set the range of its sweep. Hipp 2022 prints correlations among four ratios and two angles of the body on lateral NHANES II films, 0.62 to 0.99 over 35,275 lumbar vertebrae (Table 8). Those are pooled over levels and people and are not Panjabi's measures, so they do not give the number either | Correlations between the rule's measures within each level, from whole columns |
| One size factor with a 7% spread, on top of spreads that already include size | Heights rise about 6.5% per level from T9 to L1 (Palm 2025), so the size factor can pass for a one-level shift, and size is counted twice | Height or another size measure with each column |
| Measurement error of 1 mm, Gaussian, at every level | Set by hand, one figure for every length; no source is given for it (bordercall README.md, "One error for all"; schwingcheck/README.md calls it "a stated assumption"). No real scan has been imported yet, so none has a measured chord error (scanimport/README.md). On thirteen synthetic CT phantoms the worst chord error per phantom runs from 0.00 to 3.12 mm; the largest is the posterior height on 5 mm slices tilted 4 degrees, inside its 5.03 mm bound. With no labels, on one body of each of eight phantoms, the error reaches +2.27 mm with the half-maximum edge alone and -1.49 mm with the blurred-step fit, the default. These errors have a direction: under clinical blur the half-maximum edge reads widths and depths long by a millimetre and more and the canal short by as much, and on slices 2.5 mm and thicker the posterior height carries most of a slice's uncertainty. One known failure carries no flag: a body bent 10 degrees sideways on 0.7 x 0.7 x 3 mm voxels reads its end-plate widths 16.5 and 15.8 mm short (all scanimport/README.md) | Repeat measures, or masks against a reader |
| Every spine has 7, 12 and 5 bodies | Yoon 2024 (450 adults aged 17 to 45 with whole-spine CT, counted down from C2 by two readers in consensus): 30 (6.7%) had other than 24 presacral bodies, and 107 (23.8%) had an atypical presacral count or 11 or 13 thoracic or 4 or 6 lumbar bodies, about one in four. Yoon reports transitional vertebrae apart from the count: thoracolumbar in 69 (15.3%), lumbosacral in 119 (26.4%). Paik 2013 (8,280 consecutive patients with lumbar MRI, counted down from C2): 214 (2.6%) had four lumbar bodies and 682 (8.2%) had six. VERIDAH (Moller 2026, Table 1; a random 6,291 NAKO MRIs labelled by one expert): 5.8% had 11 or 13 thoracic bodies and 9.7% had 4 or 6 lumbar; the paper does not say how many had both. The rule has no place for an L6 or a T13, so it never names one right. Nothing makes it abstain on such a column. It answers whenever its top posterior reaches 0.99, as on any spine (bordercall README.md, Statement 3 and Other limits). It answers about as often there as on typical spines: with all 32 measures at three bodies, it names 87.9% of six-lumbar fields and 85.7% of T13 fields at 0.99, against 87.0% of typical fields. With all 32 measures at three, five and nine bodies, 5.1% to 7.2% of its answers at 0.99 on six-lumbar and T13 spines are wrong. Nearly all the wrong answers are fields that hold the L6 or the T13 (bordercall schwingcheck/README.md, What it found, and Spines the rule has no place for). Places for these bodies are proposed, not built (bordercall TODO.md, item 6) | Reader-counted anomalous columns |
| Every place from C3 to L5 equally likely | bordercall says real fields cluster where surgery is done and lumbar fields are commoner (archive/docs/SCHWINGCHECK_LEVELCHECK.md, Limitations 5; README.md, Uniform prior). But its record holds no series of real fields: "no series has given one" (archive/docs/EXACT_IDENTIFICATION_RESULTS.md). The cost is computed, not measured: a name at 0.99 whose rival place is ten times as common in a series is right 90.8%, not 99% (SCHWINGCHECK_LEVELCHECK.md, Statement 5) | A series of real C-arm fields, each with the level it is centred on, such as where surgeons centre the C-arm (TODO.md, item 6) |

## What they cost, by bordercall's own checks

At three bodies unless an item says otherwise, for answers given at 0.99:

- A table re-estimated from twelve people: right 95.5%, not the promised 99.78% (archive record exact-2026-10-26); in the build-up study (bordercall PR 18) 95.75% with the seven measures and 98.05% with all 32.
- The same from 50 people or more: 99.36% or more (PR 18, the data axis). The axis was built ahead of the owner's 2,000 real CTs (bordercall TODO.md, item P step e). It does not show where they would land. Its tables come from people drawn from the generator with one size and no measurement error. So its rows give the cost of estimating a table from that many people, and nothing more (schwingcheck/README.md, the build-up study). The CTs would be measured from SPINEPS masks, which so far give only the seven measures. With the seven, these 200 columns cannot tell whether a table from 50, 200 or 2,000 people keeps the 99% promise. Such a table scores 99.41%, 99.38% and 99.54%, which is 1.1 to 1.6 standard errors above 99% once the variation between tables is counted, and the table as printed scores 99.54% on the same columns. Where a rule built on a table from real CTs would stand beside these rows has not been measured. Nor has whether real spines vary the way the generator's people do.
- 3 mm of measurement error: between 82.6% and 96.85% across the rules (archive/docs/SCHWINGCHECK_LEVELCHECK.md, Limitations 3).
- A table re-estimated from twelve spines, columns drawn with 1.5 mm of error, and each patient's offsets correlated 0.4 between measures, all together: 92.9% (527 wrong of 7,449 over 20 worlds; archive record exact-2026-10-26, robust.combined_worlds; archive/docs/SCHWINGCHECK_LEVELCHECK.md, Limitations 5). The rule in that world still assumes 1 mm of error and no correlation. Limitations 5 calls this "measures correlated 0.4", but the code correlates a patient's offsets, not the measures (archive/bordercall/panjabi.py, synthesize). With rho 0.5, two measures given size then correlate 0.25 x 0.4 = 0.1 before measurement error, and less after it (exact.py, joint_place_loglik). On its own, with the printed table and 1 mm, the same correlation leaves 99.88% right (2 wrong of 1,624; the same record, robust.misspecified at r_between 0.4). So the correlation alone is not what drives the fall.
- Six lumbar bodies or a T13, all 32 measures, at three, five and nine bodies: 5.1% to 7.2% of the answers at 0.99 wrong. Nearly all the wrong answers are fields that include the L6 or the T13, and at nine bodies every one is (TODO.md, rung 0; schwingcheck/README.md, What it found).

Every test of the rule is synthetic. Its columns are drawn by bordercall's own generator from Panjabi's tables (bordercall README.md: "Every number below comes from synthetic columns drawn from Panjabi's tables."). The tests above were built so that the columns do not come from the generator the rule assumes (archive/bordercall/exact.py, part D: "what survives when the generator is not the truth"; schwingcheck/README.md, the build-up study). They change the rule's table, the measurement error, the correlation between measures or the anatomy. The rule's table is re-estimated from 12 to 2,000 simulated people. The error reaches 3 mm, "past what any rule assumes" (exact.py). The measures are drawn correlated, though the rule takes them as independent. The spine has an L6 or a T13, which the rule has no place for. Even these columns come from Panjabi's numbers: the L6 is extrapolated from L4 and L5, and the T13 is a blend of T12 and L1 (schwingcheck/columns.py). The 0.99 promise holds by construction only while the generator is the truth (exact.py, guarantee 3). These tests show how far it falls when that fails. They do not show how the rule does on patients.

The exact rule has not been scored on any real spine. The bordercall README says "No patient has been measured yet", and the white paper says the rule's first real test, on SPINEPS masks of full-spine CTs counted from C2, "has not been run" (archive/docs/SCHWINGCHECK_LEVELCHECK.md, Limitations 1). Of bordercall's own methods, only the archived junction classifier has been scored on real spines. It placed the T12/L1 junction in the 713 of CTSpinoPelvic1K's 802 CTs that show it, against the dataset's own labels (archive/HANDOFF.md). None of the 802 shows C2, so none was checked against a count from C2, and the owner excludes them as truth (archive/HANDOFF.md; TODO.md). The record also reads the vertebra masks of ENHANCE.PET's 1,597 studies, but only to count whole spines and check their labels against the ribs (holdings/gold-tier-3.md). No record shows the exact rule or the junction classifier run on them, and scanimport says "No real scan has been imported yet" (scanimport/README.md).

## Where within-person correlations could come from

What is published relates one person's levels only over part of the column, or in measures other than Panjabi's. Meng 2020 released a C1 to L5 shape model, for non-commercial use, that captures the correlations between vertebrae. It was fitted to 80 VerSe 2019 CTs, none of which shows a whole spine; 57% of its data matrix was missing, the junction of the cervical and thoracic spine was "critically sparse", and it prints no correlation table. Palm 2025 gives mean ratios of body heights between every pair of levels from T1 to L5 in 262 people (TotalSegmentator on CT; per-person data on request). Population studies of vertebral deformity compare each body's posterior height with its neighbours, T4 to L4. One gives ratios to the body above and below at three European centres (J Bone Miner Res 1994;9:1895-1907). EVOS predicts that height from up to four adjacent bodies in 13,562 people (Ismail 1999). Hipp 2022 gives the mean and SD of the absolute differences between adjacent vertebrae in six shape metrics, ratios and angles, on NHANES II films (7,364 lumbar, 9,662 cervical). Pavlova 2017 is one T10 to L5 shape model built from 1,529 DXA images of 1,511 people. A 2026 scoping review counts 42 studies that model several lumbar levels together (Wai 2026).

Those correlations have to be measured in many people, with many levels measured in each person. A whole column counted from C2 gives every pair of levels at once, and it names each level: "Only full-spine CTs from C2 to the pelvis count as truth, because a level's name is a count from C2" (bordercall TODO.md, Rules that hold for every item). bordercall's plan measures the within-person correlation on gold-tier masks, which show C2 to the sacrum in one study (TODO.md, item P step e and item 5). A partial column gives directly only the pairs of levels it holds. A model can fill in the rest, under its own assumptions, as Meng 2020 did.

The source bordercall names for that is one the lab already holds: the census's existing SPINEPS masks of full CTs, each column counted from C2 (bordercall TODO.md, item 5; bordercall-corpus.md). Three things limit it. How many of those masks reach C2 is the first table of the bordercall pilot. The pilot has the owner's word for two CPU jobs but has not been sent, since nothing goes to the grid until he says otherwise (bordercall-corpus.md, What is not known). They are machine masks, and under the owner's tiers machine masks never raise a tier (TODO.md, item 5). And item 5 asks for readers to count a sample of columns from C2, so that the names do not rest on SPINEPS alone.

The owner's own plan is a separate set. In his words of 2026-10-27 at 16:43 UTC: "i am working to get 2000 population samples from real patient annotated CTs, and incorpoate all literature populations similar to panjabi" (TODO.md, item P step e). The project counts at most 1,637 persons, or 1,651 if TotalSegmentator's count is 156, whose whole spine, C2 to the sacrum, carries a person's annotation (ENHANCE.PET, VerSe and TotalSegmentator; gold3-ct/README.md, sections 1 and 2); counting ENHANCE.PET's reader-corrected MOOSE masks as a person's annotation is a choice made on the owner's behalf (What the lab already holds). Whether those masks may feed the table is the owner's call (What waits for the owner, item 2). bordercall's rules use the existing SPINEPS masks and never TotalSegmentator (TODO.md, Rules that hold for every item).

A different Shin paper is among the 42. spine-program's synthetic_spine/sources.csv cites it as Shin 2024, but it was printed in Brain Spine 2025;5:104162. It has the same first and last authors as the paper above. It measures disc space height and foraminal size on CT from L1-L2 to L5-S1 in 700 young patients without spinal pathology, 350 women and 350 men. The level table copies only its middle disc heights.

Griffith 2016 is the largest single series among the 42, but not the only large one. It used CT of 1,080 patients in Hong Kong to measure the lumbar canal from L1 to L5 (synthetic_spine/sources.csv, column n; the paper, p. 672). Eleven more have 200 to 700 people or skeletons each. The three largest are Shin 2025 (CT of 700, lumbar disc height), Pierro 2017 (MRI of 604, lumbar canal depth) and Duman 2026 (CT of 517, body heights from T12 to L2). The collection files Shin 2025 as shin2024, but it is a different paper from the Shin 2024 in the table above. Bonczar 2024 is a meta-analysis. It pools 1,481 people from 18 studies, and Panjabi 1992 is one of them (sources.csv notes). All of these series are lumbar or thoracolumbar, and the collection keeps only their means and spreads by level (data/level_references.csv).

Searched for and not found: a covariance of Panjabi-type measures across the whole column from 200 or more people; a C2 to L5 table of Panjabi's measures from hundreds of people.

One review bears on the first of these, and only weakly. Wai 2026 (Front Bioeng Biotechnol 2026;14:1884249) is a scoping review of lumbar spine shape models: 85 studies presenting 129 models, built from 3 to 2,520 participants. It kept to the lumbar spine. It excluded only models that leave the lumbar spine out, and it says comparing the entire spine in one review would not be useful. Neither its text nor its 46 references cite Meng 2020. Its per-study details are in a supplement not read here. So its silence is no evidence that a whole-spine model or covariance is missing. The nearest found is Meng 2020 (ShapeMI 2020), a shape model of 24 vertebrae, C1 to L5, from 80 VerSe 2019 CTs. That is fewer than 200 people, and it is not in Panjabi's measures.

Not found either: per-level CT morphometry by a segmenter, along the thoracolumbar column, from more people than Palm's 262 (Palm 2025: 262 people, TotalSegmentator, anterior, central and posterior heights T1 to L5). Larger CT series exist, but each covers only part of the column. Shin 2024 measured the lumbar canal and pedicles at L1 to L5 in 1,000 patients aged 18 to 35. Griffith 2016, among the 42, measured the lumbar canal at L1 to L5 in 1,080 patients, with a seed-growing program corrected by hand (Quant Imaging Med Surg 6(6):671-679). Duman 2026, among the 42, had one radiologist measure body heights and pedicles from T11 to L2 in 517 adults (Tomography 12(8):109).

Three near misses were found and set aside. Ma 2024 measured anterior, middle and posterior heights from T1 to L2 with a 3D U-Net on low-dose chest CT, by sex and age (Calcif Tissue Int 115(4):362-372). Its abstract gives no count and its full text did not load, so it may yet be larger than Palm's. Ruhling 2024 measured each body's volume and bone density from T1 to L5 with a neural network, but in only 168 adults, over 1,554 scans and 22,720 vertebrae, most of them oncology patients; it gives volumes, not heights, and its authors say the raw data will be made available (Front Endocrinol 15:1352048). Parenteau 2020 processed CTs of 31,537 adults and 1,951 children semi-automatically, but measured the thoracic column as a whole (T1 to T12 height, curvature depth, kyphosis and canal radius), and its abstract gives no per-vertebra values (SAE 2020-01-0520).

## What more data fixes, and what it does not

More people remove sampling error, not bias. For the measures a mask can take (SPINEPS has no pedicle label; bordercall archive/bordercall/SPINEPS_MAPPING.md), 2,000 whole columns would fix the sampling error of each level's mean and spread. They would also fix two constants now set by hand. One is rho, how much one person's levels move together. The other is the correlation between measures (bordercall TODO.md, section 5).

The other hand-set constants need more than columns. The size spread needs height or another size measure beside each column. The slopes of age, sex and height need those values beside each column (the table above). The census's tier lists have no age, sex or height column; only a note marks TotalSegmentator's children by age (spine-program 06 TIERS.md, sections 1 and 3). The header census that would count these, tools/covariates.py, has no committed result. The measurement error needs repeat measures or masks against a reader.

The columns would give anomaly rates among the people scanned, as the masks count them. Those are not the population's rates. Two mask faults pass as countable one body short: a fused instance, and a sacrum that swallowed the lowest lumbar body (bordercall-corpus.md, item 2). So a rate holds only once readers count a sample from C2 (bordercall TODO.md, section 5). Tier 1 of the census holds four fixed sets and the CTs flagged for a transitional anomaly or a labellers' disagreement (TIERS.md, sections 0 and 2). The pilot scans at most 4,000 rows, tier 1 first. Tier 1 has 4,981 rows with a SPINEPS mask, so every pilot row is tier 1 (tools/bordercall_corpus.py, grid/bordercall.sbatch). A rate from the pilot is a rate in a sample chosen for those flags and sets.

The columns would move the table from dry bone to CT, not to fluoroscopy (below). They would not fix a segmenter's bias, which no record has yet measured on the SPINEPS masks the table would be built from. The posterior-height gap above is not an example of one. It was found on CTSpinoPelvic1K's own labels, whose edge sat where the bone ends, 0.00 mm at every level probed over 40 cases (BODY_HEIGHT_INVESTIGATION.md). Nor would they fix who is scanned (hospital and cancer patients, not surgical candidates), where operations happen (the prior over places), how the same bodies look under a C-arm, how often a machine count is wrong (that needs a reader-counted sample), or columns whose count breaks (fused blocks, hemivertebrae, metal).
