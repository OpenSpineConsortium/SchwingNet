# Flepp's and Killeen's approaches to level naming: where they break, and where SchwingNet is better and worse

Source: issue #140, worked by the thread "SchwingNet textbook and Flepp critiques" on 2026-10-23. It answers the owner's ask of 2026-10-22: "the fundamental flaws in these two researchers' approaches to this problem and how I'm approaching it better and worse". The two researchers are Roman Flepp (github.com/rflepp: arXiv 2506.13292, multi-view X-ray/CT registration by bone substructure contours, IPCAI 2025 and IJCARS) and Benjamin D. Killeen (github.com/benjamindkilleen: arXiv 2403.08059, FluoroSAM and FluoroSeg, and arXiv 2412.08020, the language-promptable digital twin).

## 1. The answer in short

- **Neither group set out to name levels, so the critique is of what their approaches would need in order to.** Flepp's method registers a distal femur for navigation (Published, arXiv 2506.13292v2 Sections 4.1 and 5). FluoroSAM is a promptable segmenter of 128 anatomical classes and tools (Published, arXiv 2403.08059v3 Methods). The digital twin points a robotic C-arm by voice (Published, arXiv 2412.08020 Section 3). The twin paper names no wrong-level problem (Published, by its absence from the paper as read).
- **Flepp's method needs the patient's CT and more. CT registration as such needs only the CT and one radiograph.**
  - Flepp's method needs four things, the second of which can run before the case:
    - the patient's own CT;
    - a per-patient fine-tune on about 800 DRRs, a training run of unknown length (Inferred for its scheduling);
    - a calibration of the views: in the paper, a removable marker of 16 metal spheres on a K-wire in the bone, or a tracking system calibrated before surgery;
    - two views, 45 degrees apart or more in the experiments, with no minimum stated.

    It was shown on five cadaveric distal femurs from five donors and one C-arm (Published; Sections 3.1, 4.1, 4.3 and 5, Appendices A and D).
  - LevelCheck, the registration that does name levels, needs the CT and one intraoperative radiograph, and no wire (Published; Lo 2015, as docs/research/lumbosacral-to-cervical-extrapolation.md section 3 records it). Where a CT exists and the registration holds, it is the accuracy ceiling. It is 100 % at 25.7 s and 99.3 % at 7.1 s, and its failures, at the faster setting, are whole-level shifts (Published; the same row).
  - Against that ceiling SchwingNet competes on having no CT, and on latency once measured, never on accuracy (Inferred). The wire and the second view are Flepp's costs, not the route's, so they count only against Flepp (Published for Flepp's needs; Inferred for the comparison).
  - On a spine, Flepp's contour match runs within one substructure and has no term across levels, and a chain of look-alike bodies is where such a match could settle one level off. Whether the patient's own CT shape prevents that is untested (Inferred).
- **Killeen's route to a level is a text prompt, and the papers show that text alone does not name levels.**
  - On the digital twin's cadaver, Table 1 of arXiv 2412.08020 gives 0.16 to 0.25 Dice for L3, L4 and L5 by name, with centroid errors of 49 to 54 mm. Section 4.1's text gives 0.04 Dice for telling "similar vertebrae levels" apart from one image, without naming its prompts (Published).
  - The authors write that FluoroSAM "is severely limited" without point prompts (Section 5, Published).
  - A point on L4 placed by a person carries the person's count, so the prompt that rescues the segmenter brings the answer the surgeon lacks. A point from a machine would need an upstream step that itself finds an anchor and counts (Inferred).
  - Its level labels are TotalSegmentator's, on 1,621 CTs that v3 takes "from the New Mexico Decedent Image Database" (Published; arXiv 2403.08059v3 Methods). TotalSegmentator has no L6: its vertebra classes run C1 to L5 and the sacrum (Published; TotalSegmentator's README). It shifts labels by one level on lumbarisation, and its junction Dice drops 29 points (Published as the owner's own audit, Schwing et al., NeurIPS 2026 poster, as docs/research/lumbosacral-to-cervical-extrapolation.md section 3 records it; not independently replicated).
  - So FluoroSAM's notion of a level may inherit the count error on transitional spines. In the owner's audit of CTSpinoPelvic1K these are 33 of 802 patients, 18 of them L6, about 4 and 2 %. The size of the effect on a learned model is not measured (Inferred). SchwingNet's own truth leans on the same labeller (section 5).
- **SchwingNet's advantages in the record are the reported stack's. Version 1's are built and not yet shown.**
  - The reported stack names levels from one frame with its own detectors, with no prompt and no CT. Its zero-shot lateral naming is 96.4 % (595/617) on C-ArmLSpine's 296-frame test set, from a mobile C-arm it never saw. Its detection recall is 80.5 % at confidence 0.74, and 93.9 % on the repaired denominator (Measured by the reported SchwingNet; demo-plan.md 0.3 and 8.3).
  - It has measured its own renders against real data on that held-out device. A detector trained on its renders alone reads 0.3 % at confidence 0.74 and 31.7 % at 0.25, against 80.5 % with other devices' real frames plus renders. Its level encoder, whose C-arm appearance came from renders alone beside real plain films, names at the 96.4 % above. The record's own conclusion is that synthetic training transfers for naming, and detection needs real frames of other devices (Measured by the reported SchwingNet; demo-plan.md 0.4 and 8.3). This tests this project's renders, not FluoroSAM's (Inferred for FluoroSAM).
  - Version 1 names the level inside each box it is given. It scores naming per level on person-keyed splits, as correct/n over bodies with a Wilson interval and the person count beside it (Measured as built; tools/evaluate.py, docs/research/evaluation/README.md). Its evaluation boxes are truth boxes, so it gets no text or point prompt but does get gold localisation. "No prompt, end to end" waits on a detector stage (Measured as built; docs/research/v1-demo/README.md).
- **Its weaknesses are confirmed too.**
  - No CT at inference is not a lead over FluoroSAM, which needs none either (Published; arXiv 2403.08059v3 Methods).
  - The latency is measured only on a synthetic dry-run model, and no number yet says anything about SchwingNet on fluoroscopy (Measured; docs/research/latency-and-sweep/README.md).
  - Version 1 has no real result yet (Measured; docs/research/evaluation/README.md). Its test part is five frozen persons, three of them L6, from a device it trains and selects its checkpoint on (Measured; data/splits/test-persons.json, data/splits/rule.json, docs/research/retraining-plan.md section 4).
  - AP naming is 43.7 % (Measured by the reported SchwingNet; demo-plan.md 0.3).
  - No verified real gold exists at any level yet (Measured; benchmarks-to-beat.md section 5). SchwingNet estimates no pose (Measured as built; docs/research/latency-and-sweep/README.md).
- **Six of the issue's starting points are corrected by the records.**
  - FluoroSAM's per-level Dice is 0.16 to 0.25 by name. The 0.04 is for telling similar levels apart (section 3.1).
  - FluoroSAM's merged groups sit beside the single vertebrae, not in place of them (section 3.1).
  - FluoroSAM's real test is one cadaver for spine and fluoroscopy, but v3 also tests on 1,131 real chest X-rays (section 3.1).
  - SchwingNet's latency has been measured on a synthetic model only, and its "no CT" is no lead over FluoroSAM (section 4).
  - The real data come to about 850 cases and 438,000 frames, not "a few pairs". The gap is in level labels (section 5).
  - There is no verified gold below L1 either, not only above it (section 5).
- **What settles the comparison is one measurement on the same frames:** FluoroSAM's released weights through #132 (rung 0 of #131), scored as naming accuracy, beside version 1 (rung 1). Each row names its pairing, so that a model given the bodies' locations is set beside another given them. The rest is section 6.

## 2. Flepp: multi-view X-ray/CT registration by bone substructure contours

### 2.1 What it assumes, and what holds in an operating room naming levels

| Assumption | What the paper says (grade; where) | In the room, naming a spinal level (grade) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The patient's own CT | "In the fine-tuning step, we use specimen-specific CT data, which is typically acquired preoperatively in clinical practice." (Published; Section 3.1) | Often there, but not always: the case SchwingNet is for is the one where only the fluoroscope is (Inferred). A preoperative CT is usually supine and the spine is operated prone, so the CT's alignment of one body to the next is not the room's (Inferred). Even so, LevelCheck registers a preoperative CT to one intraoperative radiograph and names levels at 100 % (Published; Lo 2015, lumbosacral-to-cervical-extrapolation.md section 3), so that change does not by itself stop registration from naming levels. SchwingNet's own real test set is not the CT-free case. Every C-ArmLSpine case carries an intraoperative CBCT of the same device (Measured; data/sources/carmlspine.json). The reported stack never reads it at inference, while version 1's evaluation boxes and every level name come from it. |
| A network fine-tuned per patient | nnU-Net pretrained on DRRs of 15 knee CTs, then fine-tuned on about 800 DRRs per specimen, from -90 to +90 degrees, for 300 epochs. Inference uses about 18 GB of GPU memory. No time is reported for the fine-tune or the registration. (Published; Section 3.1, Appendix F) | A training run per patient of unknown length, which can run before the case from the preoperative CT (Inferred). What the room pays is the registration time, which the paper does not report (Published, by its absence). |
| A calibration of the views | The images "are calibrated using a custom-made fiducial marker": a radiolucent 3D-printed marker with 16 metal spheres, "mounted on a K-wire attached to the femur", found by a PnP search. "After the X-ray images are acquired, the marker can be removed." The method can be extended to optical or electromagnetic tracking "calibrated with the phantom prior to the surgery". (Published; Section 3, Appendix A, the abstract) | The marker gives the geometry of the views, not the anatomy, and its level is never used. On a spine it could sit on any rigidly attached pin, as a navigation reference array does. It adds hardware and invasiveness but no circularity about the level (Inferred). Tracking in its place is the patient array that spine navigation already uses (Inferred). |
| Two views far apart | Each registration in the experiments used "two views with a perspective change of at least 45 degrees", and no minimum is stated as a requirement. Single-view registration, after prior work, "is significantly less reliable". In its own single-view test on one specimen, no run reached a mean reprojection distance under 1 mm. (Published; Section 4.3, Appendix C.2) | Two exposures and a C-arm turn per check (Inferred). The single-view failure is at a sub-millimetre threshold on a symmetric femur. A level needs centimetre accuracy, and LevelCheck names levels from one radiograph. So whether one view would do for a level is untested by Flepp and shown possible by LevelCheck (Inferred). |
| A rigid bone cut into substructures | The femur is divided into diaphysis and two condyles and matched within a substructure only, which the authors call "an ad hoc solution tailored to the femur" (Published; Sections 3 and 5) | A spine is a chain of near-identical substructures. Flepp's cost matches contours within one substructure and has no term across levels. With the patient's own CT, the cues to the level are three: the count from an end in view, each vertebra's own shape, and the spacing and curvature between bodies, which change from supine to prone. Whether the last two stop a contour match settling one level off is untested (Inferred). LevelCheck, a registration of the patient's CT to one radiograph, names levels at 100 % at 25.7 s, and its failures at the faster setting are whole-level shifts (Published; Lo 2015, lumbosacral-to-cervical-extrapolation.md section 3). That shows the slip is a real failure mode of registration (Inferred). |
| One device, five specimens | "the testing of our method was conducted using data from a single C-arm device". Five cadaveric distal femurs come from five donors, with ten views each. A humerus was tried on synthetic X-rays from a patient CT only (Published; Sections 4.1 and 5, Appendices C.6 and D) | Device and population spread untested (Published). |

### 2.2 What it does that SchwingNet cannot

- Sub-millimetre pose of a bone from two real X-rays (Published; Section 4.3):
  - mean reprojection distance 0.67 mm, against 5.35 mm for the commercial method and 4.03 mm for Zhang's;
  - 96.1 % of runs at 1 mm or under;
  - 92 % from random starts within plus or minus 50 mm and 180 degrees, and 100 % with the restart (Appendix C.3).
- SchwingNet estimates no pose at all (Measured as built: the level stage outputs a level per box; docs/research/latency-and-sweep/README.md).
- A pose projects the CT's labels onto every pixel of the frame. That is the supervised 3D-to-2D prior of #127 step 5. So the method is a tool SchwingNet could use, not a competitor at naming (Inferred).
- Its use here is limited. The code is "available for non-commercial research purposes upon reasonable request", under a pending patent EP25168706.7 (Published; the repository's README). Its data are a femur set (Zenodo 15753063, benchmarks-to-beat.md section 1). #127 therefore takes DiffDRR first.

### 2.3 The fundamental flaw, for this problem

The method answers "where is this bone, given its CT". SchwingNet's question is "which bone is this, given only the frame". Turning the first into the second with Flepp's method needs four things the room does not reliably give:

- the CT;
- a calibration of the views, by a fiducial or by tracking;
- two views;
- an in-room registration time the paper never reports, beside a per-patient fine-tune that can run before the case (Published for the absence of any time; Inferred for the scheduling).

It also needs a match that cannot slip a level, which the paper does not test (Inferred). CT registration for level naming in general needs less: LevelCheck uses the CT and one radiograph (Published). Where a CT is present and the registration holds, CT registration is the accuracy ceiling (LevelCheck 100 % at 25.7 s and 99.3 % at 7.1 s, Published). SchwingNet should claim no accuracy above it. Its claim is two answers in one frame interval (Inferred, until measured as section 6 says):

- against LevelCheck, the same answer without the CT;
- against Flepp only, the same answer without the calibration and the second view.

What renders carry, in the record, since the first row's question cuts both ways:

### 3.2 What they do that SchwingNet cannot

- **Open vocabulary.** Masks for 128 anatomical classes and 464 tool models from text, points or a mask (Published; v3 Methods). The vertebrae as one class score 0.78 Dice from text alone on the twin's cadaver (Published; arXiv 2412.08020 Section 4.1 and Appendix A Table 1). SchwingNet segments nothing and names only levels (Measured as built).
- **Scale.** About 2.95 million renders of 1,621 CTs, from volumetric and mesh-based rendering built on DeepDRR (Published; v3 Methods, benchmarks-to-beat.md section 3).
- **3D localisation and control.** A sparse twin from 2 to 5 views, and voice control of collimation and viewfinding: 83.54 % end-to-end success over 158 prompts (Published; benchmarks-to-beat.md section 1). SchwingNet localises in no third dimension and drives no device (Measured as built).
- **A second real domain.** Zero-shot whole-lung segmentation on 1,131 real chest X-rays at 0.66 Dice from text alone, above MedSAM's 0.48 with a box, though MedSAM trained on that task (Published; v3 Table 1). SchwingNet has no result outside the spine (Measured as built).
- **Released code, weights and data.** FluoroSeg under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, the weights at 2.5 GB, and the twin's cadaver archive (Published; benchmarks-to-beat.md sections 1 and 6).

### 3.3 The fundamental flaw, for this problem

FluoroSAM, as the twin uses it, treats a level as a segmentation class to be found by its appearance and named by a text prompt. The papers' own numbers show that appearance in one frame does not tell one level from the next (Published, arXiv 2412.08020):

- 0.16 to 0.25 Dice and 49 to 54 mm centroid error for L3, L4 and L5 by name (Table 1);
- 0.04 for similar levels told apart (Section 4.1's text).

A level is a position in a count from an end. The count needs an anchor in view or context outside the frame, and its truth breaks on transitional anatomy. FluoroSAM has no count, no anchor, and a label source that breaks there (Inferred from the Published figures above). Prompts patch the gap in one of two ways (Inferred):

- by a person's point, which carries the person's count;
- by an upstream step that would itself have to find the anchor and count.

The strongest reply is this. FluoroSAM's one-class column mask, 0.78 Dice from text alone, could be split into bodies and counted from its sacrum mask, which would name levels with no person's prompt. That is the reported stack's route, and its count from the detected sacrum is 97.0 % (demo-plan.md 0.3, A4.7). Here, though, the anchor would be FluoroSAM's weak sacrum row, 0.35 Dice from text alone (Table 1), and the counting step is not FluoroSAM's. Neither paper tries it (Published for the Dice values; Inferred for the rest). Rung 0 of #131 already scores FluoroSAM this way (benchmarks-to-beat.md 4.2), and section 6 keeps it as an arm of its own.

## 7. What this means for the talk

- What it can claim once measured: level naming on single real C-arm frames by a model with its own detector, with no CT, no prompt and no fiducial, per level with intervals and latency. That claim is set against FluoroSAM scored on the same frames (#132), not against a Dice from another cadaver (Inferred, the claim's shape).
- What it cannot claim:
  - more accuracy than CT registration (LevelCheck, Published);
  - being first to learn naming from renders (Huang 2022, Published);
  - any angle, before the per-angle curve;
  - real time, before version 1's p95, with its detector, is under 97 ms;
  - for version 1, a test on an unseen device, since its test persons are of a device it trained on (section 5).
- The 0.04 is a Dice, not an accuracy. It is quoted as Section 4.1's text, beside Table 1's 0.16 to 0.25 for single levels by name, and it is not a row of Table 1 (benchmarks-to-beat.md section 1).
- The case against TotalSegmentator's count rests in part on the owner's own audit. A slide cites it as such (Schwing et al. 2026), beside TotalSegmentator's own README for the missing L6.
- Nothing of this note goes on a slide before the provisional is filed (docs/project.md).
