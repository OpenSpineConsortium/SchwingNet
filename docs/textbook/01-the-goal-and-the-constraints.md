# 01 The goal and the constraints

Label: schwingnet-internal. This chapter gives SchwingNet's goal, its results, its rules and its dates but none of its mechanism, which Part III holds; like the whole book it is confidential until the provisional is filed and stays in this private repository.

This chapter settles what every later chapter takes as given: what SchwingNet is for and for whom, and what done means on the record's two horizons, the CNS talk of 2 November 2026 and the validation on a hospital's own fluoroscopy planned for 2027. It sets out why the project treats wrong-level spine surgery as a counting problem and how far the registry, survey and cost figures carry that framing; names the two lines of work, counting from an anchor and naming from morphology, by what each has achieved; and lists the constraints, the calendar and what drops first, so that a planner knows what may be run, what may be said and what is not yet measured.

## What SchwingNet is for

### One question

The handoff of 8 October 2026 puts the programme in one sentence: can a vertebral level be named from what a surgeon already has in the room, an operating-room C-arm exposure or a plain film, without counting down from C2 and without a registered preoperative scan ([Handoff: SchwingNet, CTSpinoPelvic1K and the imaging programme](../../artifacts/handoff-schwingnet-ctspinopelvic1k-and-the-imaging-programme-rbcdmf/)). Three projects answer it from different sides: CTSpinoPelvic1K, the public CT release; OpenSpineToolkit, the public measurement library; and SchwingNet, the level-identification programme, whose code the handoff says is never pushed public. The next SchwingNet is built in this repository, while the reported SchwingNet and its numbers live in spine-program's folder `01_carm_level_detection` ([docs/project.md](../../docs/project.md)).

The words "what a surgeon already has in the room" decide whom the work is for. The [Level Counting Visual Abstract](../../artifacts/level-counting-visual-abstract/), written for a surgeon and a hospital's finance officer, says the method is "for spine surgeons at the hospitals that will never buy a navigation system", on the C-arm the hospital already owns. The [AIDaS Seed Grant Proposal](../../artifacts/aidas-seed-grant-proposal/) says it needs no capital equipment, no preoperative CT and no tracker, "so it can reach community and rural operating rooms that do not have a navigation suite". The device the work must succeed on is therefore the hospital's own, which is why the case for the operating-room study asks for the hospital's own images.

### The goal as it now stands

On 20 October 2026 the owner restated the goal, and [docs/project.md](../../docs/project.md) carries it: "low-latency, high-accuracy vertebral classification on fluoroscopic video or images", built as a workflow that grows between 20 October and the talk, "so that every number the talk shows is reproduced by a command from a record in this repository". The [retraining plan](../../docs/research/retraining-plan.md), version 2, written from the owner's words that day, makes it concrete: given a frame or a stream of frames, name each visible vertebra from C1 to the sacrum within the frame budget, and keep naming them as the C-arm moves.

The same words changed what counts as progress. The owner wants no comparison with a poorly done baseline, but an extensible workflow never rerun from scratch when a dataset is added, whose well-done version counts as the first version. So the reported SchwingNet, which the repository's notes call the reported stack, is context and not a baseline: it holds the record's only naming numbers on real intraoperative fluoroscopy, and no paired comparison with it is a deliverable. Version 1 is the first model the new workflow trains to work, and every later version is measured against it on the same frozen test persons, in one table that grows by a row per version. The plan's working rules bind every experiment: nothing is run from scratch when data is added, one model per stage and one seed come before any study of seeds, folds or ensembles, and evaluation is drawn on the same real frames from the first checkpoint onward. [CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md) records the goal and these rules as the owner's decision of 20 October.

## What done looks like

### By the talk

[docs/project.md](../../docs/project.md) lists four things, and the retraining plan's list of five makes the latency an item of its own. First, a dataset is added by one pull request and one incremental run, and no epoch already run is run again. Second, version 1 of SchwingNet exists, one model per stage trained to work on the registry as it stands, with its card. Third, an evaluation page draws fixed real frames, the witness frames, at every checkpoint, and tables every version with intervals and its latency. Fourth, the recorded C-arm sweep with the overlay and the measured latency is ready to show once the provisional is filed. The retraining plan says the first three do not fall off, and names what falls off first (What drops first, below); [chapter 18](18-metrics-intervals-and-witness-frames.md) holds the metrics and the witness frames, and [chapter 12](12-detectors-the-architecture-and-the-version-1-training-plan.md) the version 1 plan.

The evaluation page is for members only: project.md and the retraining plan put it on the repository's docs site, but the later memory says it is read on GitHub and never put on Pages, since Pages on a private repository may be public on this plan, and the later word holds ([docs/memory/evaluation-and-wandb.md](../../docs/memory/evaluation-and-wandb.md)). And the talk shows a recording, not a live run, because no laptop latency exists and the CPU path took seconds per frame. The frame budget is the sweep's own rate, about 10.3 frames a second, so 97 ms per frame, with real-time meaning the 95th percentile strictly under it ([docs/memory/latency-and-the-recorded-sweep.md](../../docs/memory/latency-and-the-recorded-sweep.md)). No machine of a surgeon's room has been measured, and the reported stack's only CPU figure is 3,350 ms a frame.

### Beyond the talk

The second horizon is the case for the operating-room study, whose canonical pages are [Counting Levels Under Fluoroscopy](../../artifacts/counting-levels-under-fluoroscopy/), written in September 2026 for a spine surgeon of the department, the [AIDaS Seed Grant Proposal](../../artifacts/aidas-seed-grant-proposal/) of October 2026 and the [Retrospective Fluoroscopy Protocol](../../artifacts/retrospective-fluoroscopy-protocol/), the later draft of the first IRB protocol ([the clusters](../../artifacts/_synthesis/clusters.md)). There, done is a guarantee of a particular shape: "not a fixed accuracy" but "a measured bound on wrong-level output at a stated decline rate, on held-out cases, re-measured whenever the device, protocol or population changes", where a decline costs a repeat exposure and not a wrong level. It is a design promise with no outcome measured yet. To earn it the proposal asks for every saved fluoroscopic series of the spine cases of 2021 to 2026, the operative notes, with post-operative imaging where it exists, as the level truth, ten to twenty hours of a resident's annotation and one session on the hospital's C-arm.

The grant's artifact turns that into a year, $20,000 for 1 January to 31 December 2027: a new IRB protocol and phantom work in the first quarter; cadaver validation and a working prototype in the second; in the third, a shadow-mode observational study under a second protocol, in which the system displays nothing to the surgeon; and an FDA pre-submission and an NIH NIBIB R21 in the fourth. The grant says plainly that FDA clearance is not achievable within 2027 and that public release of the corpus is not a deliverable. The artifact is a draft whose PI slot is a placeholder, since the call requires a tenure-track or tenured faculty member of AIDaS as PI and the owner, a resident, is not eligible. No artifact records whether the proposal went in by the deadline of 16 October 2026, 11:59 pm ET, so [the OR-study contradictions](../../artifacts/_synthesis/contradictions/the-case-for-the-or-study-proposal-seed-grant-and-irb-protoc.md) could not tell (row 57); but the repository's research index records that a packet revised after the draft went to the AIDaS office on 16 October, before the deadline ([docs/research/README.md](../../docs/research/README.md)), its PI and co-PI, both affiliated with AIDaS, having approved its final PDF, as the checkpoints of 14 and 16 October record. On this horizon [Protocol 1 - eProtocol draft](../../artifacts/protocol-1-eprotocol-draft-mkmh7p/) of 15 September 2026, the first draft of the same protocol, is superseded. The later draft narrows the collection to one neurosurgeon's archived C-arm images and cine loops of 2021 to 2026, asks for Expedited Category 5 rather than Exempt Category 4 because the data may support an FDA 510(k) pre-submission, and moves the shadow-mode study to a protocol of its own. The older draft alone still holds its wider first design, an Exempt Category 4 study of about 40,000 imaging studies from 2006 onward with aims on CT, intraoperative images and fractures (hypotheses H1 to H5) not carried forward, and a reading of the registry the later pages do not keep (next section).

## Why wrong-level surgery, and why a count

### Rare per operation, common per career

The visual abstract's summary, rare per operation and common per career, is borne out by the figures the grant and the protocol cite. In a registry of 34,544 degenerative spine operations at 27 hospitals from 2012 to 2022, the wrong level was operated in 0.12 percent (95% CI 0.09 to 0.16), about 1 in 830: 0.42 percent in the thoracic spine (CI 0.16 to 1.08, about 1 in 240), 0.13 percent in the lumbar spine and 0.05 percent in the cervical (Furuya 2026). By self-report the rate is lower, 418 events in 1.3 million procedures or 1 in 3,110, yet half of 415 surveyed surgeons had performed one (Mody 2008), as had almost half of 569 respondents to the AANS/CNS Spine Section survey (Groff 2013). An event too rare to show in one hospital's year is one that about half of spine surgeons meet in a career.

Three survey figures are easily run together, and [the OR-study contradictions](../../artifacts/_synthesis/contradictions/the-case-for-the-or-study-proposal-seed-grant-and-irb-protoc.md) keep each with its survey (row 11): 15 percent of surgeons had prepared the wrong level and caught it before incision (2008, per surgeon); 17 percent of the 418 events led to legal action (2008, per event); and nearly 20 percent of the 569 respondents had been sued for one (2013, per surgeon), which is the visual abstract's "nearly one in five".

### Why the project calls it a counting error

Four strands make wrong-level surgery a counting problem in these pages, and they are of unequal strength. The first is the authors' premise, stated in the grant without a source: a C-arm shows a few vertebrae at a time, so the surgeon counts from whatever landmark is visible. The record confirms it is asserted, not cited, with the registry's cause figure as the nearest support ([the level-naming contradictions](../../artifacts/_synthesis/contradictions/level-naming-and-counting.md), row 69). The second is that figure: the grant and the later protocol report, citing the registry paper without quoting its categories, that intraoperative disorientation was the cause in 81.3 percent. The visual abstract and the September proposal round it to 81 percent and read it as a count that went wrong under fluoroscopy, while the superseded eProtocol draft says that image misinterpretation was named in 18.8 percent. The two shares sum to about 100 percent, which suggests separate categories, so whether a miscount under fluoroscopy falls in the first or the second turns on the paper's definitions; the OR-study contradictions cannot tell, and carry the figure in the grant's words until those are read (row 8).

The third strand is better grounded: the safeguards in use are themselves counts. Site-verification protocols failed to prevent about one-third of cases in the one study that assessed them (Longo 2012); the recommended check, imaging after exposure against a marked fixed structure, is another count (DeVine 2010); the standard intraoperative technique counts upward from the sacrum on lateral fluoroscopy; and surgeons disagree on the thoracic landmark, 67 percent using the pedicle and 49 percent the spinous process (Strong 2021; Mayer 2014). The fourth strand is cost: every way of not counting costs time, dose or capital, as the next section shows. The framing therefore rests firmly on the safeguards and the cost and only partly on the registry, and a planner should not present the 81.3 percent as a measured share of miscounts.

The superseded draft departs from the later pages on two more details, and a planner should repeat neither. It calls the source "a recent single-system series" where the grant and the later protocol call it a registry at 27 hospitals; one health system can run 27 hospitals, so the contradictions page cannot tell and carries the grant's fuller description until the paper's methods are read (row 10). And it says every event was at L4-5 or L5-S1 in the sentence that gives a thoracic rate of 0.42 percent; the page confirms that both cannot hold, so the regional rates stand and that sentence is dropped (row 9).

### What it costs, and what can be priced

The harm is a never event, and its costs are only partly priced. Of 68 litigated wrong-disc-level cases, 81 percent (55 of 68) ended in a payment, and surgeons won 42 percent of jury trials (Goodkin 2004). The median National Practitioner Data Bank payment for a surgical never event is $75,220 (IQR $28,847 to $215,003, in 2025 dollars; Bettis 2026), and the mean paid claim is about $133k to $136k (Mehtsun 2013; Tan 2023). In the NHS, 978 spinal claims over five years were valued at GBP 535.5M, 26 of them never events (Machin 2018). The grant and the protocol add the Joint Commission's 127 wrong surgeries in 2024, nine wrong-level and seven spinal, with severe permanent harm in 36 percent, and the protocol NHS England's 185 wrong-site never events in 2024/25, but both name only the organisation, with no report title (row 43); the visual abstract's references name Joint Commission Sentinel Event Data 2024 for the counts and nothing for the 36 percent, so the counts are carried as reported and the 36 percent and the NHS figure as unverified.

Avoiding the count costs time and dose. One centre placed 179 preoperative gold fiducials over 38 months, 176 of them thoracic, at a mean of 51.9 minutes and 183 mGy each (Siminski 2023); at $33.78 to $37.45 per operating-room minute (FY2014, before physician fees; Childers 2018) that is $1,750 to $1,950 per placement. That figure is the operating-room time of the placement visit and not a price for the marker, although the visual abstract's wording reads as if it were (row 14). Thoracic localization takes about 15 minutes by needle and count and about 3 with a pre-placed fiducial, in two small series (Mongardi 2021, 19 against 11 patients; Upadhyaya 2012, 26 against 26).

The visual abstract builds a per-hospital model from these inputs. It assumes ten operating-room minutes saved per case, priced at the 2014 cost multiplied by 1.35 to reach 2026 dollars: $140k to $150k a year at 300 spine cases, $275k to $300k at 600 and $460k to $500k at 1,000, set against a navigation suite amortised at $360k a year. The expected cost of the events themselves is small, about $8k, $17k and $28k a year at those volumes, though a single event costs the indemnity range above. Three inputs carry the model and none is firm: the ten minutes rest on the two small thoracic series and are applied to every case, which the abstract itself calls "the weakest input here"; the factor of 1.35 has no source beyond the abstract's own aside that 2014 costs are "30-40% more today", whose midpoint it is (row 13); and the navigation figure, $1.5M of capital over seven years plus 10 percent a year in service, is labelled an assumption. Without the factor the minutes are worth about $101k to $112k a year at 300 cases and $338k to $375k at 1,000, in 2014 dollars, so whether they exceed the assumed $360k at 1,000 cases turns on the unsourced factor and the ten-minute assumption. The abstract's own conclusion is the one to carry: "the dollars are operating-room minutes; the never event is a harm and a reputation, not a line item."

Two absences recur across the abstract, the grant and the protocol: no verified capital cost for a navigation suite, and no published study showing that navigation reduces wrong-level surgery, with no measure of surgeon workload and no figure for navigation's reach by site of care. None of the pages describes the search behind those absences, so the book carries them as not found by the authors (row 44).

## The two lines of work, and where each stands

The record holds two answers to the programme's question, and a planner must know which one an experiment serves. [Chapter 16](16-method-decisions-and-what-would-reverse-them.md) holds how the two coexist.

### Counting from an anchor

The first line reports a level as a count from a trusted landmark, the sacrum or C2, and never as a name: in the September proposal's words it asserts "four from the sacrum", not "L4", so a transitional spine is counted the same way it is operated on. It is the method the case for the operating-room study validates: an internal summary of 18 September, [Vertebral Levels Without CT](../../artifacts/vertebral-levels-without-ct-a4rydj/), had described a naming design that never counts, and the OR-study contradictions treat that page as an earlier, naming-based design and carry count, not name, as the method under validation (row 36). [Chapter 10](10-counting-from-an-anchor-along-a-sweep.md) holds the counting line.

Its results come from a manuscript in preparation and are reported, not measured here. Tested apart from the rest of the system, the count was exact on 99.17 to 100 percent of the counts it completed, in twelve arms of 16 to 801 spines. From C2 it was exact on 834 of 834 on 76 whole-spine studies, and on 1,205 of 1,209 on 262 real lateral radiographs (99.67 percent; 95% CI 99.16 to 99.91); from the sacrum on 6,234 of 6,255 (99.66 percent; CI 99.49 to 99.79 in the later protocol) on 801 abdominopelvic spines, and on 7,168 of 7,228 (99.17 percent) on 522 spines from a second institution. The confirming exposure was assigned to the right vertebra in 99.31 percent of 5,385 confirmations on the 801 spines and 98.57 percent of 6,343 on 501 whole-spine studies. Exactness is regional, and each figure belongs to its arm: on the 801 spines 99.73 percent lumbar and 95.45 percent thoracic, where the registry's wrong-level rate is highest; on the 522, the one arm that reaches the cervical spine, 99.33 percent lumbar, 97.41 percent thoracic and 83.84 percent cervical. The September proposal tables five of these arms and splices the two arms' regional figures (row 7); the grant's fuller version is the one carried (row 52). Both pages conclude that the count is not where the risk lies: what remains unmeasured is how the whole system performs on the hospital's own machines and patients, the one number that, in the visual abstract's words, none of this can predict.

### Naming from morphology

The second line names each visible vertebra from the image itself, which is what the goal of 20 October asks of version 1. All its numbers on real fluoroscopy come from the reported stack, on C-ArmLSpine, a public set of 149 lumbar intraoperative cases from one mobile C-arm, which the zero-shot arm quoted here never trained on ([SchwingNet Data Registry](../../artifacts/schwingnet-data-registry/)). On lateral frames of that device the reported stack named 595 of 617 correctly, 96.4 percent [92.3, 98.4], as [docs/research/demo-plan.md](../../docs/research/demo-plan.md) tables it from spine-program's `01/STATUS.md` (A2.1); the note does not name the interval's method, and the interval is wider than a Wilson interval over bodies ([docs/research/flepp-and-killeen-critique.md](../../docs/research/flepp-and-killeen-critique.md)). The [Any-View Spine Plan](../../artifacts/any-view-spine-plan/) gives the same figure, and the programme contradictions carry it in place of an earlier September one (row 12), as does [the group ruling](../../artifacts/_synthesis/contradictions.md#every-confirmed-number-collision-read-again). On AP frames it named 276 of 631, 43.7 percent, with no interval in the note, an error the record describes as a one-level cranial shift; the concept ledger marks 43.7 percent current ([Row 107](../../artifacts/_synthesis/concepts.md#row-107-zero-shot-ap-exact-level-accuracy-on-c-armlspine)). With one body in view the handoff gives 74.8 percent, which the programme contradictions (row 10) and the group ruling carry in place of an earlier figure, though the source gives neither its n nor an interval. A whole-lateral-frame figure also exists, but the record has not settled what it measures, so it is not quoted here. Within this line the clusters mark Vertebral Levels Without CT superseded by [Naming Vertebrae Without CT](../../artifacts/naming-vertebrae-without-ct-hjy4vc/) of the same day, which reports the same chain with its measurements; the older page alone holds the chain's state before them. [Chapter 11](11-naming-from-morphology-context-and-a-sweep.md) holds the naming line and [chapter 14](14-transitional-anatomy-two-anchors-disagreement-and-abstention.md) its transitional cases.

### What neither line has yet

Besides the unmeasured latency, two gaps bound everything above. No number in the record comes from the new workflow on a real frame: version 1 has run only on a synthetic bank, which, in the evaluation page's words, measures the workflow, not SchwingNet ([docs/research/evaluation/README.md](../../docs/research/evaluation/README.md)). No number exists on a transitional spine read on a real frame: the [research directions](../../artifacts/research-directions-from-the-data-holding-kcyckb/) of 8 October count none verified of the 35 at-risk persons, each named without error, that a Wilson lower bound of 90 percent needs.

## The constraints every experiment runs under

### Confidentiality until the provisional

The invention disclosure went to the university's technology commercialization office on 25 September 2026; the provisional is planned for 30 October and is not recorded as filed, as the programme contradictions say to carry it (row 60). Until it is filed, SchwingNet is confidential. [docs/project.md](../../docs/project.md) puts anything public out of scope: no push to a public repository, no preprint and no slide of the method. The owner's answer of 20 October, recorded in [the demo plan](../../docs/research/demo-plan.md), sets the order: the provisional will be filed by 30 October, the demo is made in private and shown only after the filing, and nothing of the method goes on a slide.

The handoff's older and stricter rules still bind anything that leaves: never the banned word for where data came from, whole-number versions only, captions of at most 60 words, every printed number entering through a manifest key and never typed, never stating the commercial goal or naming the product, never re-adding level-identification text to a public repository, the affiliation given as General Surgery (the owner's current department; the grant and the later protocol name an older one), and a disclosure audit before any talk or submission. The [spine-program student onboarding](../../artifacts/spine-program-student-onboarding/) adds that nothing from the private repositories is quoted in a talk or upload without the owner's word. Dataset request letters go out only on the owner's word, and no external text says or implies that a model, a neural network, machine learning, AI or training is built or used ([CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md)). Paper 1 was complete on 8 October and held pending the technology-transfer office.

The handoff of 8 October allowed only the public v12 deck at the CNS talk, and the demo plan added that the demo needs the provisional filed, or counsel's written OK, before 2 November; the owner's answer above is the later word. If the filing slips past the talk, the demo stays private and the v12 rule stands; that is this chapter's reading of the records, not a recorded decision.

### The owner's word and the number

No grid job, Claude run or training starts without the owner's word and the number of jobs ([docs/project.md](../../docs/project.md); [CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md)), and the retraining plan applies it step by step, nothing submitted before that step's word and number. The rule has been tightened, not relaxed. A note quoting the owner on 21 October as saying grid jobs need no number was superseded, and the project instructions sessions received on 23 October added fetches to the rule ([docs/research/state-of-knowledge.md](../../docs/research/state-of-knowledge.md), section 3, item 3). The synthesis reads that keep this book's record current hold a standing word and number, the owner's choice of 23 October: ten runs a day unless a variable says otherwise, fifty at most.

Three data rules belong with it. CT-RATE, at 21.3 TB, is never submitted without the owner's own number of terabytes, and the grid pipeline records it switched off by the owner's decision of 21 October. The RSNA RATIC download, whose link expires on 28 October at 02:38 UTC, is the owner's own fetch, since the link is the owner's and no gate word may carry it ([SchwingNet grid pipeline](../../artifacts/schwingnet-grid-pipeline/)). And every fetch reads its exact size from its source before a byte lands and is refused whole if it would overflow the volume's quota, by the owner's rule of 23 October ([docs/memory/the-size-rule.md](../../docs/memory/the-size-rule.md); [chapter 22](22-quotas-cost-and-the-budget-of-a-run.md)). A schedule is therefore a list of targets, each waiting on a word the owner has not yet given.

### No usage credits and no API key

Usage credits are never used. Claude runs on subscription tokens and never on an API key, and nothing in the repository holds a credential; the one Claude credential the project keeps, by the owner's choice of 23 October, is a subscription token for the synthesis reads, held as a secret and never in a file ([docs/project.md](../../docs/project.md); [docs/memory/how-this-repository-works.md](../../docs/memory/how-this-repository-works.md)). The demo plan adds that no Claude token run, API key or usage credit enters the demo's work, and that its grid jobs run under the owner's own allocation.

### The private-forever list

Some things stay private after the filing too. [The Lab Recipe](../../artifacts/the-lab-recipe-1t2gvp/) names them: level-identification code, FluoroCap, model weights, `spinesurg-ct-nnunet` and XRSpinoPelvic1K, never in a public repository and never on the website. ### The IRB umbrella

The programme's public data sit under one determination of Not Human Participant Research, HPR 2026-269 of 2 September 2026. The Lab Recipe gives its condition: a dataset is under it only if all five criteria hold, namely that it is public and de-identified by the source, downloadable without application, permission or data-use agreement, not restricted-use, carries no re-identification key, and is the subject of no linkage attempt; anything else needs its own filing before use. VinDr-SpineXR, held under PhysioNet's credentialed agreement, is covered by the supplement HPR 2026-330, whose notice came on 29 September 2026, so the registry page's line calling it outside the determination is stale ([docs/memory/vindr-is-covered-by-hpr-2026-330.md](../../docs/memory/vindr-is-covered-by-hpr-2026-330.md)). The owner recorded on 14 October that permission and the data use agreements are in hand for all the data. The hospital's own fluoroscopy is the new case: the grant says the operating-room study needs a new protocol, and the later protocol asks for Expedited Category 5. [Chapter 9](09-splits-leakage-and-licences.md) and [chapter 24](24-decision-log-data-licences-and-splits.md) hold the licences and the decisions.

### The evidence rules

Five rules govern what a result counts as: four from the handoff and the onboarding page, one from the goal. Every number, date and path names the file it came from, and what no file records is written as not recorded, never guessed. Held out means the person was never seen by the checkpoint or by any ancestor it was initialised from, the owner's rule of 23 September; a derived image inherits the person of its source, and unknown is not clean. A study is pre-registered, then scored. Every number the talk shows is reproduced by a command from a record in this repository. And the numbers of the families the handoff marks leaked are records only, never results; [chapter 3](03-what-is-measured-and-what-is-claimed.md) and [chapter 9](09-splits-leakage-and-licences.md) name them. They are why the counting results are graded reported here.

### Two tracks, and the grid's own rules

The Lab Recipe keeps students, the grid and non-commercial data as the research track, from which nothing moves to a commercial one ([chapter 23](23-who-may-do-what.md)). And the grid has rules of its own, among them that nothing computes on the login node and that an agent which submits a job writes down its estimate and stops ([chapter 21](21-the-grid-how-a-run-starts-waits-and-finishes.md)).

## What we know

SchwingNet exists to name or count the vertebral level on the C-arm a hospital already owns, for surgeons without navigation. Since 20 October its goal is low-latency, high-accuracy classification of each visible vertebra on fluoroscopic video or images, built as an extensible workflow whose first well-trained model, version 1, is the reference for what follows. Done by the talk is an incremental dataset path, version 1 with its card, a members-only evaluation page and the recorded sweep with its latency, shown only after the filing.

Wrong-level surgery is rare per operation, 0.12 percent of 34,544 registry operations and 0.42 percent in the thoracic spine, and common per career; the safeguards in use are counts, and avoiding a count costs time, dose or capital. What can be priced is operating-room minutes, not the never event, and only on assumed or unsourced inputs.

The count, tested apart from the rest of the system, is reported exact on 99.17 to 100 percent of completed counts in twelve arms, less so in the thoracic and cervical spine. Naming by the reported stack on lateral frames of a C-arm its zero-shot arm never trained on is measured at 96.4 percent [92.3, 98.4]; on AP frames it is 43.7 percent, which the ledger marks current. The AIDaS proposal, revised from the draft the book reads, went in on 16 October.

Every experiment runs under the same rules: nothing public before the provisional, results and not methods at the talk, nothing run or fetched without the owner's word and the number, no usage credits or API key, the private-forever list, the IRB umbrella with a new protocol for the hospital's images, and every number naming its file.

## What we do not know

We do not know whether the provisional will be filed by 30 October; the record has it planned, not filed, and the demo depends on it. Nor do we know how the system performs on a hospital's own machines and patients, the deciding number: there is no version 1 number on a real frame, no latency on any operating-room machine and no verified transitional test person on a real frame.

We do not know how the registry's 81.3 percent should be read, nor whether its 27 hospitals form one health system, since no page quotes the paper; whether the Joint Commission's 36 percent is right, since no page sources it; what lies behind the 1.35 factor beyond the abstract's own aside; or what search lies behind the navigation absences, since none is described. The papers were not checked, for want of network access, nor the umbrella's five criteria, which come from the Lab Recipe alone, against the determination letter, which is not in the repository; its number agrees across the Lab Recipe, the later protocol and the memory.

We do not know the outcome of the AIDaS proposal, due on 16 December, or what the revised packet that went in on 16 October says, since this book has read only the draft. We do not know which of the gates G0 to G3 are closed or whether version 1 is the flagship recipe they hold back. And we do not know how far the schedule's later dates will move, since no real frame had been ingested by 23 October.

## What would change our mind

The registry paper's own cause categories would decide the counting framing's middle strand: if intraoperative disorientation turns out not to mean a miscount under fluoroscopy, the framing rests on the authors' premise, the safeguards and the cost alone, and the visual abstract's line that almost every case is a counting error should go. A measured saving of operating-room minutes on the hospital's own cases would replace the ten-minute assumption; if it is much smaller, the economic case shrinks to the never event. A published study showing that navigation reduces wrong-level surgery, or a verified capital cost, would change the comparison from the other side.

The first version 1 number on real C-ArmLSpine frames would become the reference row, whatever its value, because the owner has made version 1 the baseline and the reported stack only context; the record already notes that version 1's naming number, as the dry run builds it, is not directly comparable with the reported stack's (the state of knowledge, section 3, item 2). A record that gates G0 to G3 are still open, with version 1 identified as the flagship recipe, would set the plan to train it by 28 October against the order the research directions gave, a conflict only the owner can settle. A filing that slips past 2 November would keep the demo private. A determination letter whose criteria differ from the Lab Recipe's five would change the IRB umbrella's text here. And only the owner's own words change the word-and-number rule, the credit rule or the private-forever list.
