# Crowdsourcing on contributors' own Claude accounts: the research

This is the record behind docs/setup/crowdsourcing.md: what Anthropic's and GitHub's documents say, what they leave open, how Cursor's runs worked as far as the sources show, and the reasoning, arithmetic and safety analysis behind each step of the how-to. The how-to links here for every "why"; this page does not repeat its commands except where an argument needs one.

## Sources and confidence

- Anthropic's and Claude Code's pages were read on 2026-10-10; the legal page, the routines, cloud environments, costs and Projects pages were read again that day for the final revision.
- docs.github.com could not be fetched from where this was written. GitHub's facts come from GitHub's documentation source, the github/docs repository at commit be38ec5 (2026-10-09), and each is cited by the docs.github.com page that source builds; the live wording may differ a little. Two facts were read from GitHub's Enterprise Server copies of a REST page, and say so.
- The template's files are cited as they stand on its default branch at commit b1e2350. FastRender's repository was read in a clone at commit 19bf103.
- Cursor's posts were never read on cursor.com. Every statement about them is a paraphrase, tagged by what it rests on: (excerpt) means search-engine excerpts of Cursor's own words, on its blog or its people's posts on X; (secondary) means coverage by others only; (unverified) means no excerpt or coverage found while revising confirmed it. Nothing from Cursor is quoted.
- Nothing was run end to end on a live organization. Numbers marked as estimates are inferred from the template's code and stated assumptions, not measured.

## What Anthropic's documents say

### The Consumer Terms

Pro and Max accounts fall under them: Claude Code's legal page lists "Consumer Terms of Service - for Free, Pro, and Max users" ([legal and compliance](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/legal-and-compliance)). The Consumer Terms, effective October 8, 2025, say ([consumer terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms)):

> You may not share your Account login information, Anthropic API key, or Account credentials with anyone else. You also may not make your Account available to anyone else.

> You are responsible for all activity occurring under your Account

They also forbid accessing the Services "through automated or non-human means, whether through a bot, script, or otherwise", "Except when you are accessing our Services via an Anthropic API Key or where we otherwise explicitly permit it". Routines, cloud sessions and Desktop scheduled tasks are Anthropic's own features, used as offered.

### Claude Code's legal page

Under "Authentication and credential use" ([legal and compliance](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/legal-and-compliance)):

> OAuth authentication is intended exclusively for purchasers of Claude Free, Pro, Max, Team, and Enterprise subscription plans and is designed to support ordinary use of Claude Code and other native Anthropic applications.

> Anthropic does not permit third-party developers to offer Claude.ai login into their own applications, or to route requests through Free, Pro, or Max plan credentials on behalf of their users. Moreover, developers may not collect, store, or intermediate Claude.ai credentials or session tokens

The same section goes on:

- "sign-in to a Claude account must complete through Anthropic's own flow."
- The rule does not restrict how customers provision their own API keys "for use by the customer's own authorized users", "provided the resulting usage is billed to the key owner under their agreement with Anthropic (or the applicable provider) and is not resold or intermediated as described above."
- "Nor does it prevent an end user from signing in to the unmodified Claude Code binary with their own Claude subscription".
- "Anthropic reserves the right to take measures to enforce these restrictions and may do so without prior notice."

Under "Acceptable use": "Advertised usage limits for Pro and Max plans assume ordinary, individual usage of Claude Code and the Agent SDK."

Under "Can customers offer Claude Code in their products?": "preinstalling or running Claude Code in your products or services (e.g. in hosted sandboxes or other agent infrastructure) requires agreeing to our Commercial Terms of Service", with conditions: "The Claude Code binary must not be modified", and "Customers may not pay for, resell, or intermediate Claude usage on their end users' behalf. Each end user must authenticate with their own Anthropic API key, Claude subscription plan credentials, or 3P inference provider credential".

### The Agent SDK

"Unless previously approved, Anthropic does not allow third party developers to offer claude.ai login or rate limits for their products, including agents built on the Claude Agent SDK." ([Agent SDK overview](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/overview)).

### The Usage Policy

Anthropic's site shows two versions. The current page says "Effective November 12, 2026", later than this record ([usage policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)); its "Previous Version" link says "Effective September 15, 2025" ([previous version](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/archive/22742366-2ef0-4c7a-a833-6523f10d3944)). The archive marks that version as replaced, but by the dates the two pages give, it is the one in force on 2026-10-10. Its "Do Not Abuse our Platform" section forbids using Anthropic's products or services to:

> Coordinate malicious activity across multiple accounts to avoid detection or circumvent product guardrails or generating identical or similar inputs that otherwise violate our Usage Policy

> Utilize automation in account creation or to engage in spammy behavior

> Circumvent a ban through the use of a different account, such as the creation of a new account, use of an existing account, or providing access to a person or entity that was previously banned

It has no item on reselling or proxying. The version effective November 12, 2026 adds one: "Resell, proxy, or otherwise provide access to Claude through unauthorized means, including services that route requests through consumer subscriptions or misrepresent the product or client being used".

### Unattended features run on, and bill, one person's own account

- Routines: "Routines belong to your individual claude.ai account. They are not shared with teammates, and their runs count against your account's usage and limits. Anything a routine does through your connected GitHub identity or connectors appears as you" ([routines](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/routines)). That sentence is about ownership and billing, not about who may start a run: the same page's example use cases include routines on a team's repository that teammates' pull requests start, such as "Bespoke code review. A GitHub trigger runs on `pull_request.opened`. The routine applies your team's own review checklist", and "Library port. A GitHub trigger runs on `pull_request.closed` filtered to merged PRs", and it says "a PR review routine can run nightly, trigger from a deploy script, and also react to every new PR".
- Cloud sessions: a cloud session "counts toward your plan's usage limits alongside the rest of your Claude and Claude Code usage" ([cloud sessions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/claude-code-on-the-web)).
- Long-lived tokens: `claude setup-token` makes a one-year token "For CI pipelines, scripts, or other environments where interactive browser login isn't available", and "This token authenticates with your Claude subscription" ([authentication](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/authentication)).
- Tokens in GitHub Actions: "For a secret shared across repositories, authenticate with an API key from the Claude Console rather than an OAuth token, since an OAuth token is tied to the subscription of the person who ran `claude setup-token`." ([GitHub Actions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/github-actions)).

### What GitHub says about secrets

"Any user with write access to your repository has read access to all secrets configured in your repository." ([secure use](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/security/secure-use)). The roles table gives Write "Create, update, and delete GitHub Actions secrets using the REST API", and the same for variables ([repository roles](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/managing-user-access-to-your-organizations-repositories/managing-repository-roles/repository-roles-for-an-organization)).

## What they allow and forbid here

Allowed, and the whole design of the how-to:

- Each contributor signs in to the unmodified Claude Code with their own Pro or Max account and works from any of these, all theirs: their machine, their cloud sessions at claude.ai/code, their routines, their Desktop scheduled tasks, or `/loop`.
- Their usage counts against their own limits; their commits and pull requests carry their own GitHub user.
- The project coordinates through GitHub alone: issues, claim branches, the board and pull requests. No Anthropic credential passes through it.

Forbidden, each with its source:

- **No contributor credential, anywhere.** The project never asks for, collects, stores or runs on a contributor's Claude credential: the output of `claude setup-token`, a login, a password or a session cookie, in a secret, a file, an issue, a chat or a service of the project. Sources: the Consumer Terms on sharing credentials; the legal page on collecting, storing or intermediating them.
- **No pool.** No token pool, dispatcher or orchestrator sends work through contributors' subscriptions on their behalf. Source today: the legal page, which bars routing requests through Free, Pro or Max plan credentials on behalf of users. From November 12, 2026, the Usage Policy's resell-and-proxy item binds everyone as well.
- **No "sign in with Claude".** No app of the project offers it: not the GitHub App in OpenSpineConsortium/symphony-app, and no Agent SDK service. Sources: the legal page and the Agent SDK page.
- **No account driven by other people.** No contributor makes their account available to anyone else. Source: the Consumer Terms.
- **No reselling or proxying.** The project never resells Claude usage and never proxies access to Claude. Sources today: the legal page's rules for third-party developers and for Claude Code run inside a product or service, which bind the project whenever it acts as either; from November 12, 2026, the Usage Policy too.
- **No coordination across accounts around guardrails, and no way around a ban.** A crowd is many accounts by design, so this is spelled out: its accounts never coordinate malicious activity to avoid detection or get around guardrails, nobody makes accounts by automation for the project, and no contributor gives access to someone Anthropic banned. Source: the Usage Policy in force.

## What the documents do not settle

For each open question the how-to takes the cautious side, and the choice is its own, not the sources'.

- **A contributor's own token in their own CI.** The authentication page documents `claude setup-token` for a person's own CI and scripts. Nothing addresses that use for a shared project. The how-to never uses it, and a contributor's token never goes into the shared repository.
- **A routine started by other people's actions.** Anthropic documents routines that teammates' pull requests start on a shared repository (the examples above). What it does not address is a trigger fired by strangers on a public repository, or by many unrelated volunteers on one person's account. A GitHub trigger's Author filter matches "PR author's GitHub username" ([routines](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/routines)), which is who opened the pull request, not who acted on it, so someone else's label, push or merge can start a run on the routine owner's account. The same holds for auto-fix, which reacts to "a reviewer" commenting ([cloud sessions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/claude-code-on-the-web)), and for a Project's threads, which watch the pull requests they open. The run carries out the owner's own saved prompt. The cautious setup is the schedule alone, outside any Project: the owner's coordinator stays on its schedule, and contributors get one narrow trigger as an option, a merge of their own pull request.
- **A routine's API trigger token held by someone else.** "Anyone holding the bearer token can send `text`" (routines page), and every run it starts spends the routine owner's usage. No source says whether handing one out counts as making the account available. The how-to treats it as a credential: the project never asks for one, and contributors never hand theirs out.
- **The owner's subscription token in a repository contributors can write to.** The template's @claude, worker and lead workflows read the secret CLAUDE_CODE_OAUTH_TOKEN and spend the owner's subscription. Every writer can read every repository secret; the Consumer Terms forbid sharing credentials; the Actions page recommends an API key "For a secret shared across repositories". That sentence is about one secret in many repositories; extending it to many writers in one repository is this record's inference. The reading taken: a crowd repository holds no subscription token where writers can read or trigger it.
- **Paying for contributors' personal plans.** The rule against paying for end users' usage binds a project that runs Claude Code in its own product or service. No source addresses an owner who pays for volunteers' personal Pro or Max plans. The choice taken: fund agents through a plan built for that (next section), never through personal accounts.
- **Unattended use and limits.** "Ordinary, individual usage" is stated as what the advertised limits assume, not as a prohibition. Anthropic's Max page says it "may limit your usage in other ways" at its discretion ([Max plan](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11049741-what-is-the-max-plan)). The legal page also says Anthropic may enforce its authentication rules "without prior notice". A contributor's worker routine is their own use of their own account; how much they run is theirs to weigh against their own limits.

## Paying for the agents centrally

**Claude Team.** Team covers 2 to 150 seats. A Standard seat is $25 a month, or $20 billed annually; a Premium seat is $125, or $100 ([pricing](https://claude.com/pricing)). "Each member's Claude Code usage draws from a per-seat allowance that resets on a rolling five-hour window and a weekly window", and admins can turn on usage credits with spend limits "at the organization, group, or individual member level" ([costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs)). `forceLoginOrgUUID` in managed settings makes Claude Code refuse a login to any other organization, but Anthropic says to "Deploy the keys through your device management tooling", because server-managed settings "can't redirect a developer's first login" ([authentication](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/authentication)); it binds only machines the owner manages, which volunteers' machines are not, and `claude setup-token` and `/install-github-app` "enforce only forceLoginMethod, so they can mint a token in a different organization" (same page). Usage falls under the Commercial Terms, and Anthropic "does not train generative models using code or prompts sent to Claude Code under commercial terms" ([data usage](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/data-usage)). Two cautions: the monthly API credits that come with Team "can't be used in Claude Code or the Claude apps" ([Team and Enterprise](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11845131-use-claude-code-with-your-team-or-enterprise-plan)), and Projects "aren't available on Team or Enterprise plans yet" ([Projects](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/claude-projects)), so Team seats use routines and cloud sessions.

**The Claude Console, at API rates.** Invite each person with the Claude Code role (Settings, Members, Invite); they log in with their Console account ([authentication](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/authentication)). Their usage goes to the Claude Code workspace, which "is the only workspace that supports per-user monthly spend limits" ([workspaces](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/workspaces)). The legal page's paragraph on customers' own keys, quoted above, covers keys "for use by the customer's own authorized users" billed to the key owner. For Actions, use an API key, or workload identity federation, which "exchanges the workflow's GitHub OpenID Connect (OIDC) token for Claude API access through a Claude Console service account" ([GitHub Actions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/github-actions)).

**A cloud provider.** Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud's Agent Platform or Microsoft Foundry, billed to the owner's cloud account (same page).

This record did not review the Commercial Terms; read them before seating people who are not your staff. The template's own rule is "No API key anywhere" (README.md); it changes only by a decision written in docs/project.md, by pull request, before any key is made.

## Cursor's history

### The runs

- "Scaling long-running autonomous coding" (Wilson Lin, Cursor, 2026-01-14) describes hundreds of agents working concurrently on one project, writing over a million lines of code and spending trillions of tokens (excerpt; [scaling-agents post](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)).
- The headline run built a web browser, FastRender, in close to a week: over a million lines across about a thousand files (excerpt). Cursor's CEO put it at more than three million lines and said it only partly works (excerpt of [his X post](https://x.com/mntruell/status/2011562190286045552)). Lee Robinson of Cursor wrote that running coding agents for weeks only partly works so far (excerpt of [his X post](https://x.com/leerob/status/2019460151074259219)).
- The browser's quality is disputed (secondary): at launch its CI was failing and it had no build instructions ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jan/23/fastrender/)); The Register was scathing about the code ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/22/cursor_ai_wrote_a_browser/)); SIG rated its maintainability 1.3 out of 5 ([SIG](https://www.softwareimprovementgroup.com/blog/quality-of-fastrender/)); critics contested Cursor's account of what was built ([Let's Data Science](https://www.letsdatascience.com/news/cursor-misrepresents-ai-built-web-browser-project-f61f9408)).
- In the repository itself, read directly, Cargo.toml depends on html5ever, cssparser and selectors from the Servo project, vendors taffy (`taffy = { path = "vendor/taffy" }`) and a patched selectors (`selectors = { path = "vendor/selectors" }` under `[patch.crates-io]`), and has an optional QuickJS binding (rquickjs) beside its own JavaScript engine, which bears on any claim that it was written from scratch.

### The designs Cursor tried

From the scaling-agents post (excerpt, unless tagged otherwise):

- **A shared file with locks.** Agents claimed tasks in one file guarded by locks. They held locks too long, forgot to release them or failed while holding them, and twenty agents fell to the throughput of two or three.
- **Optimistic concurrency.** Simpler and more robust, but with no hierarchy the agents turned risk-averse: they avoided hard tasks and made small, safe changes.
- **Separate roles**, which worked. Planners keep exploring the code and creating tasks, and spawn sub-planners for areas, so planning runs in parallel and recursively. Workers take one task each, do not coordinate with each other, and push when done. At the end of each cycle a judge decided whether to continue, and the next cycle started fresh.
- An integrator for quality control was added and removed: it caused more bottlenecks than it solved, since workers could resolve conflicts themselves (excerpt; secondary coverage reports the same).
- Open problems: planners should wake when their tasks complete, and periodic fresh starts are still needed against drift and tunnel vision. Cursor also picks a model per role.

From the follow-up, "Towards self-driving codebases" (Lin, 2026-02-05; [self-driving post](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)):

- The final design has a root planner that owns the whole scope and does not write code (unverified: found only in paraphrases, such as [ZenML's summary](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/scaling-multi-agent-autonomous-coding-systems)), sub-planners that each fully own a narrow slice, and workers on their own copy of the repository who end with a single handoff to the planner that asked (secondary: as Lee Robinson relayed it).
- The system peaked near 1,000 commits an hour over a week (excerpt).
- Requiring every commit to be correct serialized the system, so Cursor accepts a small steady error rate and suggests a final fixup pass on a green branch (excerpt).
- Agents rewrite a scratchpad file rather than append to it, against drift (secondary).
- Lessons on stating intent: a vague instruction to implement the spec sent agents deep into obscure, rarely used features; leaks and deadlocks needed process-based resource limits; agents carry instructions through to the end, good or bad (all three unverified).

A later post, "Agent swarms and the new model economics", reports five failure modes, each with a fix: split-brain, planner contention, merge conflicts, megafiles and ossification (secondary only, through [dsebastien.net](https://www.dsebastien.net/cursor-agent-swarms/)).

In FastRender's repository, AGENTS.md splits the work into nine workstreams, each with what it owns and what it does not own, and makes hard time and memory limits mandatory for every command ([FastRender AGENTS.md](https://github.com/wilsonzlin/fastrender/blob/main/AGENTS.md)). docs/triage.md there says: "Split by failure class / hotspot, not by page" and "If you can't show a measurable delta, you are not done." ([FastRender triage](https://github.com/wilsonzlin/fastrender/blob/main/docs/triage.md)). These are the only quoted words from Cursor's side, read in the repository itself.

### Where the crowd version differs

As far as the excerpts and coverage show, Cursor's runs were centralized: one harness, one organization's model access, and about 300 agents per large machine (secondary: Lin in [Simon Willison's interview](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jan/23/fastrender/)). No source describes crowdsourcing across people's own accounts. Here the in-process handoff and the shared machine become GitHub objects: a task is an issue, a claim is a branch, a handoff is a pull request, and the shared state is the board. And GitHub's per-repository guidance sets a ceiling Cursor never had (Scale, below).

## Why the owner's steps are as they are

### Own computer and gh scopes

The owner's commands run on their own computer because a cloud session's GitHub access depends on how it was connected. Through the Claude GitHub App, the session gets the app's permissions, which include "Administration | Read" ([GitHub Actions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/github-actions)); through `/web-setup`, it gets the user's own gh token, which `/web-setup` sends "to your Claude account" ([cloud sessions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/claude-code-on-the-web)), and the GitHub proxy attaches credentials on the server side. Either way the proxy "rejects requests to GitHub's GraphQL endpoint", and "`gh` subcommands that use GraphQL, such as `gh pr` and `gh issue`, get the same 403", whatever token is supplied ([cloud environments](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/cloud-environments)). scripts/setup-repo.sh and docs/setup/owner-checklist.md say a cloud session has no gh at all; that is out of date, since gh "is pre-installed" (same page).

gh's default token has `read:org`. GitHub's REST description accepts "admin:org or repo" for PATCH /orgs/{org}, and requires admin:org for the organization's secret and variable lists; its team-management endpoints name no classic scope, and GitHub's scope definitions put team management under write:org and admin:org, so the team and membership calls very likely fail without the refresh (plausible, not verified). `workflow` is needed to push any file under .github/workflows/. One refresh at the start covers both.

### A Free organization

A public repository in a Free organization gets teams and the five roles ([GitHub's plans](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/learning-about-github/githubs-plans), [repository roles](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/managing-user-access-to-your-organizations-repositories/managing-repository-roles/repository-roles-for-an-organization)), enforced rulesets with bypass lists ([about rulesets](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-rulesets/about-rulesets)), CODEOWNERS with required review ([code owners](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-code-owners)), a merge queue ([merge queue](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/configuring-pull-request-merges/managing-a-merge-queue)), environments with protected secrets ([environments](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/workflows-and-actions/deployments-and-environments)) and free Actions minutes on standard runners ([Actions billing](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/concepts/product-billing/github-actions)). A personal account cannot have teams, which belong to organizations ([about teams](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/organizing-members-into-teams/about-teams)), and never gets bypass lists (docs/setup/tiers.md).

Two-factor authentication: every contributor will have Write, so their accounts deserve the owner's care. "Before you can require ... to use two-factor authentication, you must enable 2FA for your account", and once it is required, "outside collaborators who do not use 2FA will be removed from the organization and lose access to its repositories" ([requiring 2FA](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/keeping-your-organization-secure/managing-two-factor-authentication-for-your-organization/requiring-two-factor-authentication-in-your-organization)), which is why HELPER needs it in route B.

### The internal template and managed users

OpenSpineConsortium/symphony-template is internal (the API reports `"visibility": "internal"`, and docs/setup/owner-checklist.md notes it). "Internal repositories are not visible to people outside of the enterprise", and "Anyone with read access to a template repository can create a repository from that template" ([about repositories](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/creating-and-managing-repositories/about-repositories), [from a template](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/creating-and-managing-repositories/creating-a-repository-from-a-template)). A new Free organization is outside that enterprise. Whether GitHub lets an enterprise member use an internal template for a repository outside the enterprise was not checked; neither route depends on it. Every route publishes the template's files and git history; making the template itself public opens its issues, pull requests and other branches too, which is why its owner must agree.

Both routes also assume an enterprise member can publish outside the enterprise. With Enterprise Managed Users that fails: managed users "Cannot be invited to organizations or repositories outside of the enterprise", "cannot interact with repositories outside of the enterprise" in ways that include "Push code to the repository", and "Only private and internal repositories can be created in organizations owned by an" EMU enterprise ([managed users](https://docs.github.com/en/admin/managing-iam/understanding-iam-for-enterprises/abilities-and-restrictions-of-managed-user-accounts)). A managed user signs in with a username "including the underscore and short code" ([managed user sign-in](https://docs.github.com/en/authentication/authenticating-with-single-sign-on/authenticating-with-a-managed-user-account)). The template's own pull requests are merged by an account whose login has no such suffix, so its enterprise is probably not EMU; that is inferred, not confirmed. Under EMU the remaining path is a non-managed account that receives the files with the template owner's agreement and pushes them as route B describes: a bundle (`git bundle create symphony-template.bundle main`, after `git fetch origin && git switch main && git merge --ff-only origin/main`, since the bundle takes whatever the local main points at) carries the default branch with its history, and `git clone --branch main symphony-template.bundle NAME` gives a clone whose fetch configuration covers every branch, so the single-branch note below does not apply. Whether the enterprise's own policy allows the files to leave it is the template owner's question.

Route B's split of duties: the clone needs someone who can read the template, a member of its enterprise; creating the repository needs an owner of ORG, since the how-to's step 1 turns repository creation off for members; switching Actions needs admin rights on the repository. The push, tools/template_init.py's labels ("Create, edit, delete labels" is a Write power in the roles table) and its setup issue need only Write, so HELPER gets Write for the duration and is removed afterwards.

### Actions off for the first push

.github/workflows/template-init.yml runs on the first push to the default branch of any repository that is not a template and still holds .template/PENDING. A pushed clone meets that condition, so with Actions on, the workflow would fill the placeholders as soon as the clone lands. For a public copy meant to stay a template, "Use this template" or `gh repo create --template` will not do: GitHub's own first commit in the new repository is a first push, so template-init would fill the copy before anyone could tick "Template repository"; the template's README asks its maintainer to tick that box "before its first push" for the same reason. With Actions off during the push and the box ticked before they go back on, template-init skips the copy on every later push. In route B, Actions off makes the README's by-hand case ("When the run failed or Actions are off, do it by hand") the one that applies, so the workflow cannot race the script. Leaving Actions on and letting the workflow initialise the pushed clone also works; then wait for the setup issue and `git pull`.

### A single-branch clone

A clone made with `--single-branch` fetches its one branch only. Without `git config remote.origin.fetch '+refs/heads/*:refs/remotes/origin/*'`, a later `git fetch origin` brings in neither other accounts' `claude/` branches nor fresh board updates: the session hook fetches the board by name into `origin/board` once per session (.claude/hooks/session-start.sh), but `git fetch origin board` in such a clone does not update `origin/board`, so the board read by the claim skill goes stale, and the claim skill's check for "a branch `claude/N` on origin" sees nothing. Route B keeps `--single-branch` rather than a full clone, because a full clone of the template would leave its other branches (claude/festive-edison-al7xu2 today) as stale origin/ refs in what may become the working clone. The line matters only when the owner is also HELPER; otherwise the owner makes a full clone of ORG/NAME afterwards.

### No token, no variables, no auto-merge

scripts/setup-repo.sh creates the ruleset from docs/setup/ruleset-main.json, turns on "Automatically delete head branches" and "Allow auto-merge", turns on secret scanning and push protection, and creates or updates the seven labels; run again, it does nothing twice. Its `--token` stores the owner's subscription token, which every writer could read and every writer's @claude comment would spend (Safety). Its `--var` sets CLAUDE_WORKER and CLAUDE_TEAM, which switch on the workflows that spend that token. Auto-merge is turned off again because any writer may "Enable and disable auto-merge on a pull request" ([repository roles](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/managing-user-access-to-your-organizations-repositories/managing-repository-roles/repository-roles-for-an-organization)): a contributor could turn it on for their own pull request, which would then land the moment an approval arrived, with nobody choosing to merge it. Only team mode needs it, and team mode stays off. The script turns it back on whenever it runs.

Cloud sessions connected through the app push through it (docs/setup/owner-checklist.md, section 1); a session set up with `/web-setup` pushes with the user's own gh token instead (Own computer and gh scopes, above), a routine's GitHub trigger needs it on the repository ("The Claude GitHub App must be installed on the repository you want to subscribe to", routines page), and Projects' threads need it. `/install-github-app` works too if the owner chooses "Skip for now" at the GitHub Actions step, which Anthropic describes as the way "to stop with only the GitHub App installed"; going on would store a subscription token or an API key in the repository ([GitHub Actions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/github-actions)). ### The goal and the setup issue

The goal goes under its own Task issue: tools/template_init.py opens "Set up this project" with no labels, so it is not in the Queue the claim skill picks from, and the protocol's "Closes #N" would close the checklist while most boxes are open; the issue says "Close this issue when every box is ticked." A first issue opened by a person also starts the first board run (docs/agents/STATE.md), and "The branch does not exist until the first run" (same page), so the fetch right after the issue fails until that run ends, about a minute. The bodies of this issue, the epics and the tasks are written under /tmp rather than in the clone: an untracked file there is picked up by the next `git add -A` of any session in that clone, and tools/check_docs.py checks untracked files that are not ignored as well as tracked ones.

The goal matters more than any setting: CLAUDE.md imports docs/project.md, so it is the instruction every session reads. Cursor reports that a vague instruction to implement the spec sent agents into obscure features, and that agents carry instructions through to the end, good or bad (both unverified); FastRender's AGENTS.md lists what counts as progress and makes time and memory limits mandatory for every command. As filled in, docs/project.md's Owners names the organization as the one who merges and says CODEOWNERS "names the same login", and OWNERSHIP.md says "The repository owner merges everything: .github/CODEOWNERS names {{OWNER}}"; both stop being true once CODEOWNERS names teams.

The setup issue's checklist (tools/template_init.py) was written for one owner's accounts: its second box runs `/install-github-app` and writes CLAUDE_CODE_OAUTH_TOKEN, its third makes a Project per account, and its fourth makes the worker in the main account's Project or sets CLAUDE_WORKER and CLAUDE_TEAM. For a crowd, the box for scripts/setup-repo.sh is ticked with a note that "Allow auto-merge" is off on purpose; the token box keeps only the app's installation; the worker box says contributors run their own workers and the variables stay unset; the Project box says no Project is needed and routines are made at claude.ai/code/routines.

### Teams

Contributors must be organization members, since outside collaborators cannot be on a team ([about teams](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/organizing-members-into-teams/about-teams)). Write is what the protocol needs: claim branches live in the repository, and a claim from a fork fails the protocol check (tools/sdlc_protocol/protocol_check.py). Code owners need write access, and "When the code owner is a team, that team must be visible and it must have write permissions" ([code owners](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-code-owners)); `closed` privacy makes a team visible, and `push` is the API's name for Write. Area maintainers join the maintainers team as well, because the queue check accepts only Maintain and Admin.

Whoever creates a team through the API becomes its maintainer automatically ([teams](https://docs.github.com/en/rest/teams/teams#create-a-team), read from GitHub's Enterprise Server copies of the page), so the owner is on every team they make, and as a member of an area team can satisfy its code-owner review. An area team of one cannot review its own member's work: "Pull request authors cannot approve their own pull requests" ([approving](https://docs.github.com/en/pull-requests/how-tos/review-pull-requests/approving-a-pull-request-with-required-reviews)), and when the code owner is a team the approval must come from it. So each area team needs at least two members, or the owner stays on it, or the owner merges the maintainer's own area pull requests by bypass. To leave a team: `gh api --method DELETE orgs/ORG/teams/AREA-maintainers/memberships/OWNER_LOGIN`; an organization owner keeps admin on every repository either way.

### Code owners

template-init fills .github/CODEOWNERS with `* @{{OWNER}}`; in an organization's copy that is the organization's name, which is neither a user nor a team, so it requests nobody. The last matching line wins, so the owner's lines follow the catch-all, the area lines come next, and the instruction files of every folder come last (Instructions in any folder, under Crowd mode as code). The owner keeps the paths that steer every contributor's agent or run code on contributors' machines: the instructions every session loads (CLAUDE.md, docs/project.md, docs/agents/PROTOCOL.md, docs/memory/MEMORY.md, .claude/rules/, .claude/skills/); the hooks in .claude/settings.json and the scripts they call under tools/; scripts/check.sh, which every contributor runs before each push, and the shared files under tests/, which check.sh runs and .claude/settings.json allows without a prompt (`Bash(bash scripts/check.sh)`, `Bash(python3 -m unittest:*)`); and the workflows and issue forms under .github/.

Each area's tests go in tests/test_AREA/, which the area's line hands to the area team; check.sh runs `python3 -m unittest discover -s tests`, which finds tests in a subfolder only when it holds an `__init__.py`. The folder's name is not AREA itself: discovery imports such a folder as a top-level package of that name, so with tests/parser/__init__.py present and src/ on the path for src/parser/, `import parser` in a test resolved to the tests folder and the test failed (tried with Python 3.13; tests/test_parser/ passed). Otherwise every pull request that adds a test would wait for the owner as well. The owner's lines do not cover everything that runs on contributors' machines: the tests import the project's own code, so code an area team approves runs on every contributor's machine at their next check, which makes area review part of that protection. The cost: every pull request that adds a fact to docs/memory/ or touches the shared tests waits for the owner; if that becomes the bottleneck, give /docs/memory/ to a small trusted team.

### Who queues work

On a public repository anyone can open an issue, and both shipped forms label it by themselves: todo on the Task form, blocker on the Blocker form. GitHub's issue-form documentation says a form's labels are added automatically; it does not say a Read user's issue is treated differently, and this was not tested ([issue forms](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/using-templates-to-encourage-useful-issues-and-pull-requests/syntax-for-issue-forms)). The Blocker form matters as much as the Task form: workers take "an unclaimed issue labelled blocker first" (docs/agents/routines/worker.md). The Blocker form's description says it "goes to the top of the board"; in fact the board lists Blockers after the Queue (tools/sdlc_protocol/board.py), and it is the workers' order that puts blockers first.

Applying a label needs Triage or higher ([repository roles](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/managing-user-access-to-your-organizations-repositories/managing-repository-roles/repository-roles-for-an-organization)), so after the forms change strangers cannot queue work, but every contributor has Write and can label any issue todo. The queue's integrity therefore rests on a check in the prompts: the worker and the coordinator act on an issue only when the most recent labeled or unlabeled event for its todo (or blocker) is a labeled event by someone with the role admin or maintain, read from the issue's events and the actor's permission. It has to be the most recent such event, read across every page. GitHub returns an issue's events oldest first, 30 to a page, so a read of the first page that takes the first match finds the maintainer's original todo; if a maintainer later removes todo after a hostile edit (Safety, Re-read on edit) and any writer adds it back, that read still passes, while the latest event, the writer's, fails. Hence `--paginate`, the filter on the label's name and `tail -n 1` in the how-to's commands. The author, or anyone with Write, can still edit a queued issue's title and body, and any writer can edit or delete anyone's comments, so a maintainer re-reads a queued issue whenever it is edited and checks the edit history of an epic and a task before acting on them (Safety).

### Crowd rules and checkpoints

"Rules without `paths` frontmatter are loaded at launch with the same priority as `.claude/CLAUDE.md`" ([memory](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)), so .claude/rules/crowd.md reaches every session. The checkpoint skill as shipped writes .claude/session.md (.claude/skills/checkpoint/SKILL.md, step 2), rewrites the marked block of CLAUDE.md in place (step 4) and commits both on the issue's branch (step 6); in a crowd every open pull request would then rewrite the same two files and conflict, and each would wait for the owner's review of CLAUDE.md. Changing step 6 alone is not enough. Steps 2 and 4 would still leave both tracked files modified after every checkpoint (.claude/session.md is tracked on the default branch), so the rebase a draft needs stops on the unstaged changes, and a later `git add -A` or `git commit -a` puts them in the pull request. Hence steps 2 to 6 are rewritten: the sections go to a scratch file outside the repository and become a comment on the pull request, by `gh pr comment` locally and by the REST comments endpoint in a cloud session, where `gh pr` is refused. The marked block stays, since `bash scripts/check.sh` fails without its markers, but its sentence telling every session to read .claude/session.md would point at a file nobody writes any more, so it points at the latest checkpoint comment on the session's pull request instead. That sentence changes through tools/marked_section.py, the block's own helper, because CLAUDE.md says not to edit the block by hand. The merge line is worded so the owner's own sessions are not told to break the owner's documented workflow: contributors never merge, nobody merges their own work, and the owner alone merges owner-path changes by bypass. Two memory facts need correcting: claude-mentions-run-on-the-main-accounts-subscription.md describes a token this repository does not hold, and a-template-copies-no-settings.md says a cloud session has "no gh".

### Bypass, review rules and the strict policy

Once code owners are required, the owner's pull requests to the paths only they own could never merge: they are the only code owner, an author cannot approve their own pull request, and the shipped ruleset has `"bypass_actors": []`. The eligible bypass actors include "Repository admins, organization owners, and enterprise owners"; in the Add bypass dialog the first two are the roles Repository admin and Organization admin, and "For pull requests only" is in the menu beside each row's "Always allow". It means the owner still opens a pull request for every change, and GitHub records each bypass on it ([creating rulesets](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-rulesets/creating-rulesets-for-a-repository)). The bypass skips the check too, so the owner waits for it anyway. If a second trusted person can review, an owners team of two on the owner lines, with no bypass, gives every change there a second pair of eyes.

The shipped ruleset asks for no review and requires branches to be up to date (`strict_required_status_checks_policy: true`). Required review and the strict policy together are costly: after every merge, each approved pull request that is now behind must update its branch, and that update is a push. With "Require approval of the most recent reviewable push", "the most recent reviewable push must be approved by someone other than the person who pushed it" ([protected branches](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-protected-branches/about-protected-branches)); with stale-review dismissal on, an approval is also dismissed when someone "clicks **Update branch**, or because a related pull request is merged into the target branch" ([available rules](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-rulesets/available-rules-for-rulesets)). Either way one merge would cost every waiting pull request a new review. So the how-to requires one approval, a code owner's review, approval of the latest push and resolved threads, leaves stale-review dismissal off (the compromise GitHub presents last-push approval as allowing, same page), and turns the strict policy off. A pull request can then land while behind, tested on its own head but not on the merged result; the check still runs on every push to the default branch, and a red default branch becomes a blocker (Scale). GitHub documents these review rules for public repositories on Free. The python transform starts from the live ruleset, so it keeps the bypass. docs/setup/github.md says the strict policy is there "so a pull request the board called 'main moved' is rebased before it lands"; with it off, contributors rebase drafts freely and ready pull requests only on a conflict.

The shipped ruleset names the required check by its context alone, `{ "context": "check" }`, which accepts a status of that name from any source. "Any person or integration with write permissions to a repository can set the state of any status check in the repository", and with "any source" the author of each status can still be checked by hand, in the merge box ([available rules](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-rulesets/available-rules-for-rulesets)); "Users with push access in a repository can create commit statuses for a given SHA" ([commit statuses](https://docs.github.com/en/rest/commits/statuses)). So, unpinned, any contributor could mark their own pull request green with one API call, whatever CI said. The transform pins the check with `"integration_id": 15368`, GitHub Actions' app id (the app the template's own check runs report under), which is the API's form of choosing GitHub Actions as the source on the ruleset page; it runs after the first pull requests, so Actions has already reported "check" in the repository. The pin stops a status set by hand. By inference, not tried, it does not stop a writer's own workflow, pushed on some other branch, from creating a check run named "check" with GitHub's token, which reports under GitHub Actions too; that takes a pushed workflow file, which the repository's activity log records, and it is one more reason to read the check's log before merging (Review before merge).

### Who may merge

The rules need one approval from someone other than the last pusher; once it is there, any writer, the author included, can merge, because Write includes merging. "Nobody merges their own work" is therefore a convention; with auto-merge off, breaking it takes a deliberate click. Only restricting who may update the default branch would enforce it: for example a second ruleset with "Restrict updates" on the default branch, with the maintainers team and the role Repository admin as its bypass, for pull requests only. It must be a separate ruleset, because a bypass skips every rule of its own ruleset, reviews included. This was not tested, nor how it combines with a merge queue. GitHub's merge-queue pages say nothing that limits who may add a pull request to the queue, so a queue is not a control here.

### The merge queue

It takes two changes. First, `merge_group:` under `on:` in .github/workflows/ci.yml, so the required check "check" reports to the queue, which GitHub requires ([merge queue](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/configuring-pull-request-merges/managing-a-merge-queue)); the protocol step there runs only on pull_request events, so it skips in the queue. In the same change, add `"gh-readonly-queue/**"` to the push trigger's `branches-ignore` beside `"claude/**"`: the queue tests each group on a temporary branch whose name "begins with the special prefix `gh-readonly-queue/{base_branch}`" (same page), and if GitHub raises a push event for it, which was not confirmed, ci.yml would start a second "check" in the same concurrency group (`ci-${{ github.ref }}`, cancel-in-progress) that could cancel the merge_group run. Expect extra board runs, since board.yml runs on every branch made or deleted. Second, a classic branch protection rule (Settings, Branches) on the default branch's exact name, since a pattern with wildcards cannot carry a merge queue, with "Require status checks to pass before merging" (check `check`) and "Require merge queue". Whether the ruleset form of "Require merge queue" is available on Free could not be confirmed; the branch protection form is documented. The strict policy stays off, as GitHub's repository-limits page advises with a merge queue ([repository limits](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/creating-and-managing-repositories/repository-limits)). The board still comments "main moved" on ready pull requests GitHub reports as behind; under a queue that is noise. Test the queue on one pull request before relying on it.

### Actions settings, secrets and variables

Every workflow in the template declares its own permissions, so a read-only default token takes nothing from them. By default only first-time contributors wait for approval of fork pull request workflows ([Actions settings](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/enabling-features-for-your-repository/managing-github-actions-settings-for-a-repository)). The REST API holds the same setting, PUT /repos/{owner}/{repo}/actions/permissions/fork-pr-contributor-approval with `approval_policy` set to `all_external_contributors`; that was found only through search excerpts of GitHub's REST reference and not tried, hence the settings page as the how-to's fallback. The repository's own secret and variable lists do not show the organization's values it inherits, and the workflows read an organization value exactly as a repository's; docs/setup/owner-checklist.md itself suggests setting the token and CLAUDE_WORKER at the organization with `--visibility all`, and a value whose visibility is all, or selected with this repository on its list, reaches it.

- CLAUDE_CODE_OAUTH_TOKEN: without it the three Claude workflows spend no Claude usage. .github/workflows/claude.yml stops at its first step; claude-worker.yml and claude-lead.yml do not run at all unless CLAUDE_WORKER (and, for the lead, CLAUDE_TEAM) is set, and even then run only short steps on GitHub's own token before the step that reports the missing token.
- COORDINATOR_TRIGGER_URL and COORDINATOR_TRIGGER_TOKEN: the token would be readable by every writer, and each fire would start a run on the owner's account. The coordinator's hourly schedule covers the work.
- SDLC_APP_PRIVATE_KEY: only an organization that set up the private OpenSpineConsortium/symphony-app has it, usually with visibility "all" (docs/setup/owner-checklist.md, section 6). template-init needs it only on the first push; otherwise a writer could read a key that acts on every repository where the App is installed.

Why the check is weekly: see Safety, No secret is a convention.

### Interaction limits

On a public repository anyone signed in to GitHub can open issues and fork pull requests and comment, and all of that text reaches contributors' sessions. "Limit to repository collaborators" "Limits activity for users who do not have write access to the repository", for at most 6 months at a time, and restricts "commenting, opening issues, creating pull requests, reacting with emojis, editing existing comments, and editing titles of issues and pull requests" ([interaction limits](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/moderating-comments-and-conversations/limiting-interactions-in-your-repository)). The `gh api` form was not tried. It does nothing about the project's own writers.

### Labels, the board and tidy

The initialisation makes neither `account:` nor `agent:` labels for anyone but the main account; the template makes agent: labels only in team mode (docs/setup/github.md, section 2), and gh refuses an issue label that does not exist. The board reads a claim's account from the claim file's `- Account:` line (docs/agents/claims/README.md), and from the issue's account label once the claim file is gone.

.github/workflows/board.yml rewrites STATE.md on the branch `board`. Its triggers: a push (the job runs only for the default branch), a branch or tag made or deleted, a pull request opened, synchronized, reopened or closed, an issue opened, edited, closed, reopened, labelled or unlabelled, a run by hand, and every six hours at 17 minutes past. A pull request whose title is edited, which is marked ready or converted to draft, or which is labelled starts no run; the change reaches the board at the next event. The job does not run on a fork pull request's own events, but every other run lists all open pull requests, forks included, with their titles (tools/sdlc_protocol/adapters/github.py); a fork pull request never appears as a claim, since a claim is a branch in this repository. The `board` branch has no protection: the workflow force-pushes it on every run, and docs/setup/github.md warns that a ruleset over it "would block that push and leave the board stale", so any writer can push to it too. .github/workflows/tidy.yml deletes `claude/<issue>` when its issue closes and no open pull request uses the branch. Both run on GitHub's own token.

### Planning tasks

The task shape follows Cursor's and FastRender's planners: one session's work for one person; each task owns a set of files no other open task owns; where two tasks meet, the interface is written into both, so each can be built before the other lands (docs/agents/TEAM.md); split by failure class or hotspot, not by page or file (FastRender's docs/triage.md); a measurable acceptance, a test that passes or a number that moves. The sub-issues API takes the issue's numeric id, not its number ([sub-issues](https://docs.github.com/en/rest/issues/sub-issues)). The `Epic: #N` line is the link team mode uses. An area maintainer opening tasks under their own epic, on their own account, is how sub-planners work here.

### The coordinator outside a Project

docs/agents/routines/coordinator.md and docs/setup/claude-project.md say to make the coordinator in the main account's Project. A Project's cloud thread, after it opens a pull request, "watches the pull request with auto-fix turned on, whether or not auto-fix is on for your other cloud sessions", this "unless you tell it otherwise"; "an idle thread wakes up and uses your plan again when CI fails or a review comment arrives on its pull request"; and scheduled work asked for in a project becomes "a routine that runs as threads in that project" ([Projects](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/claude-projects)). The coordinator opens pull requests (its step 4), so in a Project any writer's review comment on one, and on a public repository any stranger's, would wake a thread on the owner's account, spend the owner's usage and get a reply under the owner's GitHub name. The routines page says nothing of a routine's runs watching their pull requests. Whether a line in the prompt suffices to stop a Project thread watching was not tested; the Projects page says to "ask in the thread for it to stop watching the pull request".

The two triggers coordinator.md asks for are skipped. The pull request events would start a separate run on the owner's account for every push to a draft, every label and every close ("A PR is opened, closed, assigned, labeled, synchronized, or otherwise updated", and "two PR updates produce two independent sessions"), up to hourly caps: "Events beyond the limit are dropped until the window resets" (routines page). The API trigger's token would sit in the repository's secrets. Nothing the coordinator does needs to happen within the hour. A run set exactly on the hour "can start several minutes late". Connectors go because "Claude can use every tool from an included connector, including writes, without asking for permission during a run" (same page).

The shipped step 1 turns "An issue or a comment that asks for more than one piece of work" into new issues "labelled todo", with no check on who wrote it; on a public repository that turns a stranger's text into queued work, done by the owner's account, which runs "without stopping for approval". It also leaves the original issue in the Queue, where a worker can claim the whole while its pieces wait, and nothing stops the next hourly run splitting it again. Claimed keeps todo (the claim skill adds claimed and removes nothing), and the how-to's pre-assigned tasks (Part 1, step 10) carry todo too, so the crowd step leaves alone issues labelled claimed, in-review or agent:<name>, or with a claim branch; splitting those would orphan a pre-assigned task, which the worker looks for as "labelled todo and agent:LOGIN", or duplicate work on a claim. The shipped step 2 releases any claim idle for 24 hours, a ready pull request waiting for review included; in a crowd, review is the slowest part, so finished work would be released every day it waits, and a cloud worker's finished draft would be released too. The crowd step releases only drafts and leaves alone a draft marked "finished; ready to mark".

The crowd prompt ends with the REST forms of everything the coordinator does on GitHub (comments, labels added and removed, new issues and the memory pull request), because a routine runs as a cloud session, where "`gh` subcommands that use GraphQL, such as `gh pr` and `gh issue`, get the same 403" ([cloud environments](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/cloud-environments)). The shipped prompt names no commands, so a run would find that out by failing.

A planner that wakes when work lands (Cursor's open problem): a routine on the owner's or a maintainer's account, triggered when a pull request closes merged, reads that task's epic and opens the next tasks. Each merge, by whoever makes it, then starts a run on that account. Its prompt is to be built.

### Invitations

Until the review rules, the shipped ruleset asked for no review and had no bypass (`required_approving_review_count` 0), so anyone with Write could have merged their own pull request on a green check, CLAUDE.md, the hooks, tools/ and CODEOWNERS included. Inviting straight into a team sends someone outside the organization an email invitation that stays pending until they accept ([team members](https://docs.github.com/en/rest/teams/members#add-or-update-team-membership-for-a-user), read from GitHub's Enterprise Server copies of the page). "If your organization requires members to use two-factor authentication, users that you invite must enable two-factor authentication before accepting the invitation"; "Invitations expire after 7 days", retried from the People page (Failed invitations, Retry invitation); a new organization can send 50 invitations a day, and after a month or on a paid plan 500 ([inviting users](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/managing-membership-in-your-organization/inviting-users-to-join-your-organization)). Removing the membership also removes Write.

## Why the contributor's steps are as they are

### Signing in

"Claude Code requires a Pro, Max, Team, Enterprise, or Console account. The free claude.ai plan does not include Claude Code access." ([setup](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/setup)). Pro and Max both have "a five-hour session limit and a weekly limit", shared across Claude and Claude Code ([Pro and Max](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11145838-use-claude-code-with-your-pro-or-max-plan)); with usage credits off, work stops at the limit. Data from Free, Pro and Max accounts is used for training "when this setting is on (including when you use Claude Code from these accounts)" ([data usage](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/data-usage)). Other credentials outrank the claude.ai login ([authentication](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/authentication)): the cloud-provider switches, ANTHROPIC_AUTH_TOKEN, ANTHROPIC_API_KEY (which Claude Code asks once whether to use), an `apiKeyHelper`, CLAUDE_CODE_OAUTH_TOKEN, and Anthropic profile and federation credentials: a profile named in ANTHROPIC_PROFILE, ANTHROPIC_FEDERATION_RULE_ID with ANTHROPIC_ORGANIZATION_ID, or an active profile whose auth mode is `oidc_federation`. A signed-in Claude apps gateway "sits outside this list" and "outranks" even the cloud-provider switches (same page). `/status` shows which is in use. On Windows PowerShell the installer is `irm https://claude.ai/install.ps1 | iex` (setup page).

### Tools and Windows

scripts/check.sh runs `python3 tools/check_docs.py` and `python3 -m unittest`, and .claude/settings.json runs `bash .../session-start.sh`, `python3 .../guard_push.py` and `python3 .../check_edit.py`. The setup page treats Git Bash as optional on native Windows; without bash and a `python3` on PATH the hooks fail to start, and the push guard then does not stand in the way of a push. WSL gives both. Git for Windows with a `python3` on PATH may work, but was not tested.

### Cloud sessions

In a cloud session gh "is pre-installed", `gh api` REST calls for attached repositories work through the proxy, and GraphQL commands are refused ([cloud environments](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/cloud-environments)). The built-in GitHub tools "read issues, list pull requests, fetch diffs, and post comments" (same page); whether they can mark a pull request ready was not checked, and no REST call does it, so a cloud session never marks ready. `/web-setup` sends the local gh token to the Claude account; it "grants repository access for cloning, but it does not install the Claude GitHub App and does not enable webhook delivery", which a routine's GitHub trigger needs (routines page).

### What loads in a session

Allow rules in a project's settings apply only after the folder is trusted ([permissions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permissions)), and hooks run as commands: "Handlers run in the current directory with Claude Code's environment" ([hooks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks)). CLAUDE.md and its imports load at launch ([memory](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)): docs/project.md, docs/agents/PROTOCOL.md and docs/memory/MEMORY.md. The SessionStart hook prints how far the branch is from origin, with a line asking for a rebase when it is behind, and the first 120 lines of the board; the PreToolUse hook, tools/guard_push.py, checks every shell command; the PostToolUse hook, tools/check_edit.py, checks each edited file. `/hooks` lists what loaded. Reading .claude/settings.json shows only which commands the hooks run: .claude/hooks/session-start.sh, tools/guard_push.py and tools/check_edit.py, and the two Python ones import tools/sdlc_protocol/guard.py and tools/check_docs.py, which is what to read to know what runs. The same file declares OpenSpineConsortium/symphony-marketplace under `extraKnownMarketplaces` and enables nothing from it (docs/project.md). Claude Code may offer to add that marketplace when the folder is trusted; whether it does was not checked, and declining loses nothing, since the repository wires the same hooks itself. The board carries titles other people wrote, strangers' fork pull requests included.

### Rebasing and approvals

The board's "main moved" comment goes only to pull requests whose state GitHub reports as behind or conflicting; GitHub reports a draft as draft and one waiting for review as blocked (tools/sdlc_protocol/adapters/github.py), so drafts and pull requests waiting for review do not get it. Drafts therefore rebase on the session hook's count. Once a pull request is ready or approved, a rebase is a new push, and the ruleset wants the latest push approved by someone else, so it costs a new approval; with the strict policy off, a ready pull request that is only behind can merge as it is. Fixes after review cost the same: each push after an approval needs a new one from someone other than its pusher, so a contributor gathers the fixes into one push.

### Finishing

The protocol check (tools/sdlc_protocol/protocol_check.py) wants the claim file present while the pull request is a draft and gone once it is ready. The check run for the deletion push sees a draft without its claim file and fails the protocol step, or is cancelled by the next run (ci.yml's concurrency cancels in progress), and ci.yml runs again on `ready_for_review`; that run is the one that should pass. The same holds when the deletion is a "Delete file" commit on GitHub followed by "Ready for review": they are two actions. The code owners are requested when the pull request leaves draft ([code owners](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-code-owners)).

### Auto-fix

Auto-fix watches CI and review comments: "when a check fails or a reviewer leaves a comment, Claude investigates and pushes a fix if one is clear", and its replies on review threads "are posted using your GitHub account" ([cloud sessions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/claude-code-on-the-web)). For an ambiguous comment it asks first, and it cannot see a merge conflict on its own. On a public repository anyone can leave a review comment, so a stranger can steer the session, spend the contributor's usage and get a reply under their name; with interaction limits on, only writers can, and they are many. It is stopped by clearing the Auto-fix toggle in the session's CI bar or telling Claude to stop watching. A Project's thread turns it on by itself for every pull request it opens.

### The worker addendum

- **The Account line and "a claim of yours".** The shipped prompt recognises a claim of yours by the issue's account label alone (worker.md, step 1). A released issue keeps its old holder's label beside the next claimant's, and the board takes the first account label in alphabetical order (tools/sdlc_protocol/labels.py), so the old holder's worker would carry on with the new claimant's branch. The claim file's Account line and the pull request's author identify the holder instead.
- **"finished; ready to mark".** A cloud worker cannot mark ready; it comments and stops, and the skip line keeps later hourly runs off that draft until its owner finishes it. Pushing the deletion without marking ready would leave a draft without its claim file, which fails the protocol check on every run, and every run would try again.
- **Rebase only on a conflict once ready.** Each rebase costs a new approval (above).
- **Do not watch or auto-fix.** For anyone who runs the worker in a Project anyway; whether a line in the prompt is enough was not tested.
- **Pre-assigned tasks.** An issue labelled agent:LOGIN is never in the Queue, and the claim skill's first step takes only issues the Queue shows, so the worker must look them up.
- **The area line** spreads workers across slices; without it every worker converges on the lowest-numbered issue (Scale).
- **Who added todo or blocker, last.** Every writer can label; only the role of whoever added the label most recently tells a maintainer's queueing from anyone else's, and an earlier maintainer's event says nothing once the label was removed and added again (Who queues work).
- **Text is data, and the checkpoint as a comment**, as in crowd.md.
- **The REST calls** replace the claim skill's `gh issue` and `gh pr`, which a cloud session refuses.

### Three ways to run a worker

A routine: each run clones the repository fresh from the default branch; the Default environment's network access is Trusted, "which allows only the default allowlist"; "The prompt input includes a model selector"; the schedule picks up reviews and failed checks, because the documented GitHub trigger events are pull request and release events only, so worker.md's line that pull request events wake it on "a review comment or a failed check" is not confirmed by the routines page. The optional merged trigger avoids every other pull request action, since the worker's own pushes are pull request events too (synchronized) and each would start another run; it is the merger's action that starts the run, the open question above. Every run spends the plan's usage; at the limit further runs are rejected unless usage credits are on; a routine turns off after 72 hours without a GitHub connection; an account can start 100 scheduled runs an hour; routines are in research preview; the on/off switch pauses one (routines page). A green status means only that the session started and exited: "It does not mean the task in your prompt succeeded" (same page), hence reading the first run's transcript. The CLI's `/schedule` makes the same routine. Made in a Project, a worker's threads would watch every pull request they open with auto-fix on.

A Desktop task: Anthropic suggests "always allow" for each prompt, after which "Future runs of that task auto-approve the same tools without prompting" ([Desktop scheduled tasks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/desktop-scheduled-tasks)). On a public queue that removes the protection prompts give, so only narrow, read-only commands are always-allowed (.claude/settings.json already allows `git fetch`, `git status`, `git log`, `git diff` and `bash scripts/check.sh`, among others), and a stalled run waits in the sidebar. The task runs only while the app is open and the computer awake; Keep computer awake is under Settings, This computer, System (same page). The isolated worktree keeps a run off the contributor's own uncommitted changes.

`/loop`: a loop runs inside the session that started it, in its folder, and each worker run switches branches and commits there (worker.md, step 3), hence its own worktree. It fires only while Claude Code is running and idle, an hourly loop runs up to 30 minutes late, it expires after seven days, and it is stopped by asking Claude to cancel it or ending the session ([scheduled tasks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/scheduled-tasks)). Its permission prompts are the session's ("Inherits from session", same page), so with prompts on, as the Safety checklist asks, each push or label change waits for an answer and the loop is not unattended. Unlike a routine run it does not start fresh: "a scheduled task fires on its interval even while the session is idle, sending your full context each time" ([costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs)), so its context and its cost grow from run to run, which is why the how-to restarts it daily.

One worker per account: two workers on one account race each other for the queue under the same label (worker.md).

## Crowd mode as code

scripts/crowd.py, tools/crowd_check.py and .github/workflows/queue.yml turn the steps above into commands. The facts below are what they rest on beyond the sections above. Most come from GitHub's REST description (the OpenAPI file api.github.com.json in github/rest-api-description, from which the REST pages on docs.github.com are rendered) and from GitHub's documentation source (github/docs), read from copies because docs.github.com could not be fetched from the session that wrote them; none was tried against a live organization.

### A template from its creation

`POST /orgs/{org}/repos` and `POST /user/repos` take `is_template`, so `publish-template` makes the public copy a template before its first push; template-init.yml and board.yml both run only when `github.event.repository.is_template == false`, so they skip it with Actions on, and the copy keeps its placeholders. The route in Actions off for the first push stays the one for a copy made any other way. The run names the copy "Public copy of SRC at SHA7"; that is how a second run tells the empty template it made, when its push failed, from any other repository of that name. `GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/git/ref/{ref}` lists 409 Conflict among its answers, which GitHub gives, with the message "Git Repository is empty.", for a repository with no commit yet (seen in practice; the description does not say when it answers 409). A clone's history carries every path any of its commits held, so the first run lists the paths only earlier commits hold as well as the files at the head.

### A copy fills later

`POST /repos/{template_owner}/{template_repo}/generate` answers before the new repository has its files, and the description offers no way to wait for them. GitHub's own CLI retries a clone of a repository it has just made from a template, three times three seconds apart, after its maintainers confirmed that cloning at once failed with "couldn't find remote ref refs/heads/main" (cli/cli issue 7055 and its fix, `cloneWithRetry` in pkg/cmd/repo/create/create.go). While the copy is empty the contents endpoint answers 404 for every file, the same answer that says .template/PENDING is gone, so setup first waits for the default branch's first commit (the reference answers 200 instead of 409), then for template-init, under one deadline.

### Pull requests from collaborators

`PATCH /repos/{owner}/{repo}` takes `pull_request_creation_policy: "collaborators_only"`. GitHub's page "Disabling pull requests" says that in an organization's repository "a collaborator is a user who has been granted the write, maintain, or admin role", and the setting is on github.com for every plan (data/features/disable-restrict-prs.yml: fpt '*'). It stops strangers' fork pull requests, which the claim protocol cannot use anyway (a claim is a branch here) and whose titles would reach every session through the board. Whether it stops Dependabot's pull requests was not checked; `--open-pull-requests` leaves the policy alone.

### The bypass actor and the pin

The crowd ruleset's one bypass actor is `{"actor_id": null, "actor_type": "OrganizationAdmin", "bypass_mode": "pull_request"}`: "If `actor_type` is `OrganizationAdmin`, `actor_id` is ignored", and "`pull_request` means that an actor can only bypass rules on pull requests" (the REST description, repository-ruleset-bypass-actor). The Repository admin role of Bypass, review rules and the strict policy is left out, because its role id is not documented and the owner is an organization owner. Pinning the required check to an app needs the app to "be installed in the repository with the statuses:write permission", to "have recently submitted a check run", and to "be associated with a pre-existing required status check in the ruleset" (available-rules-for-rulesets.md). A fresh copy has no check run until setup's own pull request: template-init pushes with GITHUB_TOKEN, and events made with it start no workflow, "with the following exceptions: workflow_dispatch and repository_dispatch events always create workflow runs" (reusables/actions/actions-do-not-trigger-workflows.md). Hence setup writes the ruleset without the pin, and `finish` pins it once that pull request's run has reported; the same exception is why queue.yml starts the board with a dispatch after it takes a label off.

### Who pushed and what it holds

`finish` merges crowd/setup past the review rules, so it must know the branch is setup's work. A commit's author is whatever the pusher typed into git (The push guard), so the commit list proves nothing. `GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/activity` "Lists a detailed history of changes to a repository, such as pushes, merges, force pushes, and branch changes, and associates these changes with commits and users", filtered by `ref`, each item with `before`, `after`, `timestamp`, `activity_type` and `actor`; the activity view's page says it associates them "with commits and authenticated users". `finish` requires the push whose `after` is the pull request's head, and every push since the pull request opened, to be the signed-in owner's; whether a ref moved through the Git refs API, as setup moves it, shows there was not tried, and finish waits rather than merges while no push shows the head. The content is checked against the tree, not the pull request's file list, which shows a rename by its new name alone and so hides the deletion of the old path: the head commit's one parent must be the default branch's head, and its tree, read with `GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/git/trees/{sha}?recursive=1` (up to 100,000 entries and 7 MB, or `truncated`), must hold the default branch's files with setup's change applied, blob for blob, each blob's id computed locally as git computes it. The owner login that change is built for is never read from the pull request, whose CODEOWNERS any writer could have rewritten in their own favour: it is the signed-in owner or `--owner`, and once crowd mode is on, the one owner the default branch's reviewed CODEOWNERS names. The audit holds CODEOWNERS to the same login, for the same reason.

### A later setup keeps your edits

Setup runs again to add an area or renew a limit, long after the owner has corrected OWNERSHIP.md or the crowd rule by pull request, as Part 1, step 6 tells them to. Rebuilding every crowd file from docs/setup/crowd/ would revert those edits, and finish, which expects exactly what setup would write, would then insist on the revert. So once crowd mode is on, setup asks the default branch's history whose a crowd file is: `GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/commits?sha=MAIN&path=FILE&per_page=1` returns the latest commit that changed it, and a file whose latest commit's title does not start with setup's own, "Turn on crowd mode", holds a later, reviewed edit and is kept. The merge commit `finish` makes only brings setup's commit in, so, as in `git log -- FILE`, it should not be the one listed; that was not tried, and a file the history misjudges is kept, the side that loses nothing; a new area's rows go into CODEOWNERS and OWNERSHIP.md in place, after the rows of the areas already there. The one-time edits to docs/project.md, CLAUDE.md and README.md are not made again. A crowd file that is still setup's is written anew, which is how a newer docs/setup/crowd/ reaches it; one that was edited takes the newer source only by hand.

### Instructions in any folder

Claude Code "discovers CLAUDE.md and CLAUDE.local.md files in subdirectories under your current working directory" and loads each "once Claude reads, writes, or edits another file in that subdirectory"; rules in a nested .claude/rules/ load the same way, and AGENTS.md is read where a folder has no CLAUDE.md ([memory](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)). The root's .mcp.json defines the project's MCP servers. A CODEOWNERS pattern with no slash but a trailing one matches at any depth, as in gitignore, so `CLAUDE.md`, `CLAUDE.local.md`, `AGENTS.md` and `.claude/` written after the area lines give those files to the owner inside an area too, where the area's own line would otherwise win; the audit probes them inside each area.

### The check holds no secret

GitHub hands a job's runner the value of every secret the job names, even one it only compares with nothing: "Although GitHub Actions scrubs secrets from memory that are not referenced in the workflow (or an included action), the `GITHUB_TOKEN` and any referenced secrets can be harvested by a determined attacker" (compromised-runners.md), and GitHub-hosted runners give steps passwordless sudo, which is how the tj-actions/changed-files compromise read secrets from the Runner.Worker process's memory. The job check runs the pull request's own scripts and tests, so it names no secret. A job of its own, values, checks nothing out, runs no file of the repository and uses no action; it compares each Claude secret with nothing and hands check only the names that are set, as its output. That a value named in one job stays off another job's runner is how vendors describe the runner (Datadog's rule against `toJSON(secrets)` says it would expose "every secret instead of only the ones required by the job"); GitHub's page says "referenced in the workflow". check runs whatever values did (`if: ${{ !cancelled() }}`), so the required check is never skipped, and fails in crowd mode when values did not succeed. Variables are no secret, and the guard step reads them itself.

### An organization's interaction limit

`PUT /repos/{owner}/{repo}/interaction-limits` answers 200 or 409: "If an interaction limit is set for the user or organization that owns this repository, you will receive a `409 Conflict` response and will not be able to use this endpoint to change the interaction limit for a single repository." The GET answer carries `origin`, which says where the limit was set, so setup leaves an organization's limit alone and the audit says to renew it with `PUT /orgs/{org}/interaction-limits`.

### Names GitHub refuses

A label's description "Must be 100 characters or fewer" (the REST description); a label's name over 50 characters is refused too, with 422, which GitHub does but the description does not say. A 422 from `POST /repos/{owner}/{repo}/labels` means the label exists only when its error's code is `already_exists`, so setup stops on any other, and an area is at most 45 characters, which keeps `area:AREA` within 50. The limit on a team's name, which is AREA-maintainers, was not checked.

## Scale

### GitHub's guidance per repository

GitHub recommends at most 6 pushes a minute, 1 merged pull request a minute, 1,000 open pull requests against one branch, and 5,000 branches per repository ([repository limits](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/creating-and-managing-repositories/repository-limits)). These are recommendations, not refusals: "Exceeding the recommended maximum limit increases the risk of degraded repository health". Cursor's system peaked near 1,000 commits an hour (excerpt), a rate no single repository under this guidance reaches. Above the ceilings below, the remedy is more repositories, not a faster one: each slice can be its own copy of the template, with its own board, queue and secrets.

### The board's API budget

This binds first, at dozens of contributors. Every figure below is an estimate from the code, not a measurement.

- **The budget.** "The rate limit for `GITHUB_TOKEN` is 1,000 requests per hour per repository" ([REST rate limits](https://docs.github.com/en/rest/using-the-rest-api/rate-limits-for-the-rest-api)); 15,000 for resources of a GitHub Enterprise Cloud account. It belongs to the repository and is shared by every run in the hour that calls the API with GITHUB_TOKEN, not given to each run.
- **How often the board runs.** board.yml runs on pushes, branches made and deleted, four pull request actions and six issue actions, with one run going and one waiting (`concurrency: board`, newest waiting run kept). Under steady activity it therefore runs back to back. Events per landed task, counted from the protocol: the claim branch made (1), labels claimed and account (2), the draft opened (1), about five work pushes (synchronize, 5), the label in-review (1), the pull request closed (1), the push to the default branch (1), the branch deleted (1), the issue closed (1), and the planner's issue opened with two labels (3): about 18. Thirty contributors landing one task every three hours make 10 tasks and about 180 events an hour, well above the 20 to 60 runs an hour the board can complete.
- **Calls per run.** From collect() and post_comments() in tools/sdlc_protocol/adapters/github.py: a page of open issues, a page of open pull requests and a page of branches; for each open pull request one detail call, a second detail call after a 3-second wait when GitHub has not computed mergeability yet (which after a push to the default branch can be all of them), and a page of its files; for each claim branch one contents call for its claim file, and one issue call when its issue is closed; one page of closed pull requests; and for each comment due, a page of the pull request's comments and the post. With every open pull request a claim, that is about 5 + 3P calls for P open pull requests, up to 5 + 4P.
- **Runs per hour.** A run is a runner start, a checkout, Python, the calls in sequence and the waits. Assuming one to three minutes, not measured, that is 20 to 60 runs an hour.
- **Where it binds.** Calls an hour = runs an hour times (5 + 3P). The budget holds while 5 + 3P stays under 1,000 divided by runs an hour: at 60 runs an hour, about 4 open pull requests; at 20 an hour, about 15. Worked example: 30 open pull requests make about 95 calls a run, 125 with every mergeability recomputed; at 20 runs an hour that is 1,900 to 2,500 calls, two to two and a half times the budget.
- **What happens then.** The run fails on GitHub's refusal, and the board stays at its last commit until the hour resets. Claims look free on a stale board, so more claim pushes are refused (one push each), new queue entries do not show, and the "main moved" and idle comments wait.
- **A second effect, inferred from board.yml and not observed.** A push to a claim branch starts a board run whose job is skipped, and the concurrency group is the workflow's, so such a run can displace a waiting full run; the change that run carried then waits for the next event.
- **Today.** Cap open claims at one per contributor in docs/project.md. By pull request to board.yml (an owner path): drop `synchronize` from the pull request types and `edited`, `labeled` and `unlabeled` from the issue types, so the most frequent events start no run; run the schedule every 15 minutes (`*/15 * * * *`) instead of every six hours as the floor; and give `push` a `branches-ignore: ["claude/**"]`, so claim pushes stop displacing waiting runs. A newly queued issue or a pull request's new files then appear at the next other event or within 15 minutes. None of this was tested.
- **To be built.** A debounce in the workflow; and rendering the board from the symphony-app's webhook server, whose installation token gets 5,000 to 12,500 requests an hour (same page), which also keeps the App's private key out of the crowd repository and lets a ruleset protect `board`.

### The push rate

6 pushes a minute is 360 an hour. Per landed task, assumed: the claim (1), about five work pushes (5), about one rebase (1), the finish (1) and the merge (1), about 9. The board force-pushes `board` after each run that changed it, at most once a run, so 20 to 60 an hour when busy; take 40. That leaves about 320 pushes an hour for tasks, or about 35 landed pull requests an hour. At one landed pull request per contributor every five hours, that is about 175 contributors.

### The merge rate

1 merge a minute is 60 an hour; at one landed pull request per contributor every five hours, 300 contributors. The push rate binds first, at roughly 175. "Each merge triggers mergeability checks for all open pull requests" (repository limits), which also lengthens the board's runs through the 3-second waits.

### Review

The owner merging by hand is Cursor's integrator: one gate for many workers, and the true bottleneck. Spread review across area maintainers with CODEOWNERS lines per area, area test folders included, and required code-owner review; keep tasks small with measurable acceptance, so a review is short; watch the two owner paths ordinary work touches, docs/memory/ and the shared tests/, and hand them to a trusted team if they queue. To be built: a review routine per area that a maintainer runs on their own account, triggered on their area's ready pull requests. Cursor's answer, dropping per-commit correctness and accepting a small error rate (excerpt), is about correctness inside one trusted organization; it does not carry over to untrusted contributions (Safety).

### "main moved" and the up-to-date rule

With the shipped strict policy on and review required, every merge makes each approved waiting pull request update its branch, rerun CI and get a new approval: one merge costs N updates, N CI runs and N re-reviews. The board's comment goes only to ready pull requests GitHub reports as behind or conflicting. GitHub's secondary limits allow about 80 content-creating requests a minute and 500 an hour, web actions included (REST rate limits page); the board's comments count against them. With the strict policy off, the merge queue is the way to test merged results without updating anyone's branch. To be built: `merge_group` in ci.yml shipped by the template, and a board that comments only on conflicts while a merge queue is on.

### Actions concurrency

GitHub-hosted runners allow 20 concurrent jobs for a whole Free account, 60 on Team and 500 on Enterprise ([Actions limits](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/limits)). Every push to a pull request runs the check, and every event the board; a busy board holds one job continuously. Keep `bash scripts/check.sh` fast (ci.yml already cancels a run a newer push makes stale), and move to GitHub Team when jobs queue.

### Queue convergence

Each worker takes "the lowest-numbered issue the board's Queue section shows" (worker.md), from a board that lags each claim, the more so once the budget above runs out. Dozens of hourly workers converge on one issue; the loser only loses a push, but every worker read the board for nothing. Today: area labels in each contributor's prompt, and pre-assigned `agent:LOGIN` tasks. To be built: deal-out across accounts, the way tools/team.py deals issues to team members by slot.

### Released claims

This is a bug. The coordinator removes `claimed` but keeps the branch and the account label (coordinator.md), and the board's queue leaves out any issue with a `claude/<issue>` branch (docs/agents/STATE.md). So a released issue disappears from everyone's queue, and nobody can claim it again, because `claude/N` exists and GitHub refuses the next account's push of that name. Today a maintainer frees the name: close the old draft with a comment that names `released/N` and mentions the old holder (renaming a branch closes an open pull request whose head it is, [renaming a branch](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-branches-in-your-repository/renaming-a-branch)); rename the branch to `released/N` on the branches page, which keeps the work for the next account to read; remove the old holder's `account:` label, so the issue, which still carries todo, returns to the Queue without a stale holder beside the next one. To be built: a released state the board understands, in the symphony-protocol library.

The 24 hours lives in three places, which must change together: step 2 of the coordinator prompt, which does the releasing; docs/agents/PROTOCOL.md, Work in the open and Release; and IDLE_AFTER in the claim protocol library, which drives the board's idle comment and the gate, not the release. The first two change here by pull request. IDLE_AFTER does not: tools/sdlc_protocol/ is a vendored copy, tests/test_vendored.py holds it to the release tools/VENDORED names, and docs/project.md says a change to the rules "is made there", in OpenSpineConsortium/symphony-protocol. One trivial commit a day keeps a claim alive.

### Checkpoint conflicts

The checkpoint skill as shipped commits .claude/session.md and the marked block of CLAUDE.md on each branch, so every open pull request would rewrite the same two files. Today: checkpoints as pull request comments, written into the crowd rules and the checkpoint skill. To be built: one checkpoint file per issue.

### Accounts by hand

Each contributor needs two labels and, for an area, a row, made by hand. The claim file's Account line is self-declared text that nothing ties to the GitHub user who pushed (docs/agents/claims/README.md). To be built: the board reads the account from the pusher's GitHub login, and one script adds a contributor (team membership, labels, row) in one step. ## Safety

### What Write allows

Write is what the protocol needs, and it is a lot of trust. With Write, a user can merge pull requests, enable auto-merge where the repository allows it, push to any unprotected branch (other people's `claude/` branches and the board branch included), rename and delete such branches, "Edit and delete anyone's comments on commits, pull requests, and issues", apply labels, set the state of any status check (Bypass, review rules and the strict policy), create and run workflows, and create, update and delete Actions secrets and variables ([repository roles](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/managing-user-access-to-your-organizations-repositories/managing-repository-roles/repository-roles-for-an-organization)); they can also read every repository secret ([secure use](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/security/secure-use)).

The comments matter as much as the branches. Any writer can quietly rewrite a maintainer's comment, a claimer's posted plan, the coordinator's release and checkpoint comments, and the comments on an epic that planners and workers read, and can edit the epic's own text. GitHub keeps the record: "Anyone with read access to a repository can view a comment's edit history", and where a writer deletes a revision's content, "the person who made the edit and when they made the edit is still visible" ([tracking changes](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/moderating-comments-and-conversations/tracking-changes-in-a-comment)).

### The push guard

tools/guard_push.py refuses a push to the default or the board branch, a plain `--force`, and `--force-with-lease` outside a `claude/` branch. An ordinary push to someone else's `claude/` branch passes it, and so does `--force-with-lease` to another account's `claude/` branch: refusal() refuses a lease only for destinations that do not start with `claude/`, and nothing checks whose branch it is (tools/sdlc_protocol/guard.py), so a contributor's session can rebase over another account's claim and overwrite it; only the protocol forbids it. The guard also runs in each contributor's own Claude Code, which they can turn off: hooks can be disabled with `disableAllHooks` ([hooks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks)), and on native Windows without bash and python3 it does not run at all. On GitHub, only the default branch is protected (docs/setup/ruleset-main.json).

Therefore: invite people with reason to trust them, known people or people whose earlier work was reviewed, and remove them promptly when they leave. Do not trust commit authors: a commit's author and committer are whatever the person configured in git, so a writer can push to someone else's claim under that person's name, and nothing in the template signs commits or records who pushed. GitHub's own record names the account behind each push: `gh api "repos/{{REPO}}/activity?ref=refs/heads/claude/N"` lists the branch's pushes, force pushes, creation and deletion with each actor (the endpoint was tried, the ref filter was not). Contributors who sign their commits and turn on vigilant mode make a commit that only claims their name show as unverified ([vigilant mode](https://docs.github.com/en/authentication/managing-commit-signature-verification/displaying-verification-statuses-for-all-of-your-commits)); whether commits made in cloud sessions and routines are signed was not checked. The real fix is to be built: a server-side check, in the symphony-app's check run, that only the claimer pushes to `claude/N`. Fork pull requests pass the protocol check only when their branch is not named `claude/`, and the board never lists them as claims, so a path for people without Write needs a claim that lives outside this repository.

### No secret is a convention

The crowd repository holds no Claude secret and no Claude variable, but that property is checked, not enforced: any writer can create a repository secret or variable through the REST API at any time. A contributor who stored their own CLAUDE_CODE_OAUTH_TOKEN would turn @claude back on: .github/workflows/claude.yml runs whenever that secret is set, so every writer's @claude comment would then spend that contributor's subscription, and every writer could read the token, which is the credential sharing the Consumer Terms forbid. A writer who set CLAUDE_WORKER to `true` would switch on claude-worker.yml (its job runs `if: vars.CLAUDE_WORKER == 'true'`), which then spends whatever token is set. Hence the weekly lists in the how-to, the crowd rule "Never add a repository secret or variable", and the required check's guard, tools/crowd_check.py, which fails while any of those secrets or variables reaches a repository in crowd mode, without the check's own job ever holding a value (The check holds no secret).

A credential a workflow truly needs goes in an environment restricted to the default branch, with a required reviewer where a run can wait: "Secrets stored in an environment are only available to workflow jobs that reference the environment. If the environment requires approval, a job cannot access environment secrets until one of the required reviewers approves it." ([environments](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/workflows-and-actions/deployments-and-environments)). Environments are free on public repositories; the template's workflows do not reference one yet. No contributor ever pastes a token into an issue, a pull request, a comment or a file; secret scanning and push protection (turned on by setup-repo.sh) and tools/check_docs.py, which refuses common secret shapes in tracked files, back that up without replacing it (docs/setup/github.md, section 5).

### Prompt injection

On a public repository, anyone signed in to GitHub can open an issue, open a fork pull request and comment. Every contributor's worker reads issue bodies and comments through `gh`, with git, gh and the shell at hand, and so does the owner's coordinator; a planted instruction can reach many accounts at once. The paths in:

- **The board, which every session reads first.** The session hook prints its first 120 lines into every session's context, and the worker and coordinator prompts begin by reading it. It carries the title of every open pull request, strangers' fork pull requests included, and of every queued issue. Nobody reviews it, and the unprotected `board` branch lets any writer force-push a board of their own.
- **Queued issues and comments edited after queuing.** The author or any writer can edit a queued issue, and any writer anyone's comment, an epic's plan included. The board re-renders on every issue edit. Reading an issue before labelling it protects nothing later.
- **Labels.** The shipped forms label by themselves, and every contributor can apply todo or blocker to any issue.
- **Review comments that auto-fix and Project threads act on.** Each wake spends that account's usage and may post a reply under its GitHub name.

The defenses:

- **Only Maintain and Admin queue work.** The forms carry no labels; a maintainer reads each issue before labelling it; the worker and coordinator act only when the latest event for todo or blocker is Maintain or Admin adding it; the coordinator opens what it splits without todo.
- **Re-read on edit.** A maintainer re-reads a queued issue whenever it is edited, checks who edited it, and removes todo if the edit changed what it asks, which holds only because the check reads the latest label event; the same goes for an epic's plan and its comments. queue.yml now takes todo or blocker off when anyone but a maintainer adds one, opens an issue with one, or edits a queued issue's title or body. It sees no edit of an epic and no comment or comment edit, which stay the maintainers' to re-read; crowd.md tells every session to look at an edit's history before acting on it, and the worker and coordinator still read the latest label event.
- **All of that text is data.** crowd.md, the worker addendum and the coordinator's step 1 say so.
- **Fewer outside writers.** Interaction limits stop strangers' issues, comments and fork pull requests, and their titles.
- **Watch the board branch.** Every push to `board` should come from the board workflow; `gh api "repos/{{REPO}}/activity?ref=refs/heads/board"` lists who pushed it. Protecting it is to be built: GitHub documents roles, teams and GitHub Apps as eligible bypass actors ([creating rulesets](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-rulesets/creating-rulesets-for-a-repository)) but not the identity GITHUB_TOKEN pushes as, and docs/setup/github.md warns that a ruleset over `board` would stop the board. A board pushed with the symphony-app's token, with a ruleset on `board` ("Restrict updates" and "Restrict deletions") whose only bypass is that App, would close this path.
- **Routines carry no connectors, and the network stays Trusted.** "Claude can use every tool from an included connector, including writes, without asking for permission during a run"; the Default environment limits outbound traffic to an allowlist (routines page). A routine always runs without prompts, which is why these matter.
- **On a contributor's own machine, permission prompts stay on**, or Claude Code's sandboxing is used, for a worker on a public queue.
- **Auto-fix only while watching, and no routine in a Project.**
- **Actions Claude stays off.** claude-code-action strips hidden content such as HTML comments and invisible characters from what it passes in, and its security notes warn that bypasses may emerge ([action security](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code-action/blob/main/docs/security.md)). Scheduled runs skip its write-access check, though not its check against bot actors ([GitHub Actions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/github-actions)).

The instructions themselves are an injection path too. CLAUDE.md, docs/project.md, docs/agents/PROTOCOL.md, .claude/rules/, .claude/skills/ and docs/memory/ are read by every session; the hooks, the scripts they call under tools/, scripts/check.sh, the tests it runs and the project's code those tests import execute on every contributor's machine. A merged change there reaches everyone, which is why CODEOWNERS keeps the shared paths for the owner and each area's code and tests for its team. On their own machine contributors can read what changed before a session (the `git log -p` line in the how-to's checklist). Unattended cloud workers get no such step: "Each repository you add is cloned on every run" (routines page), and a run uses whatever was merged last. For what lands in the repository, review before merge is their protection; for the board and the text of issues and pull requests, which nobody reviews, it is the rule that such text is data.

### The owner's Claude workflows

Three workflows authenticate with CLAUDE_CODE_OAUTH_TOKEN, the owner's subscription token: .github/workflows/claude.yml, which answers @claude, claude-worker.yml and claude-lead.yml. docs/setup/claude-action.md says a run "spends the main account's own usage: the five-hour window and the weekly limit"; with OAuth, "runs use your Claude subscription instead of API billing" ([GitHub Actions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/github-actions)). On issue and pull request events, "the triggering user must have write access to the repository" (same page), and every contributor has it, so any contributor's @claude comment would start a run, and so would any contributor's epic label while team mode is on; a Triage-only user is refused at that check, after the workflow's own GitHub-token steps have run. Scheduled runs skip the write check, though not the check that refuses bot actors. And every writer could read the token. So the default is no token. To offer @claude to everyone on the owner's bill, use what Anthropic recommends for a shared secret, a Console API key or workload identity federation, which is API billing and needs the decision in docs/project.md first. For @claude for the owner alone, claude.yml must check that the actor is the owner and read the token from an environment limited to the default branch; a trigger check alone is not enough, because a writer's own workflow could still read a repository secret. That change is to be built.

### Review before merge

- **Required review.** One approval, a code owner's review, approval of the latest push by someone other than its pusher, and resolved threads. After that approval the author can still merge (Who may merge).
- **Read the diff, not the description.** The board's Files column shows the files the pull request changes, not what the claim listed (docs/agents/STATE.md), and the claim file is gone by review time. Compare the diff with the claim file as first committed on the branch (`git show "$(git rev-list --reverse origin/{{DEFAULT_BRANCH}}..origin/claude/N | head -n 1)":docs/agents/claims/N.md`, or the first commit in the pull request's Commits tab), or with the plan posted on the issue. Check new dependencies and network calls, and anything under .github/, .claude/, tools/, scripts/ and tests/ line by line.
- **No auto-merge.** Team mode's members land by auto-merge on a green check (docs/agents/TEAM.md); that is for members the owner runs, never for contributions from other people.
- **Look at the check's log.** A green check proves only what `bash scripts/check.sh` tests. A project that takes crowd work should make that script test what matters, because it is the one judge every pull request meets.

### For each contributor

- A routine "appears as you" on GitHub and on any connector left in; keep connectors out.
- Auto-fix replies under your name to whoever comments; keep it on only while you watch, and keep your worker out of a Project.
- A Pro or Max cloud session shared as Public is "visible to any user logged into claude.ai" ([cloud sessions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/claude-code-on-the-web)); keep worker sessions Private.
- Never run work for the project on someone else's account, and never let anyone else run work on yours.
